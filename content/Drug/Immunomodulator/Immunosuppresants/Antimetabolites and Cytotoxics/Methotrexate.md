### Methotrexate
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class:**
    *   Folic acid antimetabolite; classical antifolate; synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) and antineoplastic agent.
*   **Target Cytokine, Receptor, or Immune Cell:**
    *   Intracellular targets: Dihydrofolate Reductase (DHFR), Thymidylate Synthase (TYMS), and 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide (AICAR) transformylase (also known as ATIC).
    *   Indirect extracellular target: Adenosine receptors (primarily A2A and A2B) on T cells, B cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells.
    *   Target cell populations: Rapidly proliferating lymphocytes (CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, B cells), keratinocytes, and neoplastic cell lines.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    *   **Cellular Influx:** Methotrexate (MTX) is actively transported across host cell membranes into the cytoplasm primarily via the Reduced Folate Carrier 1 (RFC-1, encoded by SLC19A1).
    *   **Polyglutamation (The Intracellular Retention Anchor):** Once inside the cell, MTX undergoes sequential addition of glutamic acid residues, a reaction catalyzed by the enzyme Folylpolyglutamate Synthetase (FPGS), to form Methotrexate Polyglutamates (MTX-PGs). These polyglutamated forms are highly charged, preventing their efflux through ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters (such as ABCC1-4 and ABCG2) and trapping them inside the cell. MTX-PGs possess a significantly higher affinity for folate-dependent enzymes than the parent monoglutamate compound. Polyglutamates can be slowly cleaved back to the effluxable monoglutamate form by the enzyme gamma-glutamyl hydrolase (GGH).
    *   **Enzymatic Blockade Pathways:**
        1.  **Dihydrofolate Reductase (DHFR) Inhibition:** MTX and MTX-PGs competitively and potently inhibit DHFR, the enzyme responsible for reducing dihydrofolate (DHF) to active tetrahydrofolate (THF). This blocks the regeneration of active THF pools, depleting critical intracellular folate cofactors such as 5,10-methylene-THF and 10-formyl-THF. This depletion halts de novo purine synthesis (depleting adenosine and guanine) and thymidylate synthesis (depleting thymidine), arresting DNA replication and cell division.
        2.  **Thymidylate Synthase (TYMS) Inhibition:** MTX-PGs directly inhibit TYMS, which catalyzes the transfer of a methyl group from 5,10-methylene-THF to dUMP to form dTMP. This directly halts de novo pyrimidine synthesis and DNA replication, causing "thymidylateless" cell death.
        3.  **AICAR Transformylase (ATIC) Inhibition & Adenosine Release (Primary Anti-inflammatory Pathway):** At low, non-chemotherapeutic anti-rheumatic doses (15 to 25 mg weekly), the primary mechanism of action is not general anti-proliferative folate starvation, but rather the induction of extracellular adenosine signaling. MTX-PGs potently inhibit ATIC, the final enzyme in de novo purine synthesis. This blockade leads to the intracellular accumulation of its substrate, AICAR (5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide). Accumulated AICAR directly inhibits two key enzymes: AMP deaminase and adenosine deaminase (ADA). This double enzymatic blockade prevents the breakdown of adenosine monophosphate (AMP) and adenosine, leading to a massive buildup of intracellular AMP and adenosine. These nucleotides are transported or released into the extracellular space, where extracellular ecto-nucleotidases (such as CD39 and CD73) rapidly convert extracellular AMP to active adenosine.
    *   **Receptor-Mediated Immunomodulation:** Released extracellular adenosine binds with high affinity to G-protein coupled A2A and A2B adenosine receptors on the surface of nearby T cells, B cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells. This binding stimulates Gαs-coupled adenylate cyclase, increasing intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP). Elevated cAMP downregulates the activity of pro-inflammatory transcription factors, suppressing the expression of inflammatory mediators.
*   **Downstream Immunological Effects:**
    *   **Suppression of Cytokine Production:** Profoundly decreases the synthesis and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-12, while concurrently upregulating anti-inflammatory IL-10.
    *   **Inhibition of Lymphocyte Proliferation:** Arrests activated, autoreactive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and B cells in the S-phase of the cell cycle, inducing clonal expansion failure and selective apoptosis of hyperactive immune lineages.
    *   **Suppression of Cell Adhesion:** Decreases the expression of endothelial cell adhesion molecules (such as ICAM-1 and E-selectin), blocking lymphocyte and monocyte homing, margination, and migration into inflamed tissues (such as synovial joint spaces).
    *   **Reduction of Rheumatoid Factor and Autoantibodies:** Leads to a gradual, mild decrease in systemic levels of rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs).
    *   **Inhibition of JAK/STAT Signaling:** Suppresses the phosphorylation and activation of Janus Kinases (JAKs) and Signal Transducers and Activators of Transcription (STATs), dampening cellular responsiveness to multiple interleukins and interferons independently of DHFR pathways.

#### Pharmacokinetics & Administration
*   **Route of Administration:**
    *   **Systemic:** Oral (tablets, solutions), Subcutaneous (single-dose autoinjectors such as Otrexup, Rasuvo, RediTrex), and Intramuscular/Intravenous injection. Note: At higher doses (>15 mg), oral absorption becomes saturated and erratic due to active transport saturation in the GI tract; switching to subcutaneous administration bypasses this limit, improving bioavailability and clinical efficacy while reducing gastrointestinal adverse effects.
    *   **Specialized:** Intrathecal injection (preservative-free formulations) for central nervous system prophylaxis or treatment of meningeal leukemia/lymphoma.
*   **Metabolism & Clearance Pathway:**
    *   **Intracellular Metabolism:** Undergoes extensive intracellular polyglutamation in hepatocytes, erythrocytes, and target cells via FPGS.
    *   **Hepatic Metabolism:** Partially metabolized in the liver to 7-hydroxymethotrexate (which is also active and undergoes polyglutamation) via hepatic aldehyde oxidase.
    *   **Renal Excretion:** Primarily cleared unchanged via the kidneys through a combination of glomerular filtration and active renal tubular secretion (mediated by organic anion transporters, primarily OAT1 and OAT3). Approximately 80% to 90% of a systemic dose is excreted unchanged in the urine within 24 hours.
    *   **Biliary Excretion:** A minor metabolic pathway (less than 10%) is excreted via the bile into the feces, undergoing enterohepatic recirculation.
*   **Onset of Action & Half-Life:**
    *   **Absorption:** Well absorbed orally at low doses, but highly variable at high doses. Peak plasma concentration (Cmax) is achieved within 1 to 2 hours post-dose.
    *   **Plasma Elimination Half-Life:** Short, typically ranging from 3 to 10 hours for low-dose therapy, but can extend to 15 hours with high-dose regimens.
    *   **Biological/Intracellular Half-Life:** Extremely prolonged. Intracellular MTX-PGs have a half-life of several weeks (typically 3 to 4 weeks), which explains why methotrexate is administered as a **single weekly dose** rather than daily, and why its therapeutic effects and toxicities persist long after the drug is cleared from the plasma.
    *   **Onset of Action:** Clinical anti-inflammatory effects in rheumatoid arthritis or psoriasis typically require 4 to 8 weeks (up to 12 weeks) to fully manifest, corresponding to the slow intracellular accumulation of active polyglutamated forms.
*   **Therapeutic Drug Monitoring Required:**
    *   **Low-Dose Regimens:** Direct therapeutic drug monitoring of plasma MTX levels is not routinely indicated. However, frequent laboratory monitoring of systemic physiological safety parameters is mandatory:
        1.  **Complete Blood Count (CBC):** To monitor for bone marrow suppression (leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, anemia). Checked every 2 to 4 weeks for the first few months, then every 1 to 3 months thereafter.
        2.  **Liver Function Tests (LFTs):** To monitor for hepatotoxicity (transaminases, albumin). Checked at the same interval as CBC.
        3.  **Renal Function (Serum Creatinine/BUN):** To assess renal clearance capacity, as any decline in GFR severely reduces MTX clearance, precipitating toxic systemic drug accumulation.
    *   **High-Dose Oncological Regimens (≥500 mg/m²):** Serial plasma methotrexate level monitoring is absolutely mandatory (at 24, 48, and 72 hours post-infusion) to guide the timing and dosing of **Leucovorin (Folinic Acid) Rescue** and to determine when it is safe to discontinue hyperhydration and urinary alkalinization (urine pH must be maintained >7.0 to prevent MTX crystal precipitation in the renal tubules).

#### Clinical Indications
*   **Primary Autoimmune / Inflammatory Indications:**
    *   **Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA):** The universally established "anchor drug" and first-line gold standard disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) for severe, active, and erosive rheumatoid arthritis in adult patients.
    *   **Psoriasis / Psoriatic Arthritis:** Treatment of severe, recalcitrant, disabling plaque psoriasis in adults who are unresponsive to topical therapies or phototherapy, and for active psoriatic arthritis.
    *   **Polyarticular Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (pJIA):** Indicated for pediatric patients with active polyarticular-course JIA who have had an insufficient response to NSAIDs.
    *   **Crohn's Disease (Off-Label):** Used to induce and maintain remission in patients with moderate-to-severe Crohn's disease who are steroid-refractory or steroid-dependent.
    *   **Systemic Lupus Erythematosus & Vasculitides (Off-Label):** Used as a steroid-sparing maintenance agent for lupus arthritis, cutaneous lupus, giant cell arteritis, and Takayasu arteritis.
*   **Oncology Indications:**
    *   **Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL):** Active component of induction, consolidation, and maintenance chemotherapy regimens in pediatric and adult ALL (systemic and intrathecal).
    *   **Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia (GTN):** First-line chemotherapy of choice for gestational choriocarcinoma, chorioadenoma destruens (invasive mole), and hydatidiform mole.
    *   **Other Solid Tumors and Lymphomas:** Adjuvant therapy for breast cancer, osteosarcoma, advanced mycosis fungoides (cutaneous T-cell lymphoma), head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, and advanced non-Hodgkin's lymphomas.
*   **Transplant Rejection / Prophylaxis:**
    *   **Graft-versus-Host Disease (GvHD) Prophylaxis:** Combined with calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine or tacrolimus) to prevent acute GvHD in patients undergoing allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.
*   **Obstetric Indications:**
    *   **Ectopic Pregnancy (Off-Label):** First-line non-surgical medical management for unruptured, hemodynamically stable tubal ectopic pregnancies (typically administered as a single or multidose intramuscular regimen).

#### Adverse Effects & Toxicity
*   **Infection Risks & Opportunistic Pathogens:**
    *   **Immunosuppression:** Suppresses cell-mediated and humoral immunity, elevating susceptibility to bacterial, viral, and fungal pathogens.
    *   **Opportunistic Infections:** Increased risk of developing Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP), disseminated herpes zoster (shingles), cytomegalovirus (CMV) reactivation, and invasive fungal infections.
    *   **Latent Reactivation:** Promotes the reactivation of latent Tuberculosis and chronic Hepatitis B/C.
*   **Malignancy Risks:**
    *   **Lymphoma:** Associated with the development of EBV-associated lymphoproliferative disorders and lymphomas, which can sometimes regress spontaneously upon drug discontinuation.
*   **Organ-Specific Toxicity:**
    *   **Gastrointestinal / Mucosal:** Very common. Nausea, vomiting, abdominal distress, anorexia, and diarrhea. **Severe ulcerative stomatitis (mucositis)** is a hallmark sign of acute folate toxicity and is often the earliest clinical indicator of a dosing error or severe overdose.
    *   **Hepatotoxicity:** Elevation of liver transaminases (AST, ALT) is common and dose-dependent. Chronic, long-term therapy can cause progressive hepatic fibrosis, cirrhosis, and portal hypertension. Risk is highly synergized by alcohol consumption, obesity, and pre-existing liver disease.
    *   **Hematologic (Myelosuppression):** Dose-dependent, severe bone marrow suppression presenting as leukopenia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and macrocytic anemia. Can be rapid, severe, and life-threatening in patients with renal impairment, older age, or folate deficiency.
    *   **Pulmonary (Methotrexate Pneumonitis):** An acute, idiosyncratic, potentially fatal interstitial pneumonitis presenting as dry cough, high fever, dyspnea, and bilateral patchy pulmonary infiltrates. Can occur at any dose and at any time during therapy, requiring immediate drug discontinuation.
    *   **Renal:** High-dose regimens can cause acute kidney injury (AKI) due to the precipitation of methotrexate and its metabolite 7-hydroxymethotrexate in the renal tubules (prevented by hyperhydration and urinary alkalinization).
    *   **Neurotoxicity:** Intrathecal or high-dose systemic administration can cause acute chemical arachnoiditis, transient or permanent paresis, and progressive demyelinating leucoencephalopathy.
    *   **Dermatologic:** Alopecia, photosensitivity, acne, and severe cutaneous reactions, including Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) and Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN).
*   **Black Box Warnings:**
    *   **Severe Toxicities and Fatalities:** Boxed warnings indicating that methotrexate can cause severe, sometimes fatal, toxicities including bone marrow suppression, hepatotoxicity, gastrointestinal toxicity (hemorrhagic enteritis, intestinal perforation), and pulmonary toxicity.
    *   **Fetal Death and Congenital Malformations:** Contraindicated in pregnant women due to high teratogenic and mutagenic risk.
    *   **Renal Impairment:** Requires dose adjustment and extreme caution; methotrexate clearance is severely impaired in patients with renal dysfunction.
    *   **Concomitant NSAID Use:** Concomitant administration of NSAIDs with high-dose methotrexate can cause severe, fatal myelosuppression and gastrointestinal toxicity due to decreased renal clearance.
    *   **Infection Risk:** Susceptibility to opportunistic infections and fatal sepsis.
    *   **Pneumonitis:** Potential for fatal, acute interstitial pneumonitis.
    *   **Dermatologic Reactions:** Risk of fatal SJS and TEN.

#### Pre-Screening & Contraindications
*   **Required Pre-Treatment Screening:**
    *   **Pregnancy Screening:** Mandatory negative serum or urine pregnancy test in females of childbearing potential.
    *   **Baseline Laboratory Panel:** Complete Blood Count (CBC) with differential, liver enzymes (ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin), and renal function (serum creatinine, estimated GFR).
    *   **Infectious Screening:** Serological screening for Hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti-HBc), Hepatitis C, HIV, and screening for latent Tuberculosis (IGRA or TST) prior to initiating chronic therapy.
    *   **Chest X-Ray:** Recommended to evaluate for pre-existing interstitial lung disease or baseline pulmonary pathology.
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    *   Pregnancy (Category X) and breastfeeding.
    *   Alcoholism, alcoholic liver disease, or other chronic liver diseases.
    *   Pre-existing severe immunodeficiency syndromes.
    *   Pre-existing severe blood dyscrasias (such as bone marrow hypoplasia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, or significant anemia).
    *   Known immediate hypersensitivity to methotrexate or any component of the formulation.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:**
    *   **Pregnancy Category:** Category X. Methotrexate is a highly potent human teratogen and abortifacient.
    *   **Fetal Methotrexate Syndrome:** First-trimester exposure causes a distinct pattern of severe skeletal and cranial malformations, including skull ossification defects, craniosynostosis, limb reduction defects, cleft palate, dysmorphic facial features, and severe developmental delay.
    *   **Contraceptive Mandates:** Highly effective contraception is mandatory during therapy. Female patients must maintain contraception during treatment and for at least **one complete ovulatory cycle** (usually defined as at least 30 days) following drug discontinuation. Male patients must utilize effective contraception during treatment and for at least **3 months** after the final dose of methotrexate to avoid mutagenic risk to the fetus.
    *   **Lactation:** Strictly contraindicated; methotrexate is excreted in breast milk and can cause bone marrow suppression and growth inhibition in the nursing infant.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    *   **Salicylates and NSAIDs (e.g., Aspirin, Ibuprofen, Naproxen):** NSAIDs compete with methotrexate for renal tubular secretion through organic anion transporters (OAT1/OAT3) and decrease renal perfusion. This blocks MTX excretion, leading to toxic, potentially fatal elevations in systemic methotrexate levels and severe bone marrow suppression. This interaction is highly dangerous with high-dose oncological regimens, but low-dose antirheumatic weekly regimens can often be safely co-administered with close monitoring.
    *   **Proton Pump Inhibitors (e.g., Omeprazole, Esomeprazole, Pantoprazole):** Co-administration can impair renal tubular secretion of methotrexate via OAT receptors, leading to elevated MTX levels and toxicity.
    *   **Penicillins (e.g., Amoxicillin, Piperacillin):** Compete for active renal tubular secretion, potentially increasing methotrexate concentrations to toxic levels.
    *   **Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (Bactrim) / Pyrimethamine:** Sulfonamides and trimethoprim act as antifolates themselves (inhibiting bacterial folate synthesis and DHFR). Concomitant use with methotrexate causes a synergistic, severe antifolate toxicity, leading to catastrophic myelosuppression, pancytopenia, and mucosal ulcerations.
    *   **Hepatotoxic Agents (e.g., Leflunomide, Sulfasalazine, Alcohol):** Synergistically elevate the risk of chronic hepatotoxicity and progressive liver fibrosis. Alcohol consumption must be strictly avoided or highly restricted during therapy.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    *   **"Weekly Dosing Trap":** One of the most frequent and dangerous medication errors in outpatient medicine. Methotrexate for rheumatoid arthritis or psoriasis must be taken **once weekly** (on a single designated day). Fatal toxic pancytopenia and severe mucosal ulceration can occur if a patient accidentally takes it daily.
    *   **"Folate Rescue":** Refers to the mandatory clinical practice of prescribing oral **Folic Acid** (typically 1 to 5 mg daily, omitted on the day of methotrexate administration) or **Folinic Acid (Leucovorin)** to mitigate methotrexate-induced mucosal, gastrointestinal, and hematological toxicities without compromising its low-dose anti-inflammatory efficacy.
    *   **"S-Phase Specific":** In oncological settings, methotrexate is classified as an S-phase cell cycle-specific antimetabolite, targeting cells during active DNA synthesis.
*   **Historical Milestones:**
    *   **Jane C. Wright and Solid Tumor Chemotherapy:** Dr. Jane C. Wright, a pioneering African-American oncologist, performed some of the earliest clinical trials of methotrexate (then known as amethopterin) in the late 1940s and early 1950s. She demonstrated its remarkable efficacy in inducing remissions in breast cancer and other solid tumors, establishing the foundational principles of modern cancer chemotherapy.
    *   **Min Chiu Li and the Cure of Choriocarcinoma:** In 1956, Dr. Min Chiu Li, working alongside Dr. Roy Hertz at the National Cancer Institute, utilized methotrexate to treat patients with metastatic gestational choriocarcinoma. By monitoring urinary human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) levels as a biomarker, Li continued administering methotrexate even after clinical symptoms resolved, until hCG levels normalized. This landmark achievement represented the first-ever cure of a metastatic solid tumor with systemic chemotherapy.
*   **Naming Conventions:**
    *   **Amethopterin:** The historic, pre-clinical name denoting its chemical structural relationship as a methylated pterin derivative.
    *   **4-amino-10-methylfolic acid:** The precise chemical structural description of the compound, differing from natural folic acid by the substitution of an amino group for a hydroxyl group at the C-4 position of the pteridine ring, and a methyl group at the N-10 position.
    *   **Rheumatrex / Trexall / Otrexup / Rasuvo / RediTrex:** Commercial brand names representing the systemic and subcutaneous formulations of the compound.
