### Prednisone
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class:**
    *   Synthetic systemic glucocorticoid; class of corticosteroids. It is a biologically inactive prodrug that requires enzymatic activation in vivo to its active form, prednisolone.
*   **Target Cytokine, Receptor, or Immune Cell:**
    *   Glucocorticoid Receptor alpha (GRα, encoded by the NR3C1 gene), widely expressed across almost all nucleated cells, particularly immune cells (CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells).
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    *   **Enzymatic Activation:** Following oral administration and absorption, the biologically inert prodrug prednisone is converted in the liver to its active form, prednisolone, by the enzyme 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1 (11β-HSD1). Prednisolone possesses a beta-hydroxyl group instead of the oxo group at position 11 on the steroid ring, which is the active moiety required for receptor binding.
    *   **Receptor Activation and Nuclear Translocation:** Active prednisolone is highly lipophilic and readily crosses host cell membranes. Within the cytoplasm, it binds with high affinity to the glucocorticoid receptor (GRα / NR3C1). This binding triggers a conformational change that causes the dissociation of various molecular chaperone proteins, including Heat Shock Protein 90 (HSP90), Heat Shock Protein 70 (HSP70), and Heat Shock Protein 56 (HSP56). The uncovered nuclear localization signals then direct the rapid translocation of the GRα-ligand complex into the cell nucleus.
    *   **Genomic Mechanisms of Action:**
        1.  **Transactivation (GRE-Dependent Gene Induction):** Homodimers of the activated GRα bind directly to specific DNA sequences known as Glucocorticoid Response Elements (GREs) in the promoter regions of target genes. This promotes the transcriptional upregulation of anti-inflammatory proteins, including:
            - **Annexin A1 (Lipocortin-1):** Inhibits cytosolic phospholipase A2 (cPLA2), preventing the cleavage of arachidonic acid from cell membrane phospholipids and thereby blocking the synthesis of all downstream inflammatory eicosanoids (prostaglandins, thromboxanes, and leukotrienes).
            - **IκBα (Inhibitor of NF-κB-alpha):** Sequesters NF-κB in the cytoplasm, blocking its nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity.
            - **Dual Specificity Phosphatase 1 (DUSP1 / MKP-1):** Dephosphorylates and inactivates mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), suppressing intracellular inflammatory cascades.
            - **GILZ (Glucocorticoid-Induced Leucine Zipper):** Interacts with and represses NF-κB and AP-1.
        2.  **Transrepression (Gene Downregulation via Transcription Factor Antagonism):** Monomers of the activated GRα physically tether to and neutralize pro-inflammatory transcription factors, primarily Nuclear Factor-kappa B (NF-κB) (the p65/p50 heterodimer) and Activator Protein 1 (AP-1) (the Fos/Jun heterodimer). This physical antagonism blocks the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes, including cytokines (IL-1, IL-2, IL-3, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-12, TNF-α, and IFN-γ), chemokines, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs).
        3.  **Chromatin Remodeling (HDAC2 Recruitment):** The GRα recruits Histone Deacetylase 2 (HDAC2) to active inflammatory gene promoters. HDAC2 deacetylates histones, resulting in compaction of chromatin structure and the transcriptional silencing of pro-inflammatory genes.
*   **Downstream Immunological Effects:**
    *   **Leukocyte Demargination & Trafficking:** Downregulates adhesion molecules (such as L-selectin and ICAM-1) on neutrophils, preventing their marginated attachment to vascular endothelial cells and causing neutrophilic leukocytosis (neutrophilia). Concurrently, it causes a rapid lymphopenia, monocytopenia, and eosinopenia by redirecting these cells from the circulation into secondary lymphoid organs and inducing their apoptosis.
    *   **Inhibition of T-Cell Proliferation and Costimulation:** Strongly inhibits T-lymphocyte activation, proliferation, and clonal expansion by suppressing IL-2 and IL-2 receptor transcription.
    *   **Inhibition of Antigen Presentation:** Downregulates the expression of MHC Class II molecules and co-stimulatory markers (CD80, CD86) on macrophages and dendritic cells, impairing antigen presentation to helper T cells.
    *   **Suppression of Cytolytic Activity:** Dampens the cytotoxic, lytic, and tumoricidal functions of CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes and Natural Killer (NK) cells.
    *   **Th1 to Th2 Polarization Shift:** Alters the helper T cell balance by suppressing pro-inflammatory Th1 and Th17 pathway cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-17, IL-22) while promoting or maintaining Th2 anti-inflammatory cytokine expression (IL-4, IL-10).
    *   **Macrophage and Mast Cell Suppression:** Inhibits phagocytosis, chemotaxis, and the release of lysosomal enzymes and pro-inflammatory cytokines from tissue macrophages, and stabilizes mast cells and basophils to prevent histamine and leukotriene release.
    *   **Reduced Vascular Permeability:** Stabilizes endothelial cell membranes and tight junctions, dramatically reducing microvascular permeability and preventing fluid extravasation and localized tissue edema.

#### Pharmacokinetics & Administration
*   **Route of Administration:**
    *   Oral (as tablets, delayed-release tablets, or oral solutions). It is rapidly and well absorbed from the stomach and small intestine.
*   **Metabolism & Clearance Pathway:**
    *   **First-Pass Activation:** Prednisone is a biologically inert prodrug that must be converted to its active form, prednisolone, in the liver via reduction of the 11-keto group to an 11β-hydroxyl group, a reaction catalyzed by the enzyme 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1 (11β-HSD1).
    *   **Inactivation:** Prednisolone is subsequently inactivated primarily in the liver by Cytochrome P450 enzymes, predominantly CYP3A4 (and to a lesser extent CYP3A5). The major inactivation pathway is 6β-hydroxylation (forming 6β-hydroxyprednisolone) and reduction of the 20-keto group.
    *   **Active Efflux Transporter:** Prednisone and prednisolone are substrates for the active efflux transporter P-glycoprotein (P-gp / ABCB1), which limits their accumulation in certain tissues (e.g., the brain) and can be a source of variability in drug response.
    *   **Excretion:** Following metabolism and conjugation, the inactive metabolites are excreted primarily via the kidneys in the urine. Approximately 20% of prednisolone is excreted unchanged in the urine.
    *   **CYP3A4 Interactions:**
        - **CYP3A4 Inducers:** Drugs that induce CYP3A4 (e.g., rifampin, rifabutin, phenytoin, phenobarbital, carbamazepine) dramatically accelerate the clearance of prednisolone, reducing its clinical efficacy.
        - **CYP3A4 Inhibitors:** Drugs that inhibit CYP3A4 (e.g., ketoconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, clarithromycin, ritonavir, cobicistat) block the clearance of prednisolone, leading to toxic drug accumulation and an increased risk of systemic glucocorticoid adverse effects (Cushing's syndrome and HPA axis suppression).
*   **Onset of Action & Half-Life:**
    *   **Bioavailability:** High, ranging from 70% to 90% (average of approximately 80%).
    *   **Onset of Action:** Rapid. Peak serum concentrations of the active metabolite prednisolone are achieved within 1 to 2 hours following oral ingestion of standard prednisone formulations. Delayed-release formulations (such as Rayos) are designed to delay release (e.g., by 4 hours) to align with circadian inflammatory peaks.
    *   **Protein Binding:** Approximately 70% to 90%, bound mainly to transcortin (corticosteroid-binding globulin) and albumin.
    *   **Plasma Half-Life:** The plasma half-life of prednisone is short (approximately 1 hour), and the terminal half-life of active prednisolone is approximately 2.5 to 4 hours.
    *   **Biological Half-Life (Duration of Action):** Intermediate, ranging from 18 to 36 hours. Because the anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive actions are driven by genomic transcription and translation changes, the pharmacological effects persist long after the drug has been cleared from the bloodstream.
*   **Therapeutic Drug Monitoring Required:**
    *   Routine therapeutic drug monitoring of serum prednisone or prednisolone levels is not clinically indicated. However, close laboratory and clinical monitoring is mandatory:
        1.  **Fasting Blood Glucose / HbA1c:** Checked regularly to detect steroid-induced hyperglycemia or diabetes mellitus.
        2.  **Serum Electrolytes:** Specifically potassium and sodium, to monitor for hypokalemia and sodium retention.
        3.  **Blood Pressure & Body Weight:** Evaluated frequently to monitor for hypertension and fluid retention.
        4.  **HPA Axis Monitoring:** Morning serum cortisol or ACTH stimulation testing is necessary during and after tapering to evaluate for secondary adrenal insufficiency.
        5.  **Bone Density (DEXA Scan):** Recommended periodically during long-term therapy (>3 months) to evaluate for osteoporosis.

#### Clinical Indications
*   **Primary Autoimmune / Inflammatory Indications:**
    *   **Rheumatic Diseases:** Severe, active Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA), Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR), Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA), polyarteritis nodosa, dermatomyositis, and polymyositis.
    *   **Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD):** Moderate-to-severe Crohn's disease and Ulcerative Colitis (used for inducing remission during acute flares).
    *   **Autoimmune Hepatitis:** First-line agent for inducing and maintaining remission, often combined with azathioprine.
    *   **Severe Allergic Conditions:** Status asthmaticus, acute bronchial asthma flares, anaphylactic shock, severe drug hypersensitivity reactions, angioedema, and contact/atopic dermatitis.
    *   **Renal Diseases:** Lupus nephritis and nephrotic syndrome.
    *   **Dermatological Diseases:** Pemphigus vulgaris, bullous pemphigoid, and linear IgA bullous disease.
*   **Oncology Indications:**
    *   **Antineoplastic Regimens:** Used as an essential component in chemotherapy regimens for hematological malignancies, including Multiple Myeloma, Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL), Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL), Hodgkin Lymphoma, and Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma. It exerts direct lympholytic and pro-apoptotic effects on neoplastic lymphoid cells.
    *   **Supportive Care:** Prevention of chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV), management of tumor-related pain, and reduction of tumor-associated vasogenic edema.
*   **Transplant Rejection / Prophylaxis:**
    *   **Allograft Rejection Prophylaxis:** Used as a key adjunctive maintenance immunosuppressant in combination with calcineurin inhibitors (e.g., cyclosporine, tacrolimus) and antimetabolites (e.g., azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil) to prevent acute and chronic rejection in renal, hepatic, and cardiac transplantation. It is also used to treat acute allograft rejection episodes.
*   **Infectious Diseases (Specialized Category from Text):**
    *   **Severe Pneumocystis jirovecii Pneumonia (PCP):** Indicated as adjunctive therapy in patients with moderate-to-severe PCP (defined by a PO2 <70 mm Hg or an alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient >35 mm Hg on room air) to reduce the inflammatory response triggered by dying organisms. It must be initiated at the onset of anti-PCP therapy (e.g., with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) to significantly decrease mortality, respiratory failure, and the need for mechanical ventilation.
    *   **Acute Bacterial Meningitis:** Used off-label or as an alternative to dexamethasone, administered before or with the first dose of systemic antibiotics (e.g., for Streptococcus pneumoniae meningitis) to reduce laryngeal and cerebral mucosal edema, hearing loss, and long-term neurological sequelae.
    *   **Melarsoprol Adjuvant Therapy:** Administered concurrently throughout the treatment of late-stage African Trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness) to control and prevent severe, life-threatening hypersensitivity reactions that occur most frequently during the second or subsequent courses of melarsoprol therapy.
    *   **Onchocerciasis Adjuvant:** Used concurrently with ivermectin during the first treatment course in patients with microfilariae in the cornea or anterior chamber to avoid severe, inflammatory eye reactions (Mazzotti reaction) triggered by dying microfilariae.

#### Adverse Effects & Toxicity
*   **Infection Risks & Opportunistic Pathogens:**
    *   **Immunosuppression:** Chronic or high-dose therapy severely impairs host cell-mediated and humoral immunity. This increases susceptibility to a broad spectrum of bacterial, viral, fungal, and parasitic infections.
    *   **Opportunistic Pathogens:** High risk of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP), cytomegalovirus (CMV) reactivation, systemic candidiasis, invasive aspergillosis, and cryptococcal meningitis.
    *   **Latent Reactivation:** Promotes the reactivation of latent Tuberculosis (TB) and latent Hepatitis B (HBV).
    *   **Strongyloides Hyperinfection:** Exposes patients with subclinical Strongyloides stercoralis infection to disseminated, life-threatening hyperinfection syndrome.
    *   **Masking of Infections:** May mask classical clinical signs of infection (such as fever and localized pain/inflammation), leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment.
*   **Malignancy Risks:**
    *   **Non-Melanoma Skin Cancer:** Increased risk of squamous cell and basal cell carcinomas due to impaired DNA repair and photosensitivity during chronic use.
    *   **Viral-Associated Malignancies:** Increased risk of Kaposi's sarcoma (due to HHV-8 reactivation).
*   **Organ-Specific Toxicity:**
    *   **Endocrine & Metabolic:**
        - **Secondary Adrenal Insufficiency:** Suppresses the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis by inhibiting CRH and ACTH release. Abrupt cessation of chronic therapy can precipitate a life-threatening adrenal crisis.
        - **Cushingoid Features:** Development of Cushing's syndrome, characterized by moon face, buffalo hump (dorsocervical fat enlargement), truncal obesity, supraclavicular fat pads, abdominal striae, and peripheral wasting.
        - **Hyperglycemia and Diabetes:** Stimulates hepatic gluconeogenesis and inhibits peripheral glucose uptake, leading to insulin resistance and steroid-induced diabetes.
        - **Growth Suppression:** Inhibits bone growth and epiphyseal maturation in pediatric patients.
    *   **Musculoskeletal:**
        - **Osteoporosis:** Inhibits osteoblast activity, stimulates osteoclasts, and decreases intestinal calcium absorption, leading to severe bone loss, pathological fractures, and vertebral compression.
        - **Avascular Necrosis:** Aseptic necrosis of bone, most commonly affecting the femoral head.
        - **Myopathy:** Symmetrical proximal muscle wasting and weakness ("steroid myopathy").
    *   **Ophthalmic:** Glaucoma (increased intraocular pressure due to trabecular meshwork outflow obstruction) and posterior subcapsular cataracts.
    *   **Neuropsychiatric:** Insomnia, anxiety, euphoria, severe mood swings, depression, suicidal ideation, and "steroid psychosis" (hallucinations, delusions).
    *   **Gastrointestinal:** Peptic ulcer disease, gastric hemorrhage, acute pancreatitis, and a highly increased risk of gastrointestinal perforation (especially when combined with NSAIDs).
    *   **Cardiovascular & Renal:** Sodium and fluid retention, peripheral edema, hypokalemic alkalosis, new-onset or exacerbated hypertension, and increased risk of congestive heart failure.
    *   **Dermatological:** Thinning of the skin, easy bruising (purpura), petechiae, striae, acne, hirsutism, and significantly impaired wound healing.
*   **Black Box Warnings:**
    *   N/A (Standard prednisone formulations do not carry Black Box Warnings, though warning labels exist for the risk of severe infections, HPA axis suppression, and potential teratogenicity).

#### Pre-Screening & Contraindications
*   **Required Pre-Treatment Screening:**
    *   **Infection Screening:** Screen for active or latent Tuberculosis (IGRA or TST) and chronic Hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti-HBc) prior to initiating chronic therapy.
    *   **Metabolic Panel:** Fasting blood glucose or HbA1c to assess baseline glycemic status; serum electrolytes (specifically potassium, sodium, and calcium).
    *   **Cardiovascular Evaluation:** Document baseline blood pressure, weight, and evaluate for pre-existing congestive heart failure.
    *   **Ophthalmic Evaluation:** Slit-lamp exam and intraocular pressure checks in patients with a history of glaucoma or cataracts.
    *   **Bone Density:** Baseline DEXA scan for patients anticipated to undergo long-term therapy (>3 months).
    *   **Pregnancy Screening:** Mandatory negative pregnancy test for female patients of childbearing potential.
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    *   Systemic fungal infections (unless accompanied by specific, effective antifungal therapy).
    *   Known immediate hypersensitivity to prednisone, prednisolone, or any excipients.
    *   Administration of live or live-attenuated vaccines while receiving immunosuppressive doses of prednisone.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:**
    *   **Pregnancy Category:** Category C.
    *   **Placental Barrier Protection:** Unlike dexamethasone, which readily crosses the placenta in active form, active prednisolone is extensively inactivated by the placental enzyme 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2) into inactive prednisone. This enzyme acts as a barrier that protects the developing fetus from maternal glucocorticoid exposure. Consequently, prednisone/prednisolone is the preferred systemic corticosteroid for treating maternal chronic diseases (e.g., asthma, SLE, GvHD) during pregnancy.
    *   **Teratogenic Risks:** Despite the placental barrier, chronic or high-dose first-trimester exposure is associated with a slightly increased risk of oral clefts (cleft lip/palate). Chronic use can also lead to intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), premature delivery, and neonatal adrenal suppression (requiring close monitoring of the newborn for adrenal crisis).
    *   **Lactation:** Excreted in human breast milk. High doses can potentially suppress growth or interfere with endogenous corticosteroid production in the nursing infant. Avoid breastfeeding during high-dose therapy or delay nursing for several hours after a dose.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    *   **"Glucocorticoid Prodrug Prototype":** Prednisone is the classic, historical gold standard of oral corticosteroid therapy, serving as the prototype drug for systemic anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive therapies.
    *   **"Steroid-Sparing":** A clinical term for the use of secondary immunosuppressive drugs (e.g., azathioprine, methotrexate) to allow a significant reduction in the maintenance dose of prednisone, minimizing its devastating long-term toxicities.
    *   **"HPA Axis Suppression":** The pathognomonic endocrine shutdown of endogenous cortisol production resulting from negative feedback, requiring strict and gradual tapering regimens to prevent life-threatening adrenal insufficiency.
*   **Naming Conventions:**
    *   **11-keto vs. 11-hydroxyl:** Prednisone contains a keto group at the C11 position, rendering it biologically inactive. Conversion to prednisolone reduces this keto group to a beta-hydroxyl group, which is the exact chemical structure required to bind and activate the glucocorticoid receptor.
    *   **Deltasone / Rayos / Orasone:** Prominent commercial brand names for oral prednisone. Deltasone was the historic, widely used formulation; Rayos is a modern, delayed-release oral tablet designed to be taken at bedtime, releasing the active drug in the early morning hours to combat morning stiffness in rheumatoid arthritis.
