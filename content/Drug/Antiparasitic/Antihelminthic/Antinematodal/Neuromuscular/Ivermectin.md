### Ivermectin
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:**
    - Class: Macrocyclic lactones.
    - Subclass: Avermectins (novel class of 16-membered macrocyclic lactones).
    - Ivermectin (22,23-dihydroavermectin B1a) is a semisynthetic analog of avermectin B1a (abamectin), which was developed originally as an insecticide for crop management. It exists as a mixture containing at least 80% 22,23-dihydroavermectin B1a and no more than 20% 22,23-dihydroavermectin B1b, which exhibit nearly identical antiparasitic activities.
*   **Target Organisms:**
    - Nematodes (parasitic roundworms): Onchocerca volvulus, Strongyloides stercoralis, Ascaris lumbricoides, Enterobius vermicularis, Trichuris trichiura, Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi, Loa loa, and Mansonella ozzardi.
    - Arthropods: Sarcoptes scabiei (scabies mites), Pediculus humanus capitis (human head lice), livestock ticks, and veterinary ectoparasites.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    - Ivermectin paralyzes nematodes and arthropods by targeting peripheral nerve and muscle systems through multiple complementary molecular pathways:
      1. It binds selectively and with high affinity to glutamate-gated chloride channels (which are unique to invertebrates and absent in mammalian cells), increasing cell membrane permeability to chloride ions.
      2. It intensifies gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-mediated transmission of signals in peripheral nerves by acting as a GABA receptor agonist or enhancing presynaptic GABA release.
      3. It binds to a novel allosteric site on the invertebrate acetylcholine nicotinic receptor to augment neurotransmission.
      - These combined actions produce a rapid, massive, and prolonged influx of chloride ions into the parasite's nerve or muscle cells, leading to hyperpolarization of the membrane, flaccid or spastic motor paralysis, and the ultimate death or expulsion of the organism.
      - In Onchocerca volvulus, ivermectin is microfilaricidal. It does not effectively kill the adult worms (macrofilariae) but blocks the release of microfilariae from the uterus of adult female worms for some months. With repeated doses, the drug exerts a low-level macrofilaricidal action and permanently reduces microfilarial production.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:**
    - Invertebrate glutamate-gated chloride channels.
    - Invertebrate GABA receptors and nicotinic acetylcholine receptors.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):**
    - Microfilaricidal (rapidly kills and clears circulating or cutaneous microfilariae of susceptible filarial species).
    - Vermicidal against adult Strongyloides stercoralis, Ascaris lumbricoides, and cutaneous larva migrans.
    - Slower, partial or low-level macrofilaricidal activity against adult Onchocerca volvulus with repeated dosing; lacks macrofilaricidal activity against adult Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi, and Loa loa.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    - Resistance to ivermectin and related agents (such as moxidectin) is widely reported in parasites of veterinary importance, and poor parasitological responses have been reported in human onchocerciasis control programs.
    - Fungal or parasite resistance is mediated by three primary mechanisms:
      1. Alterations or overexpression of P-glycoprotein (P-gp) transport homologs, which actively pump ivermectin out of the parasite's cells and decrease drug accumulation.
      2. Mutations or genetic polymorphisms in the genes encoding the subunit components of glutamate-gated chloride channels, reducing ivermectin's binding affinity.
      3. Broad multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotypes selected by escalating drug exposure, which convey cross-resistance to other anthelmintics (such as levamisole and pyrantel), but not to albendazole.
      - In filarial nematodes like Wuchereria bancrofti, beta-tubulin gene mutations associated with resistance have been selected during mass drug administration programs co-administering albendazole and ivermectin.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:**
    - Oral: Rapidly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract following oral administration. Peak plasma concentrations are achieved within 4 to 5 hours (maximum plasma concentrations occur 4 hours after a standard 12-mg oral dose).
    - Bioavailability is decreased or variable in patients with severe intestinal pathology or poor gastrointestinal transit.
    - Parenteral: Parenteral veterinary formulations of ivermectin have been administered off-label via the subcutaneous route in humans with life-threatening Strongyloides hyperinfection syndrome where gastrointestinal absorption is severely compromised.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:**
    - Protein Binding: Highly bound to plasma proteins (approximately 93% bound).
    - Volume of Distribution: Exhibits a large apparent volume of distribution (approximately 50 L), reflecting its high lipophilicity.
    - Tissue Accumulation: Widely distributed throughout systemic tissues, concentrating preferentially in adipose tissue (fat), skin, sebum, and hair follicles.
    - Central Nervous System: Extremely poor penetration across the blood-brain barrier into the central nervous system (CNS) under normal conditions. This is due to the active efflux transport mediated by P-glycoprotein (P-gp) located in the endothelial cells of the brain microvasculature, which continuously pumps the drug out of the brain parenchyma. (Studies in transgenic mice lacking the P-glycoprotein gene show extensive drug accumulation in the brain and severe neurotoxicity). Additionally, ivermectin has a much lower binding affinity for mammalian CNS receptors compared to invertebrate receptors, ensuring a wide margin of systemic safety.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    - Extensively metabolized in the liver, principally by the Cytochrome P450 enzyme isoform CYP3A4.
    - It is converted into at least 10 metabolites, which are predominantly hydroxylated and demethylated derivatives.
    - Possible adverse drug interactions involving co-administered agents that inhibit, induce, or are metabolized by CYP3A4 have not been fully evaluated.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    - Excretion: Excreted almost exclusively in the feces, as both unchanged drug and its metabolites. Virtually no ivermectin appears in human urine in either unchanged or conjugated form.
    - Half-life: The terminal elimination half-life is long, reflecting its low systemic clearance (approximately 1 to 2 L/hour) and extensive tissue depot binding.
      - One source cites a terminal half-life of approximately 16 hours.
      - Another source reports a terminal half-life of approximately 57 hours in adults.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - Renal Impairment: No specific dosage adjustments are routinely required or defined due to negligible renal excretion of active drug.
    - Hepatic Impairment: Requires caution; however, specific dosing adjustments are not detailed in the sources.
    - Pediatric & Weight-based limits: WHO mass campaign guidelines recommend against administering ivermectin to children who are younger than 5 years of age, weigh less than 15 kg, or are less than 90 cm in height.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    - Onchocerciasis (River Blindness) caused by Onchocerca volvulus: Drug of choice for both individual treatment and mass drug administration (MDA) eradication programs. Administered as a single oral dose of 150 to 200 mcg/kg, with water on an empty stomach, repeated every 6 to 12 months. It rapidly clears microfilariae from skin and ocular tissues (arresting the development of further ocular pathology and relieving intense pruritus), though it must be repeated annually/biannually for up to 10 years or longer until adult worms die.
    - Strongyloidiasis caused by Strongyloides stercoralis: Drug of choice, administered as a single oral dose of 150 to 200 mcg/kg once daily for 2 days. It is more effective than a 3-day course of albendazole. A second course is generally recommended 1 week after the first dose.
    - Strongyloides Hyperinfection Syndrome: Used off-label, often requiring prolonged, repeated, or suppressive monthly dosing. Subcutaneous veterinary ivermectin has been used off-label when oral absorption is compromised.
    - Cutaneous Larva Migrans (creeping eruption caused by dog/cat hookworms): First-line therapy, administered as a single oral dose of 200 mcg/kg.
    - Scabies caused by Sarcoptes scabiei: Highly effective.
      - Uncomplicated scabies: Two oral doses of 200 mcg/kg administered 1 to 2 weeks apart.
      - Severe, crusted (Norwegian) scabies: Administered in repeated doses. A standard regimen is 7 doses of 200 mcg/kg given with food on days 1, 2, 8, 9, 15, 22, and 29.
    - Human Head Lice: Effective as an oral treatment at 200 mcg/kg.
    - Lymphatic Filariasis (LF) Elimination: Administered in mass drug administration (MDA) campaigns as a single-dose combination with albendazole in regions where onchocerciasis or loiasis are co-endemic to safely interrupt transmission.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:**
    - Susceptible: Onchocerca volvulus (microfilariae), Strongyloides stercoralis, Ascaris lumbricoides, Enterobius vermicularis, Sarcoptes scabiei, Pediculus humanus capitis, Wuchereria bancrofti (microfilariae), Brugia malayi (microfilariae), Loa loa (microfilariae), and Mansonella ozzardi (microfilariae).
    - Variably Susceptible or Unresponsive: Trichuris trichiura (variably affected), Necator americanus (unresponsive/resistant), and Ancylostoma duodenale (unresponsive/resistant).
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    - Off-label subcutaneous administration of parenteral veterinary ivermectin in humans with life-threatening Strongyloides hyperinfection syndrome when intestinal absorption is severely impaired.
    - Long-term monthly suppressive oral therapy for recurrent or incurable Strongyloides infection in severely immunocompromised hosts.
    - Off-label treatment of human head lice.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:**
    - Gastrointestinal: Mild nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea.
    - Neurological: Fatigue, dizziness, somnolence, headache.
    - Dermatological: Pruritus, mild skin rashes, urticaria.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    - Mazzotti-like Reaction: An immunologic inflammatory reaction triggered by host response to the proteins released by rapidly dying microfilariae.
      - Usually mild in onchocerciasis, consisting of mild itching and swollen, tender lymph nodes (lasts a few days, treated with aspirin/antihistamines).
      - Severe systemic manifestations (occurring in up to 3% of patients, with a 0.1% risk of highly severe reactions) include high fever, tachycardia, severe hypotension, bronchospasm, prostration, facial and peripheral edema, and myalgia/arthralgia (requires systemic corticosteroid therapy).
    - Encephalopathy and Neurological Disability in Co-infected Patients: Administration of ivermectin in patients with heavy Loa loa microfilaria loads (specifically circulating microfilariae levels >= 30,000 microfilariae/mL of blood) can precipitate a rare but catastrophic, life-threatening Loa encephalopathy characterized by marked neurological disability, coma, and death.
    - Ocular Inflammation: Occasional development of transient corneal opacities and other eye lesions several days after treatment due to microfilarial death in ocular tissues. (Seldom exacerbates pre-existing ocular lesions compared to diethylcarbamazine).
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:**
    - Catastrophic, life-threatening encephalopathy and permanent disability occurring specifically in patients with heavy Loa loa co-infections (density >= 30,000 microfilariae/mL) following ivermectin administration.
    - Systemic Mazzotti-like reaction presenting with severe pruritus, hypotension, tachycardia, and tender lymphadenitis, directly proportional to the host's microfilarial worm burden.
    - Neurotoxicity (seizures, coma, death) occurring specifically in patients with impaired blood-brain barrier function or those lacking functional P-glycoprotein.

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    - Known history of severe hypersensitivity, anaphylaxis, or serious toxic reactions to ivermectin or any component of the formulation.
    - Conditions associated with an impaired blood-brain barrier: This includes patients with active meningitis or African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness), where the loss of blood-brain barrier integrity allows ivermectin to enter the CNS, leading to life-threatening neurotoxicity.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - Pregnancy: Ivermectin should not be used during pregnancy. It is classified as contraindicated during pregnancy by the WHO in mass drug campaigns.
    - Lactation: Lactating women secrete low levels of ivermectin in breast milk (infant consequences are unknown). WHO recommends against ivermectin use in mass campaigns for lactating women during the first week after birth.
    - Young Children: Not approved for use in children younger than 5 years of age or weighing less than 15 kg (or under 90 cm in height) due to limited safety data.
    - Co-infection with Loa loa: High-burden Loa loa co-infection requires extreme caution and pre-treatment microfilarial screening to avoid fatal encephalopathy.
    - Severe Illness: The WHO recommends against treating severely ill individuals in mass campaigns.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - GABA-Active Drugs: Avoid concurrent administration of ivermectin with drugs that enhance central GABAergic activity—such as barbiturates, benzodiazepines, and valproic acid—due to the potential for synergistic or additive CNS depression.
    - CYP3A4 Inhibitors and Inducers: Although not fully evaluated clinically, inhibitors (e.g., cimetidine) or inducers (e.g., rifampin) of the hepatic CYP3A4 enzyme may significantly alter ivermectin plasma levels.
    - Dexamethasone, Praziquantel, Cimetidine: Known to increase exposure and plasma levels of other co-administered benzimidazoles (e.g., albendazole), but their direct pharmacokinetic interactions with ivermectin's CYP3A4-mediated pathway require clinical monitoring.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:**
    - Strictly avoided during pregnancy. Although there is little direct evidence of teratogenicity or carcinogenicity in human epidemiological datasets, animal models warrant caution, and WHO mass eradication guidelines strictly exclude pregnant women.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "Mectizan Donation Programme," referencing the major, long-running global drug donation initiative by manufacturers to provide ivermectin free of charge for onchocerciasis control.
    - "Mazzotti reaction," describing the classic immune-mediated systemic reaction to dying microfilariae.
    - "P-glycoprotein shield," highlighting how the P-gp active efflux transporter at the blood-brain barrier protects the human CNS from ivermectin penetration and toxicity.
    - "Loa loa encephalopathy risk," referencing the fatal neurological hazard of treating patients with high Loa microfilaria density.
    - "Biannual OEPA regimens," referring to the successful twice-yearly treatment schedules of the Onchocerciasis Elimination Programme in the Americas.
    - "Collie dog neurotoxicity," referencing the well-known veterinary genetic mutation in the P-glycoprotein (MDR1) gene of Collie dogs that makes them highly sensitive to ivermectin-induced CNS death.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - Gastrointestinal Absorption Exception: In patients with life-threatening Strongyloides hyperinfection syndrome where gastrointestinal absorption of the oral drug is severely compromised, clinicians bypass the oral-only rule for humans by administering parenteral veterinary ivermectin off-label via the subcutaneous route.
    - Safe Larval Ocular Killing: Unlike diethylcarbamazine (DEC), which is strictly contraindicated in active onchocerciasis because it causes rapid microfilarial destruction that exacerbates ocular inflammation and worsens blinding lesions, ivermectin kills ocular microfilariae much more slowly and safely, seldom worsening corneal or retinal pathology, making it the preferred agent.
    - Hookworm Unresponsiveness: While ivermectin has outstanding broad-spectrum efficacy against most soil-transmitted helminths and filarial microfilariae, it exhibits an exception in that it has virtually zero efficacy against the major hookworm species Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale.
    - Stage-Specific Filarial Activity: Although highly potent at eradicating circulating and cutaneous microfilariae of Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi, Loa loa, and Mansonella ozzardi, ivermectin has absolutely zero clinical efficacy against the adult (macrofilaria) stages of these same filarial species, meaning it is not directly curative for these infections.
