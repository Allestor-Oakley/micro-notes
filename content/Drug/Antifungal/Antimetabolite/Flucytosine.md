### Flucytosine
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:**
    - Synthetic pyrimidine analog (fluorinated pyrimidine) subclass of antifungal agents.
*   **Target Organisms:**
    - Cryptococcus neoformans, Candida species, and dematiaceous molds causing chromoblastomycosis.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    - Flucytosine is actively transported into fungal cells by the enzyme cytosine permease.
    - Inside the fungal cell, it is deaminated to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) by the enzyme cytosine deaminase. Selectivity is achieved because mammalian cells lack cytosine deaminase, preventing conversion of the drug to its toxic metabolites in host tissues.
    - The generated 5-FU is subsequently metabolized to 5-fluorouracil-ribose monophosphate (5-FUMP) by the enzyme uracil phosphoribosyl transferase (UPRTase, also known as uridine monophosphate pyrophosphorylase).
    - 5-FUMP is then either:
      1. Converted to 5-fluorouridine triphosphate (5-FUTP) and directly incorporated into fungal RNA, leading to faulty translation and inhibition of protein synthesis.
      2. Converted by ribonucleotide reductase to 5-fluoro-2-deoxyuridine-5-monophosphate (5-FdUMP), which is a potent, irreversible inhibitor of thymidylate synthase. This blockade prevents the conversion of deoxyuridine monophosphate (dUMP) to deoxythymidine monophosphate (dTMP), depleting the pool of thymidine required for DNA synthesis and resulting in impaired fungal DNA replication.
    - Synergistic activity occurs when combined with amphotericin B, possibly because amphotericin B-induced cell membrane damage enhances the penetration of flucytosine into the fungal cell.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:**
    - Fungal RNA (disrupted by 5-FUTP incorporation) and the enzyme thymidylate synthase (inhibited by 5-FdUMP). Cytosine permease, cytosine deaminase, and UPRTase are essential fungal pathway enzymes required for activation of the drug.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):**
    - Fungistatic when used alone, but exhibits synergistic fungicidal activity when combined with amphotericin B.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    - Secondary resistance develops rapidly during flucytosine monotherapy.
    - The major resistance mechanisms include:
      1. Loss or downregulation of the cytosine permease transporter necessary for drug uptake.
      2. Decreased activity of cytosine deaminase or UPRTase, preventing intracellular activation.
      3. In Candida albicans, a specific nucleotide substitution (thymidine for cytosine at nucleotide 301) in the FURI/FUR1 gene encoding UPRTase causes a cysteine-to-arginine amino acid substitution, modestly increasing resistance. Resistance is further increased if both FURI/FUR1 alleles in the diploid fungus are mutated.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:**
    - Rapidly and almost completely absorbed (>90%) from the gastrointestinal tract following oral administration, with peak serum concentrations achieved 1 to 2 hours post-dose.
    - For patients too ill to take oral medications, it is administered via intravenous infusion.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:**
    - Poorly bound to plasma proteins.
    - Widely distributed throughout body tissues and fluid compartments, including the aqueous humor.
    - Exhibits excellent penetration into the central nervous system, with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) concentrations reaching approximately 65% to 90% of simultaneous plasma levels.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    - Not metabolized by host hepatic enzymes, and it does not significantly interact with or inhibit the Cytochrome P450 system.
    - Mammalian cells cannot convert flucytosine to 5-fluorouracil due to the absence of cytosine deaminase. However, intestinal microflora (gut bacteria) can deaminate flucytosine to 5-fluorouracil, which is absorbed systemically and accounts for major host toxicities.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    - Eliminated almost entirely by the kidneys via glomerular filtration.
    - Approximately 90% of an administered dose is excreted unchanged in the urine.
    - The plasma elimination half-life is approximately 3 to 4 hours (or 3 to 5 hours) in patients with normal renal function, but is extremely prolonged in renal impairment.
    - The drug is efficiently removed by hemodialysis.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - Renal Impairment: Strict dosage reductions and/or extensions of the dosing interval are mandatory in patients with impaired renal function to avoid rapid drug accumulation and severe toxicity.
    - Serum drug concentrations should be monitored periodically in patients with renal failure, adjusting doses to maintain peak concentrations between 50 and 100 mcg/mL (and always below 100 mcg/mL to minimize toxicity).

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    - Cryptococcal meningitis: Administered in combination with amphotericin B (particularly during the first 2 weeks of induction therapy in both AIDS and non-AIDS patients).
    - Chromoblastomycosis: Administered in combination with itraconazole.
    - Severe, deeply invasive candidiasis (such as systemic candidemia or candidal endocarditis): Administered in combination with amphotericin B.
    - Candidal urinary tract infections: Has limited utility as monotherapy for infections caused by fluconazole-resistant Candida strains.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:**
    - Active against Cryptococcus neoformans, Candida species (such as Candida albicans, though some strains display intrinsic or acquired resistance), and the dematiaceous molds causing chromoblastomycosis. It lacks useful antibacterial, antiparasitic, or broader mold activity (such as against Aspergillus or agents of mucormycosis).
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    - An all-oral combination regimen of flucytosine plus fluconazole has been used as an alternative therapy for AIDS patients with cryptococcosis, although this combination is associated with substantial gastrointestinal toxicity without established clinical superiority.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:**
    - Gastrointestinal disturbances including nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea.
    - Reversible alopecia.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    - Bone marrow suppression/myelosuppression (manifesting as dose-limiting anemia, leukopenia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia).
    - Severe colitis and enterocolitis.
    - Hepatotoxicity (manifesting as rare clinical hepatitis or transaminase elevations).
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:**
    - High risk of severe, concentration-dependent bone marrow toxicity or enterocolitis if flucytosine doses are not promptly adjusted downward when renal impairment (azotemia) is induced by concurrent amphotericin B therapy.
    - Myelosuppression and alopecia caused by the metabolic conversion of flucytosine to the chemotherapeutic agent 5-fluorouracil by host gastrointestinal microflora.

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    - Documented history of severe hypersensitivity, anaphylaxis, or serious toxic reactions to flucytosine or any component of the formulation.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - Preexisting renal impairment or renal insufficiency (requires intensive monitoring of renal function and serum drug levels).
    - Preexisting bone marrow suppression or hematological disorders (requires weekly monitoring of leukocyte and platelet counts).
    - AIDS patients (heightened risk of developing myelosuppressive toxicity).
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - Amphotericin B: Highly synergistic combination; however, amphotericin B-induced nephrotoxicity (azotemia) directly impairs the renal clearance of flucytosine, causing rapid flucytosine accumulation and severe myelosuppression if the dose is not adjusted.
    - Uracil: Co-administration is reported to decrease the myelosuppressive toxic effects of flucytosine on the host bone marrow without impairing its antifungal efficacy.
    - Other myelosuppressive drugs: Concurrent use may result in additive hematological toxicity (the risk of anemia is additive in patients receiving concurrent zidovudine).

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "Ancobon," the proprietary trade name.
    - "Cytosine permease and cytosine deaminase," the specific pathway enzymes required for intracellular activation.
    - "5-FU converter," referencing its conversion to 5-fluorouracil.
    - "Thymidylate synthase inhibitor," describing the mechanism of DNA synthesis blockade.
    - "Conversion by gut bacteria," highlighting the gastrointestinal flora-mediated toxicity pathway.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - While virtually all other systemic antifungal agents (such as polyene macrolides, azoles, and echinocandins) target fungal cell membrane or cell wall components, flucytosine is unique because it acts intracellularly to directly inhibit DNA and RNA synthesis.
    - Although flucytosine is highly effective in combination, it is almost never used as monotherapy because resistance develops rapidly when it is used alone, with the rare exception of localized lower urinary tract infections caused by fluconazole-resistant Candida.
    - Although designed to be selectively toxic to fungi due to the lack of cytosine deaminase in mammalian cells, the host's normal gut bacteria express this enzyme and deaminate flucytosine to the highly toxic antimetabolite 5-fluorouracil, causing systemic bone marrow suppression and alopecia.
