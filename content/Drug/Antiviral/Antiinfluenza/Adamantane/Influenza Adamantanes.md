### Influenza Adamantanes
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:** Antiviral agents; uniquely configured tricyclic amines; adamantanes (amantadine and rimantadine).
*   **Target Organisms:** Influenza A virus exclusively (including H1N1, H1N2, and H3N2 subtypes). They have no activity against influenza B.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:** They inhibit an early step in viral replication, specifically viral uncoating. In some strains, they also affect a late step in viral assembly, likely by altering hemagglutinin processing. The primary action is blocking the M2 proton ion channel function of the virus particle. Normally, the host cell endosome is acidified following an influx of hydrogen ions (H+) through the M2 ion channel, which promotes dissociation of the ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex and its release into the host cytoplasm (uncoating). By interfering with this function of the M2 protein, amantadine and rimantadine inhibit the acid-mediated dissociation of the RNP complex early in replication. Later in replication, they also potentiate acidic pH–induced conformational changes in the hemagglutinin during its intracellular transport.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:** The M2 proton ion channel (an integral membrane protein) of the influenza A virus particle.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):** Virustatic (they are active only against replicating viruses and do not affect latent virus).
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:** Resistance results from a single point mutation in the RNA sequence encoding the transmembrane domain of the M2 protein. Resistant isolates typically appear in treated patients within 2 to 3 days of initiating therapy. Utility of these drugs is severely limited by the rapid development and spread of resistance; virtually all H3N2 and seasonal H1N1 strains circulating worldwide are resistant (>99% resistance).

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:** Both drugs are well absorbed after oral administration (>90% oral bioavailability). Peak plasma concentrations of amantadine average 0.5–0.8 mcg/mL on a 100-mg twice-daily regimen in healthy young adults. Doses of 100 mg of rimantadine twice daily give peak and trough plasma concentrations of approximately 0.4–0.5 mcg/mL and 0.2–0.4 mcg/mL, respectively. Food has a negligible effect on their AUC.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:** Both drugs have very large volumes of distribution. Nasal secretion and salivary levels of amantadine approximate those found in the serum, and amantadine is excreted in breast milk. Amantadine is 67% protein-bound. Rimantadine is about 40% protein-bound. Rimantadine concentrations in nasal mucus average 50% higher than those in plasma, and cerebrospinal fluid levels are 52–96% of those in the serum.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:** Amantadine is excreted largely unmetabolized (less than 10% metabolized). In contrast, rimantadine is metabolized extensively (approximately 75%) by hydroxylation, conjugation, and glucuronidation in the liver prior to renal excretion. Neither is recognized as a significant CYP inhibitor.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    *   **Amantadine:** Excreted largely unchanged in the urine through glomerular filtration and tubular secretion. The plasma elimination half-life is approximately 12–18 hours in young adults. It is highly dependent on renal function, increasing up to 2-fold in the elderly and even more in those with renal impairment.
    *   **Rimantadine:** Undergoes extensive metabolism before renal excretion of metabolites and unchanged drug (only 25% excreted unchanged). The elimination half-life averages 24–36 hours.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    *   **Amantadine:** Dose reductions are required for the elderly (who require only half the weight-adjusted dose of younger adults to achieve equivalent trough levels of 0.3 mcg/mL) and for patients with renal impairment (creatinine clearance <= 50 mL/min).
    *   **Rimantadine:** Dose reductions are required for the elderly, for patients with renal insufficiency (creatinine clearance <= 10 mL/min), and for patients with severe hepatic insufficiency.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:** Prevention (chemoprophylaxis) and treatment of infections caused by susceptible strains of influenza A virus. Prophylaxis with either agent (200 mg/day in one or two divided doses in young adults) is 70–90% protective against influenza A illness. Early treatment (200 mg/day for 5 days in adults, initiated within 48 hours of symptom onset) reduces the duration of fever and systemic complaints by 1–2 days, speeds functional recovery, and decreases the duration of virus shedding. In children (>=1 year of age), the dose is 5 mg/kg/day, up to 150 mg, administered once or twice daily, which reduces illness severity and viral titers but may be associated with more prolonged shedding of virus.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:** Influenza A viruses exclusively (including susceptible H1N1, H1N2, and H3N2 subtypes). They have no activity against influenza B.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    *   **Amantadine:** Widely used for its mildly beneficial effects in Parkinson's disease due to alteration of dopamine neurotransmission.
    *   **Rimantadine:** In vitro inhibition of Trypanosoma brucei, the cause of African sleeping sickness, at concentrations of 1–2.5 mcg/mL.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:** Gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, anorexia) and central nervous system symptoms (nervousness, difficulty in concentrating, insomnia, light-headedness).
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:** High plasma concentrations of amantadine (1.0–5.0 mcg/mL) are associated with serious neurotoxic reactions, including delirium, hallucinosis, confusion, agitation, seizures, coma, and cardiac arrhythmias. Exacerbations of preexisting seizure disorders and psychiatric symptoms may occur. Both agents are teratogenic and embryotoxic in rodents, and birth defects have been reported after exposure during pregnancy.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:** Dose-related serious neurotoxic reactions (delirium, hallucinations, and seizures) specifically associated with high plasma levels (1.0–5.0 mcg/mL) due to drug-induced alterations of dopamine neurotransmission.
*   **Black Box Warnings:** N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:** History of severe hypersensitivity to amantadine, rimantadine, or other adamantane derivatives.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    *   **Pregnancy & Lactation:** Both are Pregnancy Category C and should be avoided during pregnancy unless benefits outweigh risks; amantadine is excreted in breast milk.
    *   **Seizure & Psychiatric Disorders:** Use with extreme caution as both agents may exacerbate preexisting seizure disorders and psychiatric symptoms.
    *   **Elderly & Renal/Hepatic Impairment:** Requires mandatory dose adjustments and close monitoring.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:** Concomitant administration of antihistamines, anticholinergic drugs, hydrochlorothiazide, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole can increase plasma concentrations of amantadine or rimantadine, increasing the risk of serious neurotoxic reactions and cognitive side effects.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** Both are Pregnancy Category C. They are teratogenic and embryotoxic in rodents, and birth defects have been reported after human exposure during pregnancy.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** M2 ion channel blocker, viral uncoating inhibitor, exclusive Influenza A coverage, dopaminergic drug for Parkinson's disease, adamantane tricyclic amines, high rate of global resistance (>99%), anticholinergic-like overdose.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   Unlike other anti-influenza classes (neuraminidase inhibitors or cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitors) which are active against both influenza A and B, the adamantanes are active only against influenza A because influenza B viruses do not possess an M2 protein susceptible to these agents.
    *   Although developed as an antiviral, amantadine is clinically utilized more frequently today in developed countries as an anti-Parkinson's disease medication due to its ability to alter dopamine neurotransmission.
    *   Despite being classified as an antiviral targeting a specific viral protein, amantadine overdose exhibits clinical manifestations that mimic acute anticholinergic poisoning.
    *   Unlike amantadine, which is eliminated almost entirely unchanged via the kidneys (highly polar), rimantadine is extensively metabolized by the liver before excretion, necessitating dose reductions in patients with severe hepatic insufficiency but making its half-life less abruptly sensitive to minor renal changes.
