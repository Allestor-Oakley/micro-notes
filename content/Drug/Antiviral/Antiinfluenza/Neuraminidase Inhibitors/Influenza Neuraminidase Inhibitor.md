### Influenza Neuraminidase Inhibitors
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:** Antiviral agents; neuraminidase inhibitors; transition-state analogs of sialic acid (oseltamivir and zanamivir) and cyclopentane analog (peramivir).
*   **Target Organisms:** Influenza A and Influenza B viruses.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:** Influenza neuraminidase cleaves terminal sialic acid residues and destroys the receptors recognized by viral hemagglutinin, which are present on the cell surface, in progeny virions, and in respiratory secretions. This enzymatic action is essential for the release of progeny virus from infected host cells. The neuraminidase inhibitors competitively and reversibly interact with the active enzyme site of viral neuraminidase. The interaction of the active form oseltamivir carboxylate with the neuraminidase causes a conformational change within the enzyme's active site and inhibits its activity. Similarly, zanamivir specifically and potently inhibits the neuraminidases of influenza A and B viruses. This inhibition of neuraminidase activity leads to viral aggregation (clumping) at the cell surface, prevents the release of completed progeny virions, and halts the spread of infection within the respiratory tract. Early administration is crucial because replication of influenza virus peaks at 24 to 72 hours after the onset of illness.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:** Active enzyme site of viral neuraminidase coded by the influenza A and B genomes.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):** Virustatic (active only against replicating viruses and does not affect latent virus).
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:** Resistance to neuraminidase inhibitors can emerge during therapy and is transmissible. In the 2008–2009 influenza season, virtually all seasonal strains of influenza A H1N1 were resistant to oseltamivir due to resistance emergence and the spread of resistant viruses, while the majority of 2009 H1N1 swine viruses remained susceptible. All such resistant H1N1 viruses tested were resistant to the adamantane antivirals (amantadine and rimantadine). Resistance is driven by selection pressure, often exacerbated by overuse of these drugs, including in veterinary applications. Some oseltamivir-resistant variants remain susceptible to zanamivir. A long-acting neuraminidase inhibitor, laninamivir octanoate, may also retain activity against oseltamivir-resistant virus. In the 2014–2015 season, >98% of H1N1 and H3N2 strains and 100% of influenza B viruses tested by the CDC retained susceptibility to both oseltamivir and zanamivir.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:**
    *   **Oseltamivir:** Administered orally. Oseltamivir phosphate is an ethyl ester prodrug that lacks antiviral activity but is highly bioavailable, with an oral bioavailability of approximately 80% for the active carboxylate form. Food has a negligible effect on its AUC.
    *   **Zanamivir:** Inhaled formulation has an oral bioavailability of <5%, with systemic absorption of 4–17% after inhalation. Food has no applicable effect. It can also be administered intravenously (currently investigational).
    *   **Peramivir:** Administered intravenously (approved as a single 600-mg IV dose).
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:**
    *   **Oseltamivir:** Low protein binding of approximately 3% for the active carboxylate form. In unweaned rats, very high doses are associated with increased brain concentrations.
    *   **Zanamivir:** Low protein binding of <10%. Systemic absorption after inhalation is low, but local concentrations in respiratory secretions are high.
    *   **Peramivir:** Excreted renally.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    *   **Oseltamivir:** Oseltamivir phosphate is converted to the active metabolite oseltamivir carboxylate. Oseltamivir phosphate and the active carboxylate do not interact with CYP enzymes in vitro, and they do not exhibit any clinically significant drug interactions. Metabolism percentage is negligible.
    *   **Zanamivir:** Negligible metabolism.
    *   **Peramivir:** Negligible metabolism.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    *   **Oseltamivir:** Active carboxylate form has an elimination half-life of 6–10 hours. It is excreted primarily via the kidneys, with approximately 95% of the dose excreted as unchanged drug in the urine.
    *   **Zanamivir:** Elimination half-life is 2.5–5 hours. It is excreted 100% renally.
    *   **Peramivir:** Primarily renally eliminated.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    *   **Oseltamivir:** Requires dosage adjustment for patients with a creatinine clearance (Clcr) of <=30 mL/min. Dosage adjustments are also considered for patients age >=65 years due to renal function decline.
    *   **Zanamivir:** No dosage adjustment required for the inhaled formulation.
    *   **Peramivir:** Requires dosage adjustment for renal insufficiency.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    *   **Oseltamivir:** Approved for the treatment and prevention (prophylaxis) of influenza A and B virus infections in adults and pediatric patients. Prophylaxis can be seasonal (70–90% protective against illness) or short-term post-exposure prophylaxis (7–10 days) protecting against influenza in household contacts.
    *   **Zanamivir:** Approved for the treatment and prevention of influenza A and B virus infections in adults and pediatric patients (inhaled formulation licensed for patients >=5 years of age for prevention).
    *   **Peramivir:** Approved as a single 600-mg IV dose for the treatment of acute uncomplicated influenza in adults (within 48 hours of symptom onset).
    *   **Emergency Use Authorization (EUA):** During H1N1 outbreaks, EUAs were granted by the FDA, CDC, and WHO to allow treatment and prophylaxis in pediatric patients <=1 year of age and to make intravenous peramivir and oseltamivir available.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:** Influenza A and B viruses (including amantadine- and rimantadine-resistant strains).
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:** Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) for pediatric patients <=1 year of age who were not covered under the original approved age group. Compassionate use of intravenous zanamivir.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:**
    *   **Oseltamivir:** Gastrointestinal symptoms, including nausea, vomiting, dyspepsia, and diarrhea. Headache.
    *   **Zanamivir:** Well tolerated in ambulatory patients. Common side effects include rash.
    *   **Peramivir:** Gastrointestinal symptoms.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    *   **Zanamivir:** Wheezing and bronchospasm. Acute, severe deteriorations in lung function, including fatal outcomes, have occurred in patients with underlying asthma or chronic obstructive airway disease.
    *   **Oseltamivir:** High doses are associated with increased mortality in unweaned rats, potentially due to elevated brain concentrations.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:** Zanamivir-induced bronchospasm and acute decrement in pulmonary function specifically in patients with underlying reactive airway disease (asthma/COPD).
*   **Black Box Warnings:** N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:** Zanamivir is not generally recommended and is contraindicated for patients with underlying airway disease (such as asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) because of the risk of serious bronchospasm and fatal deterioration in lung function.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    *   Pregnancy Category C for both oseltamivir and zanamivir. Preclinical studies show no direct teratogenicity, but safety in human pregnancy is not established.
    *   Oseltamivir use in infants <=1 year of age (permitted under EUA, but should be used with caution).
    *   Caution when administering inhaled zanamivir to patients with serious bronchopulmonary disorders or those who are intubated.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:** No clinically significant drug interactions have been recognized to date for oseltamivir or zanamivir. Oseltamivir phosphate and carboxylate do not interact with CYP enzymes in vitro. Zanamivir does not diminish the immune response to injected influenza vaccine.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** Both are pregnancy Category C. Zanamivir preclinical studies revealed no evidence of mutagenic, teratogenic, or oncogenic effects. Oseltamivir does not appear to impair fertility or be teratogenic in animal studies.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Sialic acid analogs, transition-state analogs, active site blockers, H1N1 swine flu stockpiling, Emergency Use Authorization (EUA), disc inhaler, Tamiflu, Relenza, peramivir single IV dose.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   Unlike amantadine and rimantadine which only target influenza A, neuraminidase inhibitors target both influenza A and B.
    *   Zanamivir is administered by oral inhalation of dry powder, bypassing the gastrointestinal tract completely due to its poor oral bioavailability (<5%).
    *   Although replication of most RNA viruses is completed in the cytoplasm, influenza virus is transcribed in the host cell nucleus.
    *   Rather than blocking early stages of viral lifecycle like cell entry or uncoating (targeted by amantadine), neuraminidase inhibitors act on the final stage of release and budding to prevent progeny virions from escaping the infected cell.
