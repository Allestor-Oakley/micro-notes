### Sulfonamide
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:**
    - Sulfonamide class of synthetic antimicrobials. They are structural derivatives of para-aminobenzenesulfonamide (sulfanilamide).
    - Subclasses are categorized based on their pharmacokinetic properties and routes of administration:
      1. Rapidly absorbed and eliminated agents (e.g., sulfisoxazole, sulfadiazine, sulfamethoxazole).
      2. Poorly absorbed agents that remain active in the bowel lumen (e.g., sulfasalazine).
      3. Topical agents (e.g., sulfacetamide, mafenide acetate, silver sulfadiazine).
      4. Long-acting agents that are absorbed rapidly but excreted slowly (e.g., sulfadoxine).
*   **Target Organisms:**
    - Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, certain protozoa (such as Toxoplasma gondii and Plasmodium species), and certain fungi (specifically the yeast-like fungus Pneumocystis jirovecii).
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    - Sulfonamides are structural analogs of para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA). They act as competitive inhibitors of dihydropteroate synthase (dihydropteroate synthetase), the bacterial enzyme responsible for the incorporation of PABA into dihydropteroic acid, which is the immediate precursor of folic acid (dihydrofolic acid).
    - By preventing normal microbial utilization of PABA, they block the synthesis of folic acid (pteroylglutamic acid). This pathway is essential for the production of purines, thymidylate, and bacterial DNA/RNA synthesis.
    - Sensitive microorganisms are those that must synthesize their own folic acid de novo. In contrast, mammalian cells require preformed folic acid from the diet, cannot synthesize it, and lack the dihydropteroate synthase enzyme. This biochemical difference provides the basis for selective toxicity.
    - The bacteriostatic effect of sulfonamides can be competitively counteracted by excess PABA or by products of tissue breakdown and pus (which contain thymidine and purines that bacteria can utilize directly, bypassing the folate pathway).
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:**
    - Dihydropteroate synthase (specifically encoded by the folP1 and folP2 genes in mycobacteria).
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):**
    - Bacteriostatic when used as monotherapy. However, when combined with dihydrofolate reductase inhibitors (such as trimethoprim or pyrimethamine), the combination introduces sequential blocks in the folate pathway, producing synergistic bactericidal activity against many susceptible strains.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    - Intrinsic resistance: Microorganisms that lack the enzymes required for folate synthesis from PABA and, like mammals, depend entirely on exogenous sources of folate are naturally insensitive to sulfonamides.
    - Acquired resistance: Highly common and typically plasmid-mediated, allowing rapid dissemination under selective pressure.
    - Major mechanisms of resistance include:
      1. Production of a mutated or plasmid-encoded dihydropteroate synthase enzyme with a low affinity for sulfonamides.
      2. Overproduction of PABA, which competitively counteracts and overcomes the drug's inhibitory effects.
      3. Impaired cell permeability to the sulfonamide drug.
    - Specific gene mutations: Mutations in genes encoding dihydropteroate synthase are associated with clinical resistance. In Plasmodium falciparum, mutations occur at codons 436, 437, 540, 58, and 613. In Pneumocystis jirovecii isolates, mutations typically consist of amino acid substitutions at positions 55 and 57 of dihydropteroate synthase. In Mycobacterium leprae, resistance mutations are encountered at codons 53 and 55.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:**
    - Oral absorbable sulfonamides (e.g., sulfisoxazole, sulfadiazine, sulfamethoxazole, sulfadoxine) are rapidly and well absorbed from the stomach and small intestine, with peak blood levels generally achieved 2 to 6 hours after oral administration.
    - Poorly absorbed oral sulfonamides (e.g., sulfasalazine) are absorbed very poorly from the gastrointestinal tract and remain active within the bowel lumen.
    - Topical sulfonamides are applied directly. Sodium sulfacetamide is highly water-soluble and penetrates into ocular fluids and tissues in high concentrations. Mafenide acetate is rapidly absorbed systemically from burn surfaces, with peak plasma concentrations reached in 2 to 4 hours.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:**
    - Oral absorbable sulfonamides are widely distributed to tissues, body fluids (including the central nervous system and cerebrospinal fluid), the placenta, and the fetus.
    - Plasma protein binding varies widely among different agents, ranging from 20% to over 90%. Therapeutic concentrations in blood are generally in the range of 40 to 100 mcg/mL.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    - A portion of the absorbed drug is acetylated (at the N4 amino position) or glucuronidated in the liver.
    - Genetic polymorphisms in N-acetyltransferase 2 (NAT2) characterize fast, intermediate, and slow acetylators. Slow acetylators have a diminished capacity to clear acetylated drugs and are at a significantly higher risk of experiencing adverse toxic effects from sulfonamides.
    - CYP450 Dynamics: N/A
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    - Sulfonamides and their inactive metabolites are excreted in the urine, primarily by glomerular filtration. Sulfonamides are weak acids and tend to be much more soluble in alkaline than in acidic urine.
    - Elimination half-lives vary significantly by drug subclass:
      - Sulfisoxazole: 5 to 6 hours.
      - Sulfamethoxazole: 11 hours.
      - Sulfadiazine: 10 hours.
      - Sulfadoxine: 100 to 230 hours (exhibits exceptionally slow renal clearance).
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - The dosage of all sulfonamides must be reduced in patients with significant renal failure to prevent severe systemic accumulation and drug toxicity. Long-acting sulfonamides (such as sulfadoxine) require extreme caution or are contraindicated in patients with marked renal impairment.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    - Nocardiosis: Sulfonamides (such as sulfisoxazole or sulfadiazine at 6 to 8 g daily, keeping plasma levels at 80 to 160 mcg/mL) are considered first-line agents or drugs of choice (often as the combination trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) for treating infections caused by Nocardia species.
    - Toxoplasmosis: Sulfadiazine administered in combination with pyrimethamine (a potent inhibitor of dihydrofolate reductase) is the first-line therapy of choice for treating acute acquired, congenital, or immunocompromised toxoplasmosis (such as toxoplasmic encephalitis in AIDS patients), co-administered with leucovorin (folinic acid) to minimize bone marrow toxicity.
    - Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs): Previously first-line agents, but because of high community resistance, they are used primarily in areas where resistance is low or when the uropathogen is confirmed sensitive (using sulfisoxazole at 2 to 4 g initially followed by 1 to 2 g four times daily). They are not used for acute pyelonephritis with high fever.
    - Malaria: Sulfadoxine combined with pyrimethamine (Fansidar) is used for the treatment and prophylaxis of mefloquine-resistant Plasmodium falciparum malaria, although its clinical utility is severely limited by toxicities and resistance. Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine combined with amodiaquine is recommended for short-term seasonal malaria chemoprevention in Africa.
    - Infected Burns: Silver sulfadiazine cream is applied topically once or twice daily to prevent bacterial colonization and infection of burn wounds (it is preferred over mafenide due to lower toxicity). Mafenide acetate cream is also used topically to prevent colonization of burns.
    - Ophthalmic Infections: Sodium sulfacetamide solutions (up to 30%) or ointments are used topically to treat bacterial conjunctivitis and as adjunctive therapy for trachoma.
    - Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD): Sulfasalazine (a combination of sulfapyridine and aminosalicylate) is widely used to treat ulcerative colitis, enteritis, and other inflammatory bowel diseases.
    - Rheumatic Fever Prophylaxis: Sulfonamides are as effective as oral penicillin in preventing streptococcal infections and recurrences of rheumatic fever in susceptible patients, and are the drugs of choice for patients who are hypersensitive to penicillin.
    - Pneumocystis jirovecii Pneumonia (PCP): The fixed-dose combination trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMZ) is the first-line drug of choice for the treatment and prophylaxis of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia in immunocompromised patients, particularly those with AIDS.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:**
    - Gram-positive bacteria: Staphylococcus species (including methicillin-susceptible S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and some MRSA), Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, viridans streptococci, Nocardia species, and Actinomyces. (Enterococci are typically resistant to folate inhibitors like trimethoprim but can have variable susceptibility to sulfonamides, and S. pneumoniae has increasing resistance).
    - Gram-negative bacteria: Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, Salmonella, Shigella, Enterobacter species, Moraxella catarrhalis, Haemophilus influenzae, Neisseria meningitidis (formerly highly susceptible, but many isolates of serogroups B and C in the US are now resistant), Vibrio species, and Brucella.
    - Protozoa: Toxoplasma gondii, Plasmodium falciparum, and certain other Plasmodium species.
    - Fungi: Pneumocystis jirovecii (formerly P. carinii).
    - Chlamydiae: Chlamydia trachomatis.
    - Resistant Organisms: Pseudomonas aeruginosa is intrinsically resistant. Anaerobic bacteria are generally poorly susceptible. Increasingly, there is high resistance among E. coli (up to 30% or more) and S. pneumoniae.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    - Alternative prophylactic agents against streptococcal infections in patients with a history of rheumatic fever who are hypersensitive to penicillin.
    - Sulfadiazine is used off-label as part of alternative regimens for treating other protozoal or atypical infections in immunocompromised individuals.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:**
    - Gastrointestinal disturbances, including nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal discomfort, and anorexia.
    - Mild hypersensitivity skin reactions, such as drug fever, pruritus, and various types of skin rashes.
    - Local pain, irritation, or intense burning at application sites for topical formulations (especially mafenide acetate).
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    - Crystalluria: Crystallization of sulfonamides in the urine can lead to renal damage, dysuria, and nephrotoxicity. (Prevented by maintaining high fluid intake of at least 2 liters daily and keeping the urine alkaline).
    - Hematopoietic Disturbances: Acute hemolytic anemia (especially in G6PD deficient patients), aplastic anemia (complete bone marrow suppression), granulocytopenia, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, or leukemoid reactions. Aplastic anemia can be fatal.
    - Severe Cutaneous Adverse Reactions (SCARs): Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), which can be fatal.
    - Metabolic Acidosis: Mafenide acetate and its metabolite inhibit carbonic anhydrase, causing alkaline urine and systemic metabolic acidosis with compensatory tachypnea and hyperventilation.
    - Kernicterus: Sulfonamides cross the placenta and enter the fetus; when taken near the end of pregnancy, they compete with bilirubin for binding to serum albumin, increasing free bilirubin and the risk of kernicterus in newborns.
    - Hepatic toxicity and clinical hepatitis.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:**
    - Extreme frequency of untoward reactions in patients with AIDS: Patients with AIDS who are treated for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia have an exceptionally high frequency of drug reactions (including severe skin rashes, drug fever, leukopenia, diarrhea, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and elevations of hepatic aminotransferases).
    - White blood cell count monitoring: It is recommended to carry out white blood cell counts once weekly during the first 8 weeks of long-term rheumatic fever prophylaxis with sulfonamides, as most serious hematologic reactions occur during this initial period.

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    - A history of immediate hypersensitivity or severe allergic reactions to sulfonamides, sulfanilamide derivatives, or trimethoprim.
    - Use near the end of pregnancy (third trimester or at term) and during lactation (due to the risk of kernicterus in the newborn).
    - Infants less than 2 months of age (except for congenital toxoplasmosis, where therapy may be indicated under close supervision).
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency (risk of provoking severe hemolytic anemia).
    - Preexisting bone marrow suppression, blood dyscrasias, or limited bone marrow reserve (e.g., patients with AIDS or those receiving myelosuppressive chemotherapy).
    - Significant renal or hepatic insufficiency.
    - Optic neuropathy or thyroid disease (specifically for iodoquinol, but general caution for halogenated or sulfonamide-like derivatives).
    - Known iodine intolerance (for halogenated compounds, if combined).
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - Local anesthetics that are esters of para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) (such as procaine) can antagonize the antibacterial effect of sulfonamides by competing for the dihydropteroate synthase enzyme.
    - Antifolate drugs (e.g., pyrimethamine, methotrexate): Co-administration increases the risk of folate deficiency and severe bone marrow suppression (myelotoxicity).
    - Sulfonamides (particularly when combined as co-trimoxazole) may interact with oral anticoagulants (like warfarin) due to displacement from albumin binding sites or inhibition of metabolism, potentially increasing the risk of bleeding.
    - Concurrent administration with other neuropathic or myelosuppressive drugs increases the risk of additive toxicities.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:**
    - Contraindicated near term or at the end of pregnancy due to the risk of competing with bilirubin for binding sites on albumin, leading to free bilirubin crossing the blood-brain barrier and causing kernicterus in the newborn.
    - Sulfonamides readily cross the placenta and enter the fetus. They have potential teratogenic risks, particularly when used in the first trimester, due to the inhibition of folate synthesis.
    - No specific FDA pregnancy category is explicitly assigned to the entire class in the texts, but long-acting sulfonamides and combinations are generally avoided during pregnancy unless absolutely necessary.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "Competitive inhibition of PABA" and "structural analog of para-aminobenzoic acid," highlighting the target of dihydropteroate synthase.
    - "Sequential blockade" or "sequential inhibition," which describes the synergistic mechanism when a sulfonamide is combined with a dihydrofolate reductase inhibitor.
    - "Prontosil" — the red azo dye containing a sulfonamide group patented in 1932 by I.G. Farbenindustrie, which led to Domagk being awarded the Nobel Prize in Medicine in 1938.
    - "One night with Venus, a lifetime with Mercury" — a historical reference to the pre-antibiotic era treatment of syphilis, before Domagk's and Ehrlich's discoveries led to the development of selective chemotherapeutic agents.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - Rickettsiae are not inhibited by sulfonamides but are instead stimulated in their growth, making infections with rickettsiae worsen if sulfonamides are administered.
    - Pseudomonas aeruginosa is completely and intrinsically resistant to sulfonamides.
    - Enterococci are typically resistant to folate antagonist therapy because they can bypass the blockade of folate synthesis by utilizing preformed exogenous folates from their environment.
    - Pus and tissue breakdown products contain high concentrations of thymidine and purines, which bacteria can take up and utilize directly to bypass the folate synthesis pathway, completely neutralizing (vitiating) the antibacterial effect of sulfonamides.
