### Trimethoprim
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:**
    - Folate antagonist (dihydrofolate reductase inhibitor), belonging to the trimethoxybenzylpyrimidine chemical class.
*   **Target Organisms:**
    - Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, certain protozoa (such as Toxoplasma gondii), and the yeast-like fungus Pneumocystis jirovecii (formerly Pneumocystis carinii).
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    - Trimethoprim selectively and competitively inhibits bacterial dihydrofolic acid reductase (dihydrofolate reductase), the enzyme that reduces dihydrofolic acid (dihydrofolate) to tetrahydrofolic acid (tetrahydrofolate).
    - Tetrahydrofolate is an essential co-factor in one-carbon transfer reactions, most notably the synthesis of thymidylate from deoxyuridylate, which is required for microbial purine production and ultimately DNA/RNA synthesis.
    - High selective toxicity is achieved because human cells do not synthesize folates de novo (utilizing preformed dietary folates instead) and because trimethoprim is a highly selective inhibitor of the lower organism's enzyme. Approximately 100,000 times more drug is required to inhibit the human dihydrofolate reductase than the bacterial enzyme (the bacterial enzyme IC50 is 0.005 µmol/L, the protozoal enzyme IC50 is 0.07 µmol/L, whereas the human enzyme IC50 is 260 µmol/L).
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:**
    - Bacterial dihydrofolate reductase (dihydrofolic acid reductase).
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):**
    - Bacteriostatic when administered as monotherapy. However, when combined with a sulfonamide (such as sulfamethoxazole), the combination introduces sequential blocks in the folate synthesis pathway, resulting in synergistic bactericidal activity against many susceptible strains.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    - Resistance can arise through three major mechanisms:
      1. Reduced cell permeability to the drug.
      2. Overproduction of the target enzyme dihydrofolate reductase.
      3. Production of a mutated or plasmid-encoded dihydrofolate reductase enzyme with a significantly reduced binding affinity for trimethoprim.
    - Plasmid-mediated resistance is highly common and frequently coded within transposons on conjugative plasmids that exhibit a broad host range, accounting for rapid and widespread dissemination of resistance among numerous bacterial species (particularly Enterobacteriaceae) under selective pressure.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:**
    - Rapidly and completely absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract following oral administration, displaying high systemic bioavailability. Peak blood and plasma concentrations are typically achieved within 1 to 4 hours after oral ingestion.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:**
    - Widely distributed throughout body tissues and fluids, reaching high concentrations in the lungs and kidneys, and fairly high concentrations in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (reaching approximately 60% to 70% of the concurrent serum levels).
    - As a weak base, trimethoprim concentrates significantly in relatively acidic environments such as prostatic fluid and vaginal fluid, achieving local concentrations that are higher than concurrent plasma levels and enhancing its regional antibacterial activity.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    - N/A
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    - Eliminated primarily by the kidneys via renal excretion, with approximately half of the administered dose excreted unchanged in the urine within 24 hours.
    - Because it is a weak base, its renal clearance and elimination by the kidneys increase as the urinary pH decreases (urine acidification).
    - The plasma elimination half-life is approximately 8 to 11 hours (or 12 to 18 hours in some patient populations).
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - Requires dosage adjustments (reduction in dose or extension of the dosing interval) in patients with significant renal impairment, as renal clearance is the primary route of excretion.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    - Treatment of acute uncomplicated lower urinary tract infections (UTIs) (administered alone or in combination with sulfamethoxazole).
    - Treatment and prevention (prophylaxis) of Pneumocystis jirovecii (P. carinii) pneumonia (PCP) in immunocompromised patients, particularly those with AIDS.
    - Prophylaxis of Toxoplasmosis in HIV-infected patients with IgG antibody to Toxoplasma and a CD4 count less than 100 cells/µL (frequently combined with a sulfonamide or dapsone).
    - Treatment of acute and chronic prostatitis (facilitated by its accumulation in acidic prostatic fluid).
    - Long-term low-dose prophylaxis of recurrent urinary tract infections.
    - Treatment of shigellosis (Shigella infections) and typhoid fever (Salmonella infections) (primarily in combination).
    - Treatment of travelers' diarrhea (primarily in combination).
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:**
    - Sensitive pathogens: Most common Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus (including methicillin-susceptible MSSA and methicillin-resistant MRSA), Staphylococcus epidermidis, Streptococcus pyogenes, the viridans group of streptococci, Streptococcus pneumoniae (though resistance is increasing), Escherichia coli (increasing prevalence of resistance, up to 30% or more, must be considered), Proteus mirabilis, Proteus morganii, Proteus rettgeri, Enterobacter species, Salmonella, Shigella, Pseudomonas pseudomallei, Serratia, Alcaligenes species, Klebsiella species, Brucella abortus, Pasteurella haemolytica, Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, Yersinia enterocolitica, and Nocardia asteroides. Also active against protozoa (Toxoplasma gondii, Cyclospora cayetanensis, Isospora belli) and the fungus Pneumocystis jirovecii.
    - Resistant pathogens: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Bacteroides fragilis, and enterococci (which are typically intrinsically resistant because they can bypass the folate pathway by utilizing preformed exogenous folates from their environment).
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    - Preferred alternative therapy (in combination) for serious Listeria monocytogenes infections in patients unable to tolerate ampicillin.
    - Niche therapy for Whipple's disease, infection by Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, and infections caused by the intestinal parasites Cyclospora and Isospora (primarily in combination).

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:**
    - Gastrointestinal disturbances, including nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal discomfort, dry mouth, and metallic taste.
    - Mild hypersensitivity skin reactions, including pruritus, drug fever, and various types of skin rashes.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    - Megaloblastic anemia, leukopenia, neutropenia, granulocytopenia, and thrombocytopenia (resulting from predictable bone marrow suppression/myelotoxicity associated with its antifolate activity, particularly when administered over a prolonged period or to patients with preexisting folate deficiency).
    - Hyperkalemia and hyponatremia (commonly observed during high-dose therapy in patients with AIDS).
    - Severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs), including Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) (particularly when administered in combination with a sulfonamide).
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:**
    - Exceptionally high frequency of adverse reactions in patients with AIDS and Pneumocystis pneumonia, who frequently develop drug fever, severe rashes, leukopenia, diarrhea, elevations of hepatic aminotransferases, hyperkalemia, and hyponatremia.
    - Mild, non-toxic elevation of serum creatinine without actual impairment of glomerular filtration rate (GFR). This occurs because trimethoprim competitively inhibits the active secretion of creatinine at the distal renal tubule, and this nontoxic effect must be distinguished from true sulfonamide-induced nephrotoxicity.
*   **Black Box Warnings:**
    - N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    - History of immediate hypersensitivity or severe allergic reactions to trimethoprim.
    - Documented megaloblastic anemia due to folate deficiency.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - Preexisting bone marrow suppression, blood dyscrasias, or limited bone marrow reserve.
    - Severe renal or hepatic insufficiency.
    - Pregnancy or lactation.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - Co-administration with other antifolate drugs (such as pyrimethamine, methotrexate) or myelosuppressive medications (such as zidovudine) significantly increases the risk of severe hematologic toxicity (bone marrow suppression).
    - Concurrent administration with other neuropathic or myelosuppressive drugs increases the risk of additive toxicities.
    - May interact with drugs that cause hyperkalemia or are cleared renally/hepatically.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:**
    - Trimethoprim should be avoided or used with extreme caution during pregnancy, particularly in the first trimester, due to its antifolate activity and the potential risk of inducing birth defects (teratogenicity, such as neural tube defects).

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
        - "Sequential blockade" or "sequential inhibition" of the microbial folate metabolic pathway, illustrating the synergistic mechanism when combined with a sulfonamide (such as sulfamethoxazole in a 5:1 formulation ratio in vitro, which translates to a 20:1 ratio in vivo).
        - "Weak base that concentrates in acidic fluids," highlighting its unique tissue distribution into prostatic and vaginal fluids.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
        - Enterococci are typically resistant to trimethoprim because they can bypass the folate synthesis blockade by utilizing preformed exogenous folates from their environment.
        - Pneumocystis jirovecii is genetically classified as a fungus but does not respond to traditional antifungal agents (like azoles or echinocandins) and is instead treated with folate antagonists like trimethoprim (in combination with sulfamethoxazole or dapsone).
