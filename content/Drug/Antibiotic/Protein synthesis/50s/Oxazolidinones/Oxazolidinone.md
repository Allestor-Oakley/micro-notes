### Oxazolidinones
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:**
    - Synthetic oxazolidinone class of antibacterial agents.
    - Class representatives include linezolid (the first member of this antibiotic family to be clinically introduced) and tedizolid (the active moiety of the prodrug tedizolid phosphate, representing a next-generation oxazolidinone).
*   **Target Organisms:**
    - Gram-positive aerobic bacteria, Gram-positive anaerobic bacteria, and certain mycobacteria.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    - Inhibits protein synthesis by binding to the P site of the 50S ribosomal subunit.
    - This binding prevents the formation of the functional larger ribosomal initiation complex (the 70S initiation complex consisting of the ribosome, mRNA, and fMet-tRNA) that initiates protein translation.
    - Specifically, linezolid inhibits the binding of N-formylmethionyl-tRNA to the 70S ribosome, halting the polypeptide chain initiation process.
    - Because of this unique binding site and mechanism of action, there is no cross-resistance with other classes of protein synthesis inhibitors.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:**
    - The 23S ribosomal RNA of the 50S ribosomal subunit (specifically at the P site near the decoding center).
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):**
    - Primarily bacteriostatic against staphylococci and enterococci.
    - Bactericidal against streptococci (including Streptococcus pneumoniae).
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    - Resistance in enterococci and staphylococci is mediated by point mutations of the 23S ribosomal RNA at the linezolid binding site.
    - Because bacteria possess multiple copies of 23S rRNA genes, clinically significant resistance generally requires mutations to occur in two or more copies of these genes.
    - Due to its unique site of action, cross-resistance with other classes of protein synthesis inhibitors is absent.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:**
    - Linezolid is 100% bioavailable after oral administration, with equivalent plasma concentrations achieved whether given orally or intravenously. Oral absorption is not significantly affected by food.
    - Tedizolid is administered as the highly bioavailable prodrug tedizolid phosphate (available in oral and intravenous formulations), which is rapidly converted to the active moiety tedizolid. Food does not significantly impact its absorption.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:**
    - Linezolid is widely distributed into tissues and body fluids. It achieves excellent penetration into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), with CSF concentrations reaching approximately 60% to 70% of concurrent serum levels.
    - Tedizolid is highly protein-bound (approximately 70% to 90%) compared to linezolid (which is only 31% bound). There are currently no clinical data on the CSF penetration of tedizolid.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    - Linezolid is metabolized by oxidative metabolism to yield two inactive metabolites. It does not undergo metabolism by hepatic Cytochrome P450 enzymes and is neither an inducer nor an inhibitor of Cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes, meaning it is free from CYP-mediated drug interactions.
    - Tedizolid undergoes conversion from its phosphate prodrug form to the active drug. Prolonged courses have not been fully studied for metabolic pathways or interactions, but it is thought to have a low risk of serotonergic toxicity.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    - Linezolid has a plasma elimination half-life of 4 to 6 hours.
    - Tedizolid has a significantly longer plasma half-life of 12 hours, allowing once-daily dosing.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - Renal Impairment: Linezolid is listed as requiring dosage adjustments in patients with renal impairment. Tedizolid does not require dose adjustment in mild to moderate renal impairment.
    - Hepating Impairment: Linezolid is not listed as requiring dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment. Tedizolid does not require dose adjustment in mild to moderate hepatic impairment.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    - Infections caused by vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium (including soft tissue infections, urinary tract infections, and bacteremia).
    - Nosocomial pneumonia and health care-associated pneumonia caused by methicillin-susceptible (MSSA) and methicillin-resistant (MRSA) strains of Staphylococcus aureus.
    - Community-acquired pneumonia caused by penicillin-susceptible strains of Streptococcus pneumoniae.
    - Complicated skin and skin-structure infections (SSTIs) caused by Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, and S. aureus (both methicillin-susceptible and -resistant strains).
    - Uncomplicated skin and skin-structure infections (dosed at 400 mg twice daily for linezolid).
    - Diabetic foot infections (600 mg twice daily, often given with or without aztreonam, vancomycin, or ampicillin/sulbactam).
    - Tedizolid is approved for the treatment of acute bacterial skin and skin-structure infections (ABSSSI) at a dose of 200 mg orally or intravenously once daily for a short 6-day course of therapy.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:**
    - Susceptible: Gram-positive cocci and bacilli, including Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA, MRSA, vancomycin-intermediate VISA, and vancomycin-resistant VRSA), coagulase-negative staphylococci, Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, Streptococcus pneumoniae (including penicillin-resistant strains), Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium (including vancomycin-resistant enterococci / VRE), Gram-positive anaerobic cocci, Corynebacterium species, Nocardia species, Listeria monocytogenes, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
    - Resistant: Poor activity against most Gram-negative aerobic or anaerobic bacteria. Most common Gram-negative organisms are intrinsically resistant.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    - Treatment of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) and extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis (XDR-TB) caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (using a lower dose of 600 mg once daily to limit marrow suppression and neurotoxicity; experts recommend supplemental pyridoxine / vitamin B6).
    - Treatment of Nocardia infections.
    - Salvage therapy for suspected or documented endocarditis caused by Gram-positive pathogens (although linezolid is bacteriostatic against staphylococci and enterococci and is not recommended as first-line therapy for endocarditis, clinical cures have been documented in salvage situations).

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:**
    - Diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, headache, rash, and dizziness.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    - Hematological Toxicity / Myelosuppression: Mild to severe bone marrow suppression, most commonly manifesting as thrombocytopenia (seen in approximately 3% of treatment courses), particularly when therapy exceeds 2 weeks (14 days). Anemia and neutropenia can also occur, especially in patients with preexisting bone marrow suppression or a predisposition to cytopenias.
    - Peripheral Neuropathy and Optic Neuropathy (Optic Neuritis): Can occur with prolonged courses of therapy (such as those required for tuberculosis). Optic neuropathy may lead to visual disturbances and visual loss, and peripheral neuropathy can be irreversible. These toxicities are related to the drug's ability to inhibit host mitochondrial protein synthesis.
    - Lactic Acidosis: A rare but life-threatening complication occurring with prolonged courses, also secondary to host mitochondrial protein synthesis inhibition.
    - Serotonin Syndrome: Can occur when linezolid is co-administered with serotonergic drugs (such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressants) because linezolid is a weak, non-selective inhibitor of monoamine oxidase (MAO).
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:**
    - Reversible thrombocytopenia and bone marrow suppression characteristically linked to therapy extending beyond 14 days.
    - Optic and irreversible peripheral neuropathy directly linked to the inhibition of mammalian mitochondrial protein synthesis during long-term therapy.
    - Serotonin syndrome precipitated by the drug's unexpected property as a non-selective inhibitor of monoamine oxidase (MAO) when combined with other serotonergic agents.
*   **Black Box Warnings:**
    - N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    - Documented history of immediate hypersensitivity or severe allergic reactions to linezolid, tedizolid, or any other oxazolidinone class drug.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - Preexisting bone marrow suppression, anemia, thrombocytopenia, or concurrent administration of other myelosuppressive drugs.
    - Avoid use as first-line therapy for suspected endocarditis due to bacteriostatic activity against staphylococci and enterococci.
    - Avoid or use with extreme caution in patients receiving concurrent serotonergic medications (e.g., SSRIs) unless closely monitored for serotonin syndrome.
    - Prolonged therapy exceeding 14 days (requires weekly monitoring of complete blood counts).
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - Serotonergic agents (including SSRI antidepressants): Co-administration can provoke life-threatening serotonin syndrome due to linezolid's non-selective inhibition of monoamine oxidase.
    - Myelosuppressive drugs: Concurrent use increases the risk and severity of bone marrow suppression and cytopenias.
    - Adrenergic or pressor agents: May potentiate pressor responses due to MAO inhibition.
    - Cytochrome P450 Enzymes: Linezolid is neither an inducer nor an inhibitor of Cytochrome P450 enzymes, meaning it does not participate in CYP-mediated drug interactions.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:**
    - No specific FDA pregnancy category or documented teratogenicity is detailed in the texts. Avoid during pregnancy unless the potential clinical benefits outweigh the theoretical risks.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "First truly new class of antibacterial agents to reach the marketplace in several decades" (Zurenko et al., 2001).
    - "Inhibition of host mitochondrial protein synthesis," explaining the unusual mechanism behind linezolid-induced neuropathies and lactic acidosis.
    - "Non-selective inhibitor of monoamine oxidase (MAO)," highlighting the pharmacodynamic basis for the unexpected serotonin syndrome drug interaction.
    - "Prevention of the 70S ribosome initiation complex," describing its distinct and unique mechanism of protein translation blockade.
    - "6-day course of therapy," describing the highly potent and abbreviated treatment regimen approved for Tedizolid.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - Although most bacterial protein synthesis inhibitors are strictly bacteriostatic (such as tetracyclines, macrolides, clindamycin, and chloramphenicol), oxazolidinones are bactericidal against streptococci (including S. pneumoniae).
    - While linezolid is bacteriostatic against staphylococci and enterococci and is generally contraindicated as first-line therapy for endovascular infections, it has been successfully used as an effective salvage therapy to cure cases of endocarditis.
    - Unlike macrolides, lincosamides, and chloramphenicol, which bind to the 50S ribosomal subunit and share overlapping binding sites with potential cross-resistance, linezolid binds to a unique site on the 23S rRNA, resulting in zero cross-resistance with other classes.
    - Linezolid is not metabolized by hepatic Cytochrome P450 enzymes and is neither an inducer nor an inhibitor of the CYP system, which is a significant exception to many other systemically administered antimicrobials that have high CYP-mediated drug interactions.
