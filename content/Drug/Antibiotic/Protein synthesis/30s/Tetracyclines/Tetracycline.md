### Tetracyclines
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:**
    - Tetracyclines are close chemical congeners of polycyclic naphthacenecarboxamide.
    - Subclasses are historically categorized by their source and pharmacokinetics:
      1. Short-acting natural products: Chlortetracycline (the class prototype, no longer marketed in the U.S.), Oxytetracycline (elaborated by Streptomyces rimosus), and Tetracycline (semisynthetic derivative of chlortetracycline).
      2. Intermediate-acting natural product: Demeclocycline (elaborated by a mutant strain of Streptomyces aureofaciens).
      3. Long-acting semisynthetic agents: Doxycycline and Minocycline.
      4. Glycylcyclines (newer subclass): Tigecycline, which is a semisynthetic 9-tert-butyl-glycylamido derivative of minocycline.
*   **Target Organisms:**
    - Highly active against a very broad range of pathogens, including:
      - Aerobic and anaerobic Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.
      - Atypical pathogens: Rickettsiae, Coxiella burnetii, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydiae (Chlamydia trachomatis, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Chlamydia psittaci), and Legionella pneumophila.
      - Spirochetes: Borrelia burgdorferi, Borrelia recurrentis, Treponema pallidum, Treponema pertenue, and Leptospira species.
      - Protozoa: Plasmodium falciparum.
      - Atypical mycobacteria (e.g., Mycobacterium marinum) and rapidly growing mycobacteria.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    - Entry into the bacterial cell: Tetracyclines enter Gram-negative bacteria via passive diffusion through outer membrane porin channels and are then actively transported across the inner cytoplasmic membrane by an energy-dependent active transport pump system. Entry into Gram-positive bacteria is also energy-dependent, but the exact process is less well understood.
    - Inhibition of translation: Once intracellular, tetracyclines bind reversibly to the 30S subunit of the bacterial ribosome. This binding physically blocks the aminoacyl-tRNA from accessing the acceptor (A) site on the mRNA-ribosome complex, preventing the addition of new amino acids to the growing peptide chain.
    - Tigecycline unique mechanism: The bulky 9-tert-butyl-glycylamido substituent on tigecycline provides steric hindrance that reduces its affinity for bacterial efflux pumps and prevents ribosomal protection proteins from displacing it from the ribosome.
    - Antiparasitic mechanism: Doxycycline inhibits protein translation in the Plasmodium apicoplast (plastid), resulting in a "delayed death" mechanism where the progeny of treated parasites fail to survive.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:**
    - The 30S subunit of the bacterial ribosome.
    - The apicoplast (parasite plastid) in Plasmodium species.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):**
    - Bacteriostatic against most susceptible organisms, meaning they inhibit growth and replication rather than directly killing.
    - Tigecycline exhibits bactericidal activity against certain streptococcal strains.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    - Three main mechanisms of acquired resistance:
      1. Impaired influx or increased active efflux of the drug:
         - Tet(AE) efflux pump in Gram-negative species: exports older tetracyclines, doxycycline, and minocycline, but is ineffective against tigecycline.
         - Tet(K) efflux pump in staphylococci: exports tetracycline but does not export doxycycline, minocycline, or tigecycline.
         - Chromosomally encoded multidrug efflux pumps in Proteus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa: export all tetracyclines, rendering these species intrinsically resistant.
      2. Ribosomal protection:
         - Production of ribosomal protection proteins (such as Tet(M)) that bind to the ribosome and displace tetracyclines. Tigecycline is resistant to this displacement due to the steric hindrance of its bulky glycylamido group.
      3. Enzymatic inactivation of the tetracycline molecule.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:**
    - Oral bioavailability varies substantially by agent:
      - Tetracycline and Demeclocycline: Modest oral absorption of 60% to 80% on an empty stomach.
      - Doxycycline: High oral absorption of 95%.
      - Minocycline: Complete oral absorption of 100%.
      - Tigecycline: Negligible oral absorption; must be administered intravenously.
    - Oral absorption occurs primarily in the stomach and upper small intestine and is enhanced in the fasting state.
    - Impairment of absorption: Absorption is markedly decreased by the concurrent ingestion of multivalent cations (calcium, magnesium, aluminum, iron, and zinc) found in dairy products, antacids, mineral supplements, and bismuth subsalicylate (e.g., Pepto-Bismol) due to chelation and the formation of non-absorbable insoluble complexes. While doxycycline and minocycline are less affected by food or milk, co-administration with antacids or iron supplements must still be avoided.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:**
    - Extremely wide distribution to organs, tissues, and intracellular compartments, resulting in a large volume of distribution.
    - Excellent intracellular penetration. Minocycline and doxycycline are highly lipophilic and achieve superior tissue and fluid levels.
    - Minocycline concentrates in the vestibular fluid of the ear and nasopharyngeal secretions.
    - Tetracyclines readily cross the placenta and accumulate in fetal bones and developing teeth.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    - N/A
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    - Classified by elimination half-life:
      - Short-acting (half-life of 6 to 8 hours): Tetracycline, oxytetracycline.
      - Intermediate-acting (half-life of 12 hours): Demeclocycline.
      - Long-acting (half-life of 16 to 18 hours): Doxycycline and minocycline.
      - Ultra-long-acting (half-life of 36 hours): Tigecycline.
    - Excretion pathways:
      - Tetracycline and minocycline are excreted primarily via the kidneys and can accumulate to toxic levels in patients with renal impairment.
      - Doxycycline undergoes nonrenal elimination (excreted in feces) and does not accumulate in renal failure.
      - Tigecycline is excreted primarily via biliary elimination into the feces, with a smaller portion excreted in the urine.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - Renal Impairment: Tetracycline and minocycline must be avoided or dosed with extreme caution in patients with renal insufficiency. Doxycycline and tigecycline require no dosage adjustment in renal failure.
    - Hepatic Impairment: Tigecycline requires dosage reduction in severe hepatic impairment (the maintenance dose should be reduced to 25 mg every 12 hours following a normal 100 mg loading dose).

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    - Rickettsial infections: Suspected or proven Rocky Mountain spotted fever, typhus (epidemic, murine, scrub), rickettsialpox, and Q fever.
    - Chlamydial infections: Sexually transmitted infections (cervicitis, urethritis), lymphogranuloma venereum, psittacosis, and Chlamydia pneumoniae respiratory infections.
    - Mycoplasma pneumoniae infections (atypical pneumonia).
    - Spirochetal infections: Lyme disease (Borrelia burgdorferi), relapsing fever (Borrelia recurrentis), leptospirosis, and yaws.
    - Syphilis: Alternative therapy in nonpregnant penicillin-allergic patients (specifically doxycycline).
    - Brucellosis (in combination with rifampin or streptomycin).
    - Plague (Yersinia pestis) and Tularemia (Francisella tularensis) - in combination.
    - Cholera (Vibrio cholerae).
    - Acne vulgaris (low-dose oral therapy to inhibit propionibacteria in sebaceous follicles).
    - Prevention or treatment of anthrax (Bacillus anthracis), including post-exposure prophylaxis for bioterrorism.
    - Gastric and duodenal ulcers caused by Helicobacter pylori (part of combination regimens).
    - Tigecycline indications: Complicated skin and skin-structure infections, complicated intra-abdominal infections, and community-acquired bacterial pneumonia.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:**
    - Gram-positive bacteria: Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA, MSSA, and coagulase-negative staphylococci), Streptococcus pneumoniae, Streptococcus pyogenes, Bacillus anthracis, Listeria monocytogenes, and Actinomyces.
    - Gram-negative bacteria: Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Brucella, Yersinia pestis, Francisella tularensis, Vibrio cholerae.
    - Atypical / Intracellular: Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia species, Rickettsiae, Coxiella burnetii, Legionella pneumophila.
    - Parasites: Plasmodium falciparum (blood stages).
    - Mycobacteria: Mycobacterium marinum, rapidly growing mycobacteria (e.g., covered by tigecycline).
    - Resistant isolates: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Proteus, and Providencia species are intrinsically resistant.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    - Demeclocycline: Used off-label in the treatment of the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH) secretion due to its ability to produce nephrogenic diabetes insipidus by inhibiting antidiuretic hormone in the renal tubule.
    - Minocycline: Used to eradicate the meningococcal carrier state, and minocycline sustained-release microspheres are used subgingivally in dentistry.
    - Doxycycline: Used in filariasis (Wuchereria bancrofti) and onchocerciasis (Onchocerca volvulus) to target and kill Wolbachia, an essential bacterial endosymbiont of the filarial worms, resulting in adult worm sterility.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:**
    - Gastrointestinal irritation: Nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea (most common reason for discontinuation).
    - Esophageal ulceration: Can occur if capsules/tablets dissolve in the esophagus (prevented by taking with a full glass of water and remaining upright for 30 minutes).
    - Photosensitivity: Induced sensitivity to sunlight or UV light, resulting in mild-to-severe skin rashes or burning, especially in fair-skinned individuals.
    - Local irritation: Venous thrombosis and phlebitis (specifically with IV tetracycline due to low pH).
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    - Hepatotoxicity: Can cause hepatic necrosis and liver failure, especially when given in high doses intravenously or to pregnant women.
    - Bone and teeth deposition: Chelate with calcium in developing bones and teeth, causing permanent yellow-brown discoloration of teeth, enamel hypoplasia, and temporary depression of bone growth.
    - Nephrotoxicity: Outdated (expired) tetracycline preparations can cause renal tubular acidosis and Fanconi syndrome. Co-administration with diuretics can also potentiate renal injury.
    - Vestibular toxicity: Vertigo, dizziness, and tinnitus (highly associated with minocycline and occasionally high-dose doxycycline).
    - Benign intracranial hypertension (pseudotumor cerebri).
    - Tigecycline increased mortality: Tigecycline is associated with an increased risk of death compared to other agents, particularly in hospital-acquired or ventilator-associated pneumonia.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:**
    - Permanent staining and discoloration of teeth and enamel hypoplasia when administered during tooth development (gestation, infancy, or childhood under 8 years).
    - Expired/outdated tetracycline-induced Fanconi syndrome (renal tubular acidosis).
    - Vestibular vertigo and dizziness highly specific to minocycline.
    - Photosensitivity-induced onycholysis and nail pigmentation.
*   **Black Box Warnings:**
    - Tigecycline: Issued a black box warning by the FDA due to an observed small but significant increase in the risk of mortality across clinical trials compared to comparator drugs. Tigecycline should be reserved for situations where alternative treatments are not suitable.

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    - Documented hypersensitivity to any tetracycline class drug.
    - Pregnancy (especially during the second and third trimesters) and lactation.
    - Children under 8 years of age (with the sole exception of doxycycline for life-threatening Rocky Mountain spotted fever).
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - Preexisting hepatic or renal impairment.
    - Concurrent administration of diuretics.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - Multivalent cations (calcium, magnesium, aluminum, iron, and zinc), dairy products, antacids, and bismuth subsalicylate (e.g., Pepto-Bismol) significantly impair oral absorption due to chelation.
    - Diuretics: Potentiate nephrotoxicity.
    - Anticonvulsants (barbiturates, carbamazepine, phenytoin): Induce hepatic drug metabolism and significantly reduce serum concentrations of doxycycline.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:**
    - Highly contraindicated during pregnancy. They cross the placenta and deposit in the fetal skeleton and deciduous teeth, leading to permanent tooth staining, enamel hypoplasia, and skeletal growth inhibition.
    - Pregnant women are also uniquely susceptible to severe, potentially fatal tetracycline-induced hepatotoxicity and hepatic necrosis.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "30S ribosomal subunit binding"
    - "Chelation with divalent and trivalent cations"
    - "Tooth discoloration and enamel hypoplasia"
    - "Outdated tetracycline and Fanconi syndrome"
    - "Steric hindrance of Tet(M)"
    - "Delayed death mechanism in Plasmodium"
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - Doxycycline is excreted nonrenally (primarily in the feces) and does not require dosage adjustments in renal impairment, unlike other class members (tetracycline, minocycline) which accumulate.
    - While the class is contraindicated in children <8 years of age, Doxycycline is the absolute first-line drug of choice for suspected or proven Rocky Mountain spotted fever in all ages, including young children, because the risk of staining is far outweighed by the severity of the potentially fatal infection.
    - Gram-negative bacilli such as Proteus, Providencia, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa are intrinsically resistant to all tetracyclines (including the advanced glycylcycline tigecycline) due to chromosomally encoded multidrug efflux pumps.
    - Demeclocycline is an antibiotic but is used clinically almost exclusively off-label for its side-effect profile to treat the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH) secretion.
