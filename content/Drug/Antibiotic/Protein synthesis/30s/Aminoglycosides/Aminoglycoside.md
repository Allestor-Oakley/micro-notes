### Aminoglycoside
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:**
    - Aminoglycosides (aminoglycosidic aminocyclitols). The chemical structure consists of two or more amino sugars joined in glycosidic linkage to a central hexose nucleus (aminocyclitol ring).
    - The aminocyclitol ring is either streptidine (found in streptomycin) or 2-deoxystreptamine (found in all other clinically available aminoglycosides).
    - Subclasses and individual agents:
      1. Streptidine-containing agents: Streptomycin (natural product derived from Streptomyces griseus).
      2. 2-Deoxystreptamine-containing agents:
         - Gentamicin complex (gentamicins C1, C1a, and C2), derived from Micromonospora purpurea.
         - Tobramycin, derived from Streptomyces tenebrarius.
         - Kanamycin, derived from Streptomyces.
         - Neomycin, derived from Streptomyces.
         - Paromomycin, derived from Streptomyces.
         - Sisomicin, derived from Micromonospora.
         - Amikacin, a semisynthetic derivative of kanamycin A, prepared by acylation of the 1-amino group of the 2-deoxystreptamine moiety with 2-hydroxy-4-aminobutyric acid.
         - Netilmicin, a semisynthetic 1-N-ethyl derivative of sisomicin.
         - Plazomicin, a semisynthetic molecule derived from sisomicin. It contains a hydroxyl-aminobutyric acid R-group at the 1-amino position (similar to amikacin) and has an unsaturated ring I.
*   **Target Organisms:**
    - Aerobic Gram-negative bacteria, certain Gram-positive bacteria (typically in combination with cell-wall active agents), certain mycobacteria (including Mycobacterium tuberculosis and atypical/nontuberculous mycobacteria), and certain protozoa (specifically Entamoeba histolytica).
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    - Intracellular entry of aminoglycosides is an active, multi-step process:
      1. Passive diffusion across the outer membrane through bacterial porin channels.
      2. Energy-Dependent Phase I (EDP1): Active transport across the cytoplasmic (inner) membrane. This transport is driven by the membrane potential (electrical gradient) and is dependent on electron transport and aerobic respiration (utilizing a polyamine carrier system). It is blocked by low pH, divalent cations, and anaerobic conditions.
      3. Binding to the ribosome and subsequent stimulation of Energy-Dependent Phase II (EDP2).
    - Once inside the cell, aminoglycosides bind reversibly to the 30S subunit of the bacterial ribosome. This interferes with protein synthesis in at least three ways:
      1. Blockade of translation initiation: Binding fixes the 30S-50S ribosomal complex at the start codon (AUG) of mRNA. As downstream ribosomes complete translation and detach, abnormal initiation complexes (termed streptomycin monosomes) accumulate, blocking further translation of the message.
      2. Misreading of mRNA: Aminoglycoside binding causes misreading of the genetic code, leading to the incorporation of incorrect amino acids into the growing peptide chain. This produces abnormal, nonfunctional, or toxic proteins.
      3. Blockade of translocation: The movement of the ribosome along the mRNA chain is arrested, or translation is terminated prematurely with the detachment of the ribosomal complex and release of incomplete peptides.
    - EDP2 is triggered when these aberrant or mistranslated proteins are inserted into the cytoplasmic membrane, resulting in altered membrane permeability and progressive disruption of the cell envelope (leakage of small ions, then larger molecules, and eventually proteins). This progressive disruption stimulates further aminoglycoside transport into the cell, resulting in rapid cell death.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:**
    - The A-site (decoding site) of the 16S ribosomal RNA of the 30S ribosomal subunit, as well as associated 30S ribosomal proteins.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):**
    - Rapidly bactericidal (unlike most other ribosomal protein synthesis inhibitors, which are bacteriostatic). They exhibit concentration-dependent killing, where higher concentrations increase the rate and extent of bacterial death.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    - There are three major mechanisms of acquired resistance:
      1. Enzymatic inactivation of the drug (the most common and clinically important mechanism): Plasmid- or transposon-encoded bacterial transferase enzymes modify and inactivate aminoglycosides through:
         - Acetylation (by acetyltransferases / AAC): targets amino groups.
         - Adenylylation (by adenylyltransferases or adenylases / ANT): targets hydroxyl groups.
         - Phosphorylation (by phosphotransferases or phosphorylases / APH): targets hydroxyl groups.
         - Modified aminoglycosides have a greatly reduced binding affinity for the ribosome. Amikacin and plazomicin are structurally designed to resist modification by many of these enzymes (amikacin is protected from modification at positions 2, 3, 4, and 5; plazomicin is protected at positions 1, 2, 4, and 5). Netilmicin is sterically protected by an ethyl group at the 1-amino position.
      2. Impaired transport of the drug into the cell:
         - Mutations affecting porin channels or transport systems (such as respiratory chain mutants with decreased membrane potential).
         - Intrinsic resistance in anaerobic bacteria due to the absence of the oxygen-dependent active transport system required for drug penetration.
      3. Mutation of the ribosomal target (ribosomal resistance):
         - Point mutations in chromosomal genes encoding ribosomal proteins (such as the rpsL gene encoding the S12 ribosomal protein) or 16S ribosomal RNA (such as the rrs gene) alter the ribosomal binding site and prevent drug binding. This is particularly common and develops rapidly with streptomycin when used as monotherapy.
         - Loss of a conserved 7-methylguanosine modification in 16S rRNA can also confer low-level streptomycin resistance.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:**
    - Highly polar polycations with extremely poor oral bioavailability. Gastrointestinal absorption after oral administration is negligible (less than 1%), and oral doses are excreted unchanged in the feces.
    - For systemic infections, they must be administered parenterally (intramuscularly or intravenously). Rapidly and completely absorbed from intramuscular injection sites, with peak concentrations reached in 30 to 90 minutes.
    - Can also be administered via inhalation (aerosolized tobramycin achieves high local concentrations in bronchial secretions with minimal systemic absorption, typically 4% to 17%) or applied topically (neomycin, gentamicin, tobramycin).
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:**
    - Because of their high polarity and hydrophilicity, they do not cross cell membranes readily. They are excluded from most cells, the central nervous system, and the eye.
    - Plasma protein binding is very low (typically less than 10%, except for streptomycin which is about 30% bound).
    - Volume of distribution is small, approximately equal to the extracellular fluid volume (0.2 to 0.3 L/kg), but can be significantly larger in neonates, pregnant women, or patients with extensive burns, sepsis, or ascites.
    - Penetration into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is extremely poor, even in the presence of active meningeal inflammation (achieving only 2% to 3% of concurrent serum levels).
    - Penetration into ocular tissues and bronchial/alveolar secretions is also low. However, high concentrations can be achieved in the renal cortex and in the endolymph/perilymph of the inner ear, where they accumulate and contribute to toxicity. High concentrations are also achieved in joint and pleural fluids.
    - They cross the placenta and can accumulate in fetal tissue.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    - Not metabolized by mammalian hepatic enzymes. They do not undergo hepatic metabolism and do not interact with or inhibit/induce the cytochrome P450 enzyme system.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    - Excreted almost entirely unchanged by the kidneys via glomerular filtration. Renal clearance is directly proportional to the glomerular filtration rate (GFR).
    - Elimination half-life is short, typically 2 to 3 hours in adults with normal renal function.
    - Elimination half-life is significantly prolonged in patients with renal impairment (reaching 100 hours or more in functionally anephric patients) and is also longer in neonates and the elderly.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - Strict dosage adjustments are mandatory in patients with renal insufficiency. This is achieved by either reducing the dose or extending the dosing interval.
    - Monitoring of serum peak and trough concentrations is essential to guide proper dosing and prevent toxic drug accumulation, especially during prolonged therapy, in the elderly, or in patients with unstable renal function.
    - They are cleared by hemodialysis (a significant amount of vancomycin and aminoglycosides is removed during a standard hemodialysis run using high-flux membranes; e.g., 50% or more of amikacin or gentamicin is dialyzable).

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    - Sepsis and serious bacteremia caused by aerobic Gram-negative bacilli (including Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter).
    - Empiric combination therapy (usually with an antipseudomonal beta-lactam) for serious, life-threatening nosocomial infections, neutropenic fever, or severe hospital-acquired pneumonia.
    - Bacterial endocarditis: Gentamicin or streptomycin is combined with penicillin, ampicillin, or vancomycin to achieve synergistic bactericidal activity against Enterococcus species, viridans group streptococci, or Staphylococcus aureus.
    - Plague (caused by Yersinia pestis) and Tularemia (caused by Francisella tularensis): Streptomycin or gentamicin is considered a first-line therapy of choice.
    - Tuberculosis: Streptomycin is used as a second-line agent in combination regimens for drug-resistant tuberculosis. Amikacin and kanamycin (where available) are also used for multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB).
    - Nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infections: Amikacin or streptomycin is used as part of combination regimens for infections caused by Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), Mycobacterium kansasii, Mycobacterium scrofulaceum, Mycobacterium chelonae, and Mycobacterium fortuitum.
    - Intestinal amebiasis: Oral paromomycin is used as an effective luminal amebicide to eradicate intestinal colonization with Entamoeba histolytica.
    - Hepatic encephalopathy / hepatic coma: Oral paromomycin or oral neomycin is used to suppress ammonia-producing intestinal microflora.
    - Surgical prophylaxis: Oral neomycin or oral kanamycin (often combined with erythromycin) is used for preoperative bowel preparation prior to elective colorectal surgery.
    - Inhaled therapy: Aerosolized tobramycin is used to manage and treat chronic Pseudomonas aeruginosa lung infections in patients with cystic fibrosis.
    - Topical therapy: Neomycin, polymyxin B, and bacitracin combinations are used for superficial skin infections. Gentamicin and tobramycin are used as ophthalmic ointments or drops for bacterial conjunctivitis.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:**
    - Susceptible pathogens: Most aerobic Gram-negative bacilli, including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, Proteus vulgaris, Enterobacter species, Citrobacter freundii, Serratia marcescens, Providencia stuartii, Morganella morganii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter species, Yersinia pestis, Francisella tularensis, Brucella species, and Haemophilus influenzae. Also active against certain Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus, including MRSA, and coagulase-negative staphylococci) and mycobacteria (Mycobacterium tuberculosis, MAC, M. kansasii, M. fortuitum, M. chelonae). Oral paromomycin is active against Entamoeba histolytica.
    - Resistant pathogens: Anaerobic bacteria are completely and intrinsically resistant due to the absence of the oxygen-dependent transport system. Streptococcus pneumoniae and Enterococcus species exhibit low-level intrinsic resistance to aminoglycosides when used as monotherapy (enterococci can bypass the effect of aminoglycosides unless combined with a cell-wall active agent). Pseudomonas aeruginosa and staphylococci can develop high-level resistance during therapy.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    - Amikacin is used off-label for treating severe Nocardia infections.
    - Plazomicin is used as a niche agent for treating serious urinary tract infections and systemic infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE).
    - Tobramycin is used off-label as an intravitreal injection for treating bacterial endophthalmitis.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:**
    - Local pain, irritation, or sterile abscesses at the intramuscular injection site.
    - Unpleasant taste and mild gastrointestinal upset with oral formulations (paromomycin, neomycin).
    - Reversible mild elevations in serum transaminases (aminotransferases).
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    - Nephrotoxicity: Reversible, dose-dependent damage to the proximal renal tubular cells. It manifests as rising serum creatinine levels, proteinuria, cylindruria, or a reduction in GFR, and is often heralded by an increase in trough serum aminoglycoside concentrations. Neomycin, tobramycin, and gentamicin are the most nephrotoxic.
    - Ototoxicity: Irreversible, cumulative damage to the eighth cranial nerve, which can involve both vestibular and cochlear functions.
      - Cochlear damage: Auditory damage manifesting initially as high-frequency hearing loss (outside the conversational range), tinnitus, and eventually permanent deafness. Neomycin, kanamycin, and amikacin are the most cochleotoxic.
      - Vestibular damage: Vestibulotoxicity manifesting as vertigo, ataxia, loss of balance, nystagmus, and equilibrium disturbances. Streptomycin and gentamicin are the most vestibulotoxic.
    - Neuromuscular Blockade: An acute, potentially fatal neuromuscular blockade and apnea due to inhibition of acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction (competing with calcium). This occurs most commonly after intrapleural or intraperitoneal instillation of large doses of an aminoglycoside but can also occur after intravenous infusion. The order of potency for inducing neuromuscular blockade is neomycin > kanamycin > amikacin > gentamicin > tobramycin.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:**
    - Aminoglycoside-induced ototoxicity is strongly associated with mitochondrial genetic susceptibility, specifically a maternal inheritance pattern linked to mutations in the mitochondrial 12S ribosomal RNA gene (e.g., the A1555G mutation), which makes individuals highly susceptible to rapid, permanent deafness even after a single dose.
    - High-dose, once-daily (extended-interval) dosing regimens exploit the concentration-dependent killing and post-antibiotic effect of aminoglycosides to achieve high efficacy while minimizing toxicity (the transiently high peak concentration does not increase toxicity, while the low trough concentration allows the renal cortex and inner ear cells to clear the drug, reducing accumulation).

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    - A documented history of immediate hypersensitivity, severe allergic reactions, or serious prior toxicities (such as profound deafness or severe renal failure) associated with any aminoglycoside.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - Myasthenia gravis (relative contraindication; aminoglycosides can precipitate or worsen neuromuscular weakness due to their calcium-antagonizing neuromuscular blocking activity).
    - Preexisting vestibular or auditory impairment.
    - Preexisting renal dysfunction or dehydration.
    - Concomitant use of other ototoxic or nephrotoxic medications.
    - Advanced age (risk of subclinical renal insufficiency).
    - Pregnancy and lactation.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - Concomitant use with loop diuretics (such as furosemide or ethacrynic acid) significantly potentiates both ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity and should be strictly avoided.
    - Concurrent administration of other nephrotoxic agents (such as vancomycin, amphotericin B, cisplatin, cyclosporine, or colistin) increases the risk of renal injury.
    - General anesthetics or neuromuscular blocking agents (e.g., succinylcholine, tubocurarine): Concomitant use increases the risk of profound, prolonged neuromuscular blockade and respiratory depression. (This blockade can be reversed by the administration of calcium salts or neostigmine).
    - Beta-lactam antibiotics: In vitro, high concentrations of certain penicillins (e.g., carbenicillin, piperacillin) can physically inactivate aminoglycosides when mixed in the same solution; they must never be administered through the same intravenous line.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:**
    - Aminoglycosides cross the placenta and can accumulate in the fetal kidney and inner ear. Systemic administration during pregnancy carries a well-documented risk of causing congenital, irreversible bilateral deafness in the fetus (fetal ototoxicity).
    - They should be avoided during pregnancy unless there are no alternative options for life-threatening maternal infections.
    - No specific FDA pregnancy category is explicitly assigned to the entire class in the texts, but individual agents such as tobramycin and amikacin are known to carry high risk of fetal harm.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "Concentration-dependent killing," highlighting that the rate and extent of bacterial eradication increase with higher drug concentrations.
    - "Post-antibiotic effect (PAE)," which describes the persistent inhibition of bacterial growth that continues after drug concentrations fall below the MIC.
    - "Oxygen-dependent active transport," explaining why these drugs lack any activity against anaerobic bacteria.
    - "Energy-dependent phase I (EDP1)" and "Energy-dependent phase II (EDP2)" of active intracellular transport.
    - "Streptomycin monosomes," representing the nonfunctional single-ribosome mRNA complexes that accumulate upon inhibition of translation initiation.
    - "Extended-interval dosing" (once-daily dosing), which maximizes concentration-dependent kill and minimizes accumulation-driven toxicity.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - While most protein synthesis inhibitors are bacteriostatic (e.g., tetracyclines, macrolides, chloramphenicol), aminoglycosides are uniquely and rapidly bactericidal.
    - Streptococcus pneumoniae and Enterococcus species are Gram-positive cocci that have intrinsic low-level resistance to aminoglycosides because the cell wall prevents adequate drug penetration; however, this resistance is completely overcome (synergized) when an aminoglycoside is combined with a cell-wall active agent (like penicillin or vancomycin), which facilitates drug entry.
    - Anaerobic bacteria are completely and intrinsically resistant to aminoglycosides because they do not possess the oxygen-dependent active transport system required to transport the drug across the cytoplasmic membrane.
    - Although aminoglycosides do not cross the blood-brain barrier and generally fail to penetrate the central nervous system, they achieve excellent therapeutic concentrations in inflamed meninges and CSF of neonates (or can be administered intrathecally in adults as a last resort).
