### Fluoroquinolone
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:** Synthetic broad-spectrum fluorinated analogs of nalidixic acid, classified as quinolones and fluoroquinolones. Quinolones are structurally defined by a carboxylic acid moiety at position 3 of the primary ring structure. Many of the newer fluoroquinolones also contain a fluorine substituent at position 6 and a piperazine moiety at position 7. The class is divided into:
    - Earlier narrow-spectrum quinolones: Nalidixic acid (isolated as a byproduct of chloroquine synthesis) and cinoxacin.
    - Second group / earlier fluoroquinolones: Norfloxacin (the least active of the fluoroquinolones, approved in the US only for UTIs), ciprofloxacin, enoxacin, lomefloxacin, levofloxacin (the L-isomer of ofloxacin), ofloxacin, and pefloxacin. In the US, ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin are the primary systemically used agents from this group.
    - Newer/respiratory fluoroquinolones: Levofloxacin, gemifloxacin, and moxifloxacin (characterized by enhanced Gram-positive activity and activity against atypical pathogens).
    - Ophthalmic-only agents: Gatifloxacin (withdrawn from systemic sale in the US due to dysglycemia).
*   **Target Organisms:** Aerobic Gram-negative bacilli and cocci, Gram-positive cocci (staphylococci, streptococci, pneumococci), anaerobic bacteria, atypical pneumonia pathogens (Chlamydia, Mycoplasma), and intracellular pathogens (Legionella, Brucella, Mycobacterium).
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:** Quinolones inhibit bacterial DNA synthesis by targeting and blocking two essential bacterial enzymes: topoisomerase II (DNA gyrase) and topoisomerase IV. To allow DNA replication or transcription, individual strands of double-helical DNA must separate, which mechanical process results in excessive positive supercoiling or overwinding of the DNA ahead of the replication fork. DNA gyrase is responsible for continuously introducing negative supercoils into DNA to resolve this mechanical obstacle; inhibiting DNA gyrase prevents relaxation of positively supercoiled DNA, halting transcription and replication. Topoisomerase IV is responsible for separating replicated sister chromatids or chromosomal DNA into respective daughter cells during cell division; inhibiting topoisomerase IV blocks cell division.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:** Bacterial DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) and topoisomerase IV. The primary target varies: in many Gram-negative microbes (such as Escherichia coli), DNA gyrase is the primary target. In contrast, in many Gram-positive bacteria (such as Staphylococcus aureus), topoisomerase IV is the primary activity inhibited.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):** Bactericidal. They exhibit concentration-dependent killing and a significant in vitro postantibiotic effect (PAE) lasting 1.5 hours or more.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:** Quinolone resistance can develop during therapy. Mechanisms include:
    - Target site mutations: Mutations in the bacterial chromosomal genes encoding DNA gyrase or topoisomerase IV. Adding C8 halogen or C8 methoxy groups (as in moxifloxacin) markedly reduces the propensity for developing resistance.
    - Active efflux: Active transport of the drug out of the cell via efflux pumps. Quinolone efflux pumps play a central role in the emergence of fluoroquinolone resistance in Streptococcus pneumoniae. Active efflux also mediates intrinsic resistance in other pathogens.
    - Intrinsic resistance: Chromosomally encoded multidrug efflux pumps of Proteus species and Pseudomonas aeruginosa account for their intrinsic resistance to all tetracyclines and some other classes, and can also impact quinolones.
    - No quinolone-modifying or -inactivating activities have been identified in bacteria.
    - Resistance is increasingly observed in Campylobacter jejuni, Salmonella, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and Streptococcus pneumoniae. Methicillin-resistant staphylococci (MRSA, MRSE) are often resistant. The relationship between quinolone exposures and resistance amplification is characterized by an inverted U-shaped curve.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:** Well-absorbed after oral administration. Bioavailability values are:
    - Levofloxacin: 95%
    - Ofloxacin: 95%
    - Moxifloxacin: >85%
    - Norfloxacin: 80%
    - Ciprofloxacin: 70%
    - Gemifloxacin: 70%
    - Divalent and trivalent cations (such as calcium in calcium supplements, magnesium, or aluminum in antacids) significantly impair gastrointestinal absorption of oral fluoroquinolones by chelation.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:** Widely and extensively distributed into tissues and body fluids. Levofloxacin achieves high concentrations in skin tissue and intrapulmonary tissues (such as epithelial lining fluid). Ofloxacin and pefloxacin are active in animal models of leprosy.
    - Peak serum concentrations (after standard oral doses):
      - Levofloxacin (500 mg): 5.7 mcg/mL
      - Moxifloxacin (400 mg): 3.1 mcg/mL
      - Ofloxacin (400 mg): 2.9 mcg/mL
      - Ciprofloxacin (500 mg): 2.4 mcg/mL
      - Gemifloxacin (320 mg): 1.6 mcg/mL
      - Norfloxacin (400 mg): 1.5 mcg/mL
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    - Moxifloxacin is primarily metabolized and cleared by nonrenal (hepatic) pathways.
    - Ofloxacin, levofloxacin, norfloxacin, and ciprofloxacin undergo minimal hepatic metabolism and are excreted largely unchanged.
    - Cytochrome P450 dynamics are N/A.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    - Excretion is primarily renal (via urine) for ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, norfloxacin, ofloxacin, and gemifloxacin (which has mixed renal and nonrenal excretion). Moxifloxacin is excreted primarily by nonrenal/biliary routes.
    - Terminal elimination half-lives:
      - Moxifloxacin: 9-10 hours
      - Gemifloxacin: 8 hours
      - Levofloxacin: 5-7 hours
      - Ofloxacin: 5-7 hours
      - Norfloxacin: 3.5-5 hours
      - Ciprofloxacin: 3-5 hours (or approximately 4 hours)
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - Renal impairment: Dosage must be reduced in patients with severely impaired renal function or renal insufficiency for agents primarily excreted by the kidneys (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, gemifloxacin, norfloxacin, ofloxacin).
    - Moxifloxacin: No dosage adjustment is required in renal failure because clearance is predominantly nonrenal.
    - Hepatic impairment: N/A.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    - Urinary Tract Infections: Fluoroquinolones (except moxifloxacin) are highly effective and are more efficacious than trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for uncomplicated and complicated UTIs, including those caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Norfloxacin and ciprofloxacin XR are FDA-approved only for UTIs.
    - Bacterial Diarrhea / Gastroenteritis: Effective for diarrhea caused by Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter, and toxigenic E. coli.
    - Bone, Joint, and Soft Tissue Infections: Used for chronic osteomyelitis caused by Staphylococcus aureus and Gram-negative rods (requires 4-6 weeks or more; recommended doses are 500 mg q12h or 750 mg bid).
    - Diabetic Foot Infections: Used in combination with an agent with antianaerobic activity to cover mixed Gram-negative rods, anaerobes, streptococci, and staphylococci.
    - Lower Respiratory Tract Infections: Levofloxacin, gemifloxacin, and moxifloxacin (respiratory fluoroquinolones) are effective for treating community-acquired pneumonia and acute-exacerbations of chronic bronchitis.
    - Anthrax Prophylaxis and Treatment: Ciprofloxacin is a drug of choice; levofloxacin is also approved for prophylaxis.
    - Tuberculosis: Ofloxacin and ciprofloxacin have been used as second-line anti-TB agents. Moxifloxacin is being studied as a first-line agent to replace isoniazid or ethambutol to shorten therapy duration.
    - Other Infections: Ofloxacin has been used for cervicitis; norfloxacin is used for bacterial prostatitis.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:**
    - Gram-Negative Aerobic Pathogens: Highly active against Escherichia coli, Salmonella, Shigella, Enterobacter, Campylobacter jejuni, Neisseria meningitidis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and Haemophilus species (MIC90 usually <0.2 mcg/mL). Ciprofloxacin is highly active against Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MIC90 ranges from 0.5-6 mcg/mL); levofloxacin also has good activity against P. aeruginosa.
    - Gram-Positive Pathogens: Good activity against staphylococci (methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) are generally susceptible, but MRSA and MRSE are often resistant). Enhanced activity against streptococci (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Streptococcus pyogenes) is limited to levofloxacin, gemifloxacin, and moxifloxacin.
    - Anaerobic Pathogens: Gemifloxacin and moxifloxacin have activity against anaerobic bacteria (such as Bacteroides species), but moxifloxacin lacks appreciable activity against P. aeruginosa.
    - Atypical and Intracellular Pathogens: Active against Chlamydia (e.g., C. trachomatis), Mycoplasma, Legionella (e.g., L. pneumophila), Brucella, and some mycobacteria (including M. tuberculosis, M. avium complex, M. fortuitum, and M. kansasii). Moxifloxacin is the most active against M. tuberculosis in vitro.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    - Eradication of Salmonella typhi in chronic carriers.
    - Treatment of invasive external otitis ("malignant otitis") caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
    - Moxifloxacin used off-label as a first-line tuberculosis agent to replace isoniazid or ethambutol in short-course clinical regimens.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:** Generally well tolerated. Most common effects are nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. Occasionally, headache, dizziness, insomnia, skin rash, or abnormal liver function tests develop.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    - Tendinitis and Tendon Rupture: Can be a serious and debilitating complication. Risk factors include advanced age, renal insufficiency, and concurrent corticosteroid use.
    - Peripheral Neuropathy: Associated with oral or intravenous fluoroquinolones; can occur at any time during treatment and may persist for months to years, and can be permanent.
    - QTc Interval Prolongation: Sparfloxacin, gatifloxacin, moxifloxacin, and to a lesser extent levofloxacin and gemifloxacin can prolong the QTc interval, risking severe cardiac arrhythmias.
    - Dysglycemia: Gatifloxacin was associated with hyperglycemia in diabetic patients and severe hypoglycemia in patients taking oral hypoglycemics (which led to several fatalities).
    - Hepatotoxicity: Trovafloxacin was associated with severe, fatal hepatotoxicity.
    - Phototoxicity: Observed with sparfloxacin, lomefloxacin, pefloxacin, and clinafloxacin.
    - Cardiotoxicity: Grepafloxacin was withdrawn due to cardiotoxicity.
    - Immune Hemolytic Anemia: Temafloxacin caused immune hemolytic anemia.
    - Cartilage Damage and Arthropathy: May damage growing cartilage in animal models.
    - Leukopenia, eosinophilia, and mild transaminase elevations (rare).
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:**
    - Tendinitis and risk of tendon rupture.
    - Phototoxicity (lomefloxacin, sparfloxacin, clinafloxacin).
    - Arthropathy due to cartilage damage in pediatric animal models.
    - GABA-A receptor antagonism by 4-quinolones (receptors are antagonized, potentially provoking CNS overexcitability or seizures).
*   **Black Box Warnings:**
    - Gatifloxacin (withdrawn), trovafloxacin (withdrawn).
    - The FDA called for updated warnings for all systemically administered fluoroquinolones in 2016, stating that these agents should be reserved for patients who do not have alternative options, particularly in less severe infections such as acute sinusitis, acute bronchitis, or uncomplicated cystitis, due to the risk of disabling and potentially permanent side effects (such as tendinitis, tendon rupture, peripheral neuropathy, and CNS effects) occurring together.

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    - Prior history of immediate hypersensitivity, anaphylaxis, or severe cutaneous reactions to any quinolone or fluoroquinolone.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - Pregnancy: Avoid during pregnancy in the absence of specific data documenting safety due to the risk of fetal cartilage damage.
    - Pediatrics: Not recommended as first-line agents in children under 18 years of age due to potential cartilage damage and arthropathy (though they may be used if needed, such as for pseudomonal infections in patients with cystic fibrosis).
    - Patients with pre-existing QTc interval prolongation, uncorrected hypokalemia, or those receiving class IA or class 3 antiarrhythmics.
    - Advanced age, renal insufficiency, or concurrent corticosteroid use (due to increased risk of tendinitis and tendon rupture).
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - Divalent and Trivalent Cations: Calcium supplements, antacids (magnesium/aluminum), iron supplements, or zinc significantly impair oral absorption. Fluoroquinolones must be administered 2 hours before or 4 hours after these cations.
    - Class IA and Class 3 Antiarrhythmics: Concurrent use with quinidine, procainamide, amiodarone, sotalol, or ibutilide increases the risk of QTc prolongation and torsades de pointes.
    - Other QTc-Prolonging Agents: Co-administration with erythromycin, tricyclic antidepressants, or other QTc-prolonging drugs must be avoided or managed with caution.
    - Corticosteroids: Concurrent use significantly increases the risk of tendinitis and tendon rupture.
    - Oral Hypoglycemic Agents: Gatifloxacin (withdrawn) interact with oral hypoglycemics to cause severe, potentially fatal hypoglycemia.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** Fluoroquinolones should be avoided during pregnancy in the absence of specific safety data. They may damage growing cartilage and cause arthropathy in the developing fetus.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV inhibitors; concentration-dependent killing; postantibiotic effect (PAE); respiratory fluoroquinolones; QTc prolongation; tendinitis and tendon rupture; C8 methoxy quinolones; divalent/trivalent cation chelation; nalidixic acid byproduct; GABA-A antagonism.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - While most fluoroquinolones are primarily excreted renally and represent highly effective therapies for urinary tract infections, moxifloxacin undergoes hepatic/nonrenal clearance, achieving relatively low urinary concentrations, and is therefore the sole systemic agent of the class that is not recommended for the treatment of urinary tract infections.
    - Unlike most standard antibiotics (like beta-lactams) where bactericidal efficacy is time-dependent and driven by time-above-MIC, fluoroquinolones exhibit concentration-dependent killing, meaning their antimicrobial activity is optimized by maximizing the peak drug concentration (Cmax) or the AUC/MIC ratio.
    - While most classes of antibiotics are subject to enzymatic drug inactivation or modification by resistant bacterial enzymes, no quinolone-modifying or -inactivating activities have been identified in bacteria; resistance instead develops exclusively via target site mutations (DNA gyrase/topoisomerase IV) or active efflux.
    - Although fluoroquinolones have historically been avoided in pediatric populations due to cartilage toxicity seen in animal models, they represent a critical exception where they are routinely used in children for the management of severe Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections in the setting of cystic fibrosis.
