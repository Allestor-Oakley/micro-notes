### 1st Generation Cephalosporins
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:** Beta-lactam antibiotics; first-generation cephalosporins. The chemical nucleus is 7-aminocephalosporanic acid, which bears a close structural resemblance to 6-aminopenicillanic acid. The subclass includes cefazolin, cephalexin, cefadroxil, cephradine, cephalothin, and cephapirin.
*   **Target Organisms:** Gram-positive cocci, specifically streptococci and staphylococci, and a modest spectrum of Gram-negative aerobic bacilli.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:** Inhibits bacterial cell wall peptidoglycan synthesis. These agents form stable covalent bonds with penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), which act as transpeptidases and carboxypeptidases on the outer surface of the cell membrane. By binding to PBPs, first-generation cephalosporins block the final transpeptidation reaction that cross-links the linear peptidoglycan polymer chains (composed of alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid residues). This prevents the cross-linking of peptide side-chains, leading to structural weakening of the cell wall, osmotic instability, cell membrane damage, lysis, and rapid cell death.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:** Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) / cell wall transpeptidases located within the bacterial periplasmic space (for Gram-negative organisms) or outer membrane surface (for Gram-positive organisms).
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):** Rapidly bactericidal (exhibiting time-dependent killing).
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    *   Enzymatic hydrolysis: The most prevalent mechanism of resistance is enzymatic destruction of the cephalosporin core by hydrolysis of the beta-lactam ring. First-generation cephalosporins are susceptible to hydrolysis by constitutively produced chromosomal AmpC beta-lactamases (such as those produced by Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterobacter species) and plasmid-mediated extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) produced by Escherichia coli and Klebsiella species.
    *   Target site alteration: Resistance in methicillin-resistant staphylococci (MRSA and MRSE) is mediated by the acquisition of an altered high-molecular-weight penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a), encoded by the mecA gene, which features an extremely low binding affinity for all traditional beta-lactam antibiotics.
    *   Permeability barriers and active efflux: Decreased outer membrane permeability or active efflux pump expression can also prevent the antibiotic from accessing target PBPs.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:** Oral agents (cephalexin and cefadroxil) are well absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract. Cefazolin is poorly absorbed from the gut, and systemic use requires parenteral administration (intravenous or intramuscular). Following a 1-g intramuscular injection of cefazolin, plasma concentrations reach a peak of 64 mcg/mL.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:** Widely distributed into most body tissues and fluids. Cefazolin is bound to plasma proteins to a great extent (approximately 85%). First-generation cephalosporins penetrate poorly into the central nervous system (CNS) and do not achieve therapeutic levels in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), even when meninges are actively inflamed.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:** N/A (They do not undergo significant hepatic metabolism or interact with human cytochrome P450 enzyme systems).
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:** Eliminated primarily unchanged by the kidneys via glomerular filtration and active tubular secretion. Cefazolin has a longer elimination half-life (approximately 1.5 to 1.8 hours) compared to other first-generation agents (such as cephalexin or cephradine), which allows for less frequent dosing. Active renal excretion of first-generation cephalosporins is slowed by the concurrent administration of probenecid.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:** Clearance is directly proportional to renal function; dosages must be reduced in patients with renal impairment based on creatinine clearance (Clcr):
    *   Cephalexin (PO): Standard adult dose is 0.25 to 0.5 g orally four times daily (qid). Pediatric dose is 25 to 50 mg/kg/day in 4 divided doses. For renal failure: reduce the standard dose to 50% for Clcr approximately 50 mL/min, and to 25% for Clcr approximately 10 mL/min.
    *   Cefazolin (IV): Standard adult dose is 0.5 to 2 g intravenously every 8 hours (q8h). Pediatric dose is 25 to 100 mg/kg/day in 3 or 4 divided doses. For renal failure: reduce the standard dose to 50% for Clcr approximately 50 mL/min, and to 25% for Clcr approximately 10 mL/min.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    *   Skin and soft-tissue infections (such as cellulitis or uncomplicated abscesses) caused by susceptible streptococci and staphylococci.
    *   Uncomplicated lower urinary tract infections caused by susceptible Enterobacteriaceae.
    *   Surgical prophylaxis: Cefazolin is the standard prophylactic agent of choice for head and neck, gastroduodenal, biliary tract, gynecologic, thoracic, vascular, orthopedic, and clean surgical procedures.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:** Active against Gram-positive cocci including streptococci (except penicillin-resistant strains), methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA), and anaerobic cocci (peptococci, peptostreptococci). They have modest activity against Gram-negative bacilli including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Proteus mirabilis. They are completely inactive against penicillin-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae, methicillin-resistant staphylococci (MRSA, MRSE), enterococci (such as Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium), Listeria monocytogenes, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, indole-positive Proteus species, Enterobacter species, Serratia marcescens, Citrobacter species, Acinetobacter species, Bacteroides fragilis, Legionella pneumophila, L. micdadei, Clostridium difficile, Xanthomonas maltophilia, and Campylobacter jejuni.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:** N/A

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:** Local injection site reactions (pain, tenderness, and phlebitis following parenteral administration), transient skin rash, drug fever, and mild gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea).
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:** Immediate hypersensitivity reactions (anaphylaxis, bronchospasm, urticaria), interstitial nephritis, granulocytopenia, and autoimmune hemolytic anemia.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:** N/A
*   **Black Box Warnings:** N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:** Prior history of immediate, severe hypersensitivity reactions (such as anaphylaxis, giant urticaria, or bronchospasm) to any cephalosporin or other beta-lactam antibiotics. First-generation cephalosporins must be avoided in patients with a documented history of penicillin anaphylaxis.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:** Administer with caution in patients with a history of mild penicillin allergy (such as maculopapular rash) due to a low rate of cross-allergenicity (approximately 1%), and in patients with pre-existing renal insufficiency.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:** Co-administration with probenecid decreases the renal tubular secretion of cephalosporins, resulting in elevated and prolonged serum drug concentrations.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** Generally considered safe for use in pregnancy. They are commonly used as alternative agents to treat urinary tract infections in pregnant patients when other first-line drugs are contraindicated.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Sardinian sewer outlet isolate; Brotzu (1948); 7-aminocephalosporanic acid nucleus; cefazolin surgical prophylaxis; oral cephalexin; transpeptidase inhibition; low cross-allergenicity; poor CSF penetration.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   Unlike natural penicillin G, which is highly sensitive to staphylococcal penicillinase, first-generation cephalosporins are highly stable against hydrolysis by staphylococcal penicillinase and represent a primary drug of choice for methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) infections.
    *   Unlike broad-spectrum beta-lactams (such as carbapenems), first-generation cephalosporins have a narrow Gram-negative spectrum and possess zero activity against key clinical pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Bacteroides fragilis.
    *   Although Pasteurella multocida (commonly isolated from cat and dog bites) is highly sensitive to penicillins and ampicillin, it is intrinsically resistant to first-generation cephalosporins.
    *   Traditional first-generation cephalosporins may appear susceptible to MRSA strains on in vitro disk-sensitivity testing, but resistant populations rapidly emerge during clinical therapy, leading to treatment failure; therefore, they must never be used to treat infections caused by MRSA.
    *   Surgical prophylaxis timing is highly specific: parenteral cefazolin must be administered during the interval beginning 60 minutes before the surgical incision to ensure therapeutic tissue levels, while in cesarean sections, the drug is administered immediately after umbilical cord clamping.
