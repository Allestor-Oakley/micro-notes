### 2nd Generation Cephalosporins
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:** Beta-lactam antibiotics; second-generation cephalosporins. The class includes oral agents (cefaclor, cefprozil, and cefuroxime axetil) and parenteral agents (cefuroxime, ceforanide, and the cephamycins: cefoxitin, cefotetan, and cefmetazole). Cephamycins are distinguished by a methoxy group at position 7 of the beta-lactam ring of the 7-aminocephalosporanic acid nucleus.
*   **Target Organisms:** Gram-negative aerobic bacilli, Gram-positive cocci, and aerobic/anaerobic cocci. A subset of second-generation agents (cephamycins: cefoxitin, cefotetan, and cefmetazole) also targets anaerobic Gram-negative bacilli, specifically the Bacteroides fragilis group.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:** Inhibits bacterial cell wall peptidoglycan synthesis. These agents form stable covalent bonds with penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), which function as transpeptidases and carboxypeptidases on the outer surface of the bacterial cell membrane. By binding to PBPs, second-generation cephalosporins block the final transpeptidation reaction that cross-links the linear peptidoglycan polymer chains (composed of alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid residues). This blocks the cross-linking of peptide side-chains, leading to structural weakening of the cell wall, osmotic instability, cell membrane damage, lysis, and cell death.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:** Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) / cell wall transpeptidases located within the bacterial periplasmic space (for Gram-negative organisms) or outer membrane surface (for Gram-positive organisms).
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):** Rapidly bactericidal (exhibiting time-dependent killing).
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    *   Enzymatic hydrolysis: Hydrolysis of the beta-lactam ring by constitutively produced chromosomal AmpC beta-lactamases (such as those produced by Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterobacter) or extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) in Enterobacteriaceae.
    *   Target site alteration: Resistance in methicillin-resistant staphylococci (MRSA, MRSE) is mediated by the acquisition of an altered high-molecular-weight penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a), encoded by the mecA gene, which features a very low binding affinity for all traditional beta-lactams.
    *   Permeability barriers and active efflux: Decreased outer membrane permeability or active efflux pump expression can also prevent the antibiotic from accessing target PBPs.
    Second-generation cephalosporins lack reliable activity against penicillin-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae, methicillin-resistant staphylococci (MRSA, MRSE), enterococci, Listeria monocytogenes, Legionella species, Clostridium difficile, Xanthomonas maltophilia, Campylobacter jejuni, KPC-producing Enterobacteriaceae, and Acinetobacter species.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:** Oral agents (cefaclor, cefprozil, and cefuroxime axetil) are well absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract. Cefuroxime axetil is the acetyloxyethyl ester of cefuroxime; it is a prodrug that is hydrolyzed by esterases during absorption in the gastrointestinal tract to release active cefuroxime. Parenteral agents (cefoxitin, cefotetan, cefuroxime, ceforanide) are poorly absorbed from the gut and require intravenous or intramuscular administration.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:** Widely distributed into most body tissues and fluids. Cefuroxime penetrates well into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the presence of inflamed meninges, achieving therapeutic levels sufficient to treat bacterial meningitis (though CSF penetration of first- and second-generation cephalosporins is generally inferior to third-generation agents).
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:** N/A (They do not undergo significant hepatic metabolism or interact with human cytochrome P450 enzyme systems).
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:** Eliminated primarily unchanged by the kidneys via glomerular filtration and active tubular secretion. Half-lives: cefaclor (~0.7 h), cefoxitin (~0.7 h), cefprozil (~1.3 h), cefuroxime (~1.7 h), ceforanide (~2.6 h), cefotetan (~3.3 h). Active renal excretion is slowed by the concurrent administration of probenecid.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:** Clearance is renal and directly proportional to renal function; dosages must be reduced in patients with renal impairment based on creatinine clearance (Clcr):
    *   Cefoxitin (IV): Standard adult dose is 1 to 2 g q6-8h. For Clcr of ~50 mL/min, reduce the standard dose to 50% to 75%; for Clcr of ~10 mL/min, reduce to 25%.
    *   Cefotetan (IV): Standard adult dose is 1 to 2 g q12h. For Clcr of ~50 mL/min, reduce the standard dose to 50%; for Clcr of ~10 mL/min, reduce to 25%.
    *   Cefuroxime (IV): Standard adult dose is 0.75 to 1.5 g q8h (up to 3 g q8h for severe infections). For Clcr of ~50 mL/min, reduce the standard dose to 66%; for Clcr of ~10 mL/min, reduce to 25% to 33%.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    *   Parenteral use: Septicemia, community-acquired pneumonia, meningitis (cefuroxime), peritonitis or pelvic infections due to ruptured viscus (cefoxitin, cefotetan), and surgical prophylaxis (cefoxitin and cefotetan for colorectal surgery, appendectomy, or hysterectomy).
    *   Oral use: Sinusitis, acute otitis media, urinary tract infections, and uncomplicated skin and soft-tissue infections.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:** Active against Gram-negative bacilli (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella species, Proteus species, Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis) and Gram-positive cocci (streptococci, methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus). The cephamycins (cefoxitin, cefotetan, cefmetazole) are active against anaerobes (Bacteroides fragilis and other Bacteroides species) but are inferior to cefuroxime against staphylococci. They are completely inactive against enterococci, Listeria monocytogenes, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and MRSA.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:** Doxycycline (100 mg IV q12h) is commonly combined with intravenous cefoxitin or cefotetan to cover anaerobes, Gram-negative bacilli, and Chlamydia trachomatis in patients with acute pelvic inflammatory disease (including endometritis, salpingitis, parametritis, and peritonitis).

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:** Local injection site reactions (pain, tenderness, and phlebitis following parenteral administration), transient skin rash, drug fever, and mild gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea).
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:** Immediate hypersensitivity reactions (anaphylaxis, bronchospasm, urticaria), interstitial nephritis, granulocytopenia, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, Clostridium difficile-associated diarrhea / pseudomembranous colitis, and platelet-mediated bleeding/hypoprothrombinemia.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:** Severe disulfiram-like reactions when co-administered with alcohol or alcohol-containing medications (specifically associated with cefotetan due to its methylthiotetrazole side chain).
*   **Black Box Warnings:** N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:** Prior history of immediate, severe hypersensitivity reactions (such as anaphylaxis, giant urticaria, or bronchospasm) to any cephalosporin or other beta-lactam antibiotics. First- and second-generation cephalosporins must be avoided in patients with a documented history of penicillin anaphylaxis.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:** Administer with caution in patients with a history of mild penicillin allergy (such as maculopapular rash) due to cross-allergenicity (~1%), and in patients with pre-existing renal insufficiency.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    *   Probenecid: Decreases the renal tubular secretion of cephalosporins, resulting in elevated and prolonged serum drug concentrations.
    *   Alcohol / Ethanol: Co-administration with cefotetan results in a disulfiram-like reaction (avoid alcohol-containing beverages and medications).
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** Generally considered safe for use in pregnancy. They are commonly used as alternative agents to treat urinary tract infections in pregnant patients when other first-line drugs are contraindicated.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Cephamycins; 7-alpha-methoxy group; Bacteroides fragilis coverage; abdominal/pelvic prophylaxis; cefuroxime axetil prodrug; disulfiram-like reaction; MTT side chain; pelvic inflammatory disease partner.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   While cephalosporins as a general class are notorious for lacking reliable activity against anaerobic pathogens, the cephamycin subgroup of second-generation cephalosporins (cefoxitin and cefotetan) features a unique 7-alpha-methoxy substitution on the beta-lactam ring that sterically hinders beta-lactamase degradation and extends their spectrum to include Bacteroides fragilis and other anaerobes.
    *   Unlike most cephalosporins, cefotetan possesses a methylthiotetrazole (MTT) side chain that can inhibit aldehyde dehydrogenase, leading to an accumulation of acetaldehyde and producing a severe, symptomatic disulfiram-like reaction (nausea, vomiting, headache, hypotension) upon alcohol ingestion.
    *   Although oral cephalosporins are usually administered directly as active drugs, cefuroxime axetil is administered orally as an inactive prodrug ester (acetyloxyethyl ester) to improve gastrointestinal absorption; it is subsequently cleaved by mucosal esterases during absorption to release active cefuroxime into the circulation.
