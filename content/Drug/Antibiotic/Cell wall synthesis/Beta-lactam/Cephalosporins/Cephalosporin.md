### Cephalosporin
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:** Beta-lactam antibiotics; Cephalosporin class. Derived from the active nucleus 7-aminocephalosporanic acid, which closely resembles the penicillin nucleus 6-aminopenicillanic acid. The class is traditionally divided into generations:
    *   [[1st gen cephalosporins|First-Generation]]: Includes cefazolin, cephalexin, cefadroxil, cephradine, cephalothin, and cephapirin.
    *   [[2nd Generation Cephalosporins|Second-Generation]]: Includes cefuroxime, cefuroxime axetil, cefprozil, cefaclor, ceforanide, and the cephamycins (cefoxitin, cefotetan, and cefmetazole), which are characterized by a 7-alpha-methoxy group on the beta-lactam ring.
    *   [[3rd Generation Cephalosporins|Third-Generation]]: Includes parenteral agents (cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, ceftizoxime, cefoperazone, moxalactam) and oral agents (cefixime, cefpodoxime proxetil, cefdinir, cefditoren pivoxil, ceftibuten).
    *   [[4th Generation Cephalosporins|Fourth-Generation]]: Cefepime and cefpirome.
    *   MRSA-Active ([[5th Generation Cephalosporins|Fifth-Generation]]): Ceftaroline fosamil.
    *   Cephalosporin-Beta-Lactamase Inhibitor Combinations: Ceftazidime-avibactam and ceftolozane-tazobactam.
*   **Target Organisms:** Aerobic and anaerobic Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, varying widely by generation. Broadly, Gram-positive activity is strongest in first-generation agents and decreases in second and third generations (with the exception of cefotaxime/ceftriaxone which retain good activity), while Gram-negative activity progressively expands from first through fourth generations. Ceftazidime and cefepime cover Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Ceftaroline specifically targets methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA).
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:** Inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis. Cephalosporins form stable covalent bonds with penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), which function as transpeptidases and carboxypeptidases on the outer surface of the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane. By binding to PBPs, these agents block the final transpeptidation reaction required to cross-link the linear peptidoglycan polymer chains (alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid residues). This prevents the cross-linking of peptide side-chains, leading to structural weakening of the cell wall, osmotic instability, cell membrane damage, lysis, and rapid cell death.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:** Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) / cell wall transpeptidases located within the bacterial periplasmic space (for Gram-negative organisms) or outer membrane surface (for Gram-positive organisms).
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):** Rapidly bactericidal (exhibiting time-dependent killing).
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    *   Enzymatic Hydrolysis: The most prevalent mechanism of resistance is enzymatic destruction of the cephalosporin core by hydrolysis of the beta-lactam ring. Susceptible to hydrolysis by constitutively produced chromosomal AmpC beta-lactamases (such as those produced by Enterobacter species, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Serratia, Providencia, Acinetobacter, and Citrobacter when constitutively expressed or derepressed) and plasmid-mediated extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) produced by Escherichia coli and Klebsiella species. They are also hydrolyzed by carbapenemases (such as KPC class A beta-lactamases) and metallo-beta-lactamases.
    *   Target Site Alteration: Resistance in methicillin-resistant staphylococci (MRSA and MRSE) is mediated by the acquisition of an altered high-molecular-weight penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a), encoded by the mecA gene, which features an extremely low binding affinity for all traditional beta-lactam antibiotics.
    *   Permeability Barriers & Efflux: Decreased outer membrane permeability (loss of specific porin channels) or active efflux pump expression can prevent the antibiotic from accessing target PBPs.
    *   None of the available cephalosporins have reliable activity against: Listeria monocytogenes, enterococci (except ceftaroline which has minor activity), Legionella pneumophila, L. micdadei, Clostridium difficile, Xanthomonas (Stenotrophomonas) maltophilia, Campylobacter jejuni, and KPC-producing Enterobacteriaceae.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:** Oral agents (such as cephalexin, cefadroxil, cefaclor, cefprozil, cefuroxime axetil, cefixime, cefpodoxime proxetil, cefdinir, ceftibuten, and cefditoren pivoxil) are well absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract. Cefuroxime axetil, cefditoren pivoxil, and cefpodoxime proxetil are administered as inactive ester prodrugs to improve absorption and are hydrolyzed by esterases during absorption to release the active drug into the systemic circulation. Parenteral agents must be administered intravenously or intramuscularly; they are poorly absorbed from the gut. Intravenous infusion of 1 g of a parenteral cephalosporin produces serum levels of 60 to 140 mcg/mL.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:** Widely and extensively distributed into most body tissues and fluids. Cefazolin is highly protein-bound (approximately 85%), as is ceftriaxone. First- and second-generation cephalosporins (with the exception of cefuroxime) penetrate poorly into the central nervous system (CNS) and do not achieve therapeutic levels in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Third- and fourth-generation agents (specifically ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, and cefepime) achieve excellent CSF penetration when meninges are actively inflamed, reaching levels sufficient to treat bacterial meningitis.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:** Cephalosporins generally do not undergo significant hepatic metabolism or interact with human cytochrome P450 enzyme systems. Standard prodrug esters are cleaved by intestinal and plasma esterases.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:** Most cephalosporins are excreted primarily unchanged by the kidneys via glomerular filtration and active tubular secretion. Active renal tubular secretion is slowed by the concurrent administration of probenecid. Exceptions to renal clearance are cefoperazone (and cefpiramide), which are excreted predominantly in the bile, and ceftriaxone, which has mixed clearance (both renal excretion and elimination in the bile/feces). Normal half-lives: cefazolin (1.5-1.8 h), cefoxitin (0.7 h), cefotetan (3.3 h), cefuroxime (1.7 h), cefotaxime (1.1 h), ceftazidime (1.8 h), cefepime (2 h), ceftaroline (2.7 h), and ceftriaxone (6-8 h - mixed clearance with a long half-life).
*   **Dosage Adjustments:** Clearance of most agents is directly proportional to renal function; dosages must be reduced in patients with renal impairment based on creatinine clearance (Clcr). Notable exceptions are ceftriaxone (no dosage adjustment required in renal failure due to mixed clearance) and cefoperazone (excreted predominantly in bile).

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    *   First-Generation: Used for skin and soft-tissue infections (such as cellulitis or uncomplicated abscesses) caused by susceptible streptococci and staphylococci; uncomplicated lower urinary tract infections; and surgical prophylaxis (cefazolin is the standard prophylactic agent of choice for head and neck, gastroduodenal, biliary tract, gynecologic, thoracic, vascular, orthopedic, and clean surgical procedures).
    *   Second-Generation: Used for community-acquired pneumonia, acute otitis media, sinusitis, urinary tract infections, peritonitis or pelvic infections due to ruptured viscus (cefoxitin and cefotetan), and surgical prophylaxis (cefoxitin and cefotetan for colorectal surgery, appendectomy, or hysterectomy).
    *   Third-Generation: Serious Gram-negative infections (septicemia, osteomyelitis, pneumonia), bacterial meningitis (ceftriaxone and cefotaxime are the drugs of choice for initial empirical treatment of meningitis in adults and children >3 months of age), gonorrhea (ceftriaxone is the recommended first-line therapy of choice for all forms), Lyme disease (ceftriaxone is the drug of choice for severe neurological, cardiac, or joint manifestations), and Pseudomonas meningitis (ceftazidime).
    *   Fourth-Generation: Empirical treatment of serious nosocomial infections where antibiotic resistance owing to ESBLs or chromosomally induced (AmpC) beta-lactamases is anticipated; also used empirically as monotherapy or in combination with other agents in patients presenting with febrile neutropenia (cefepime).
    *   MRSA-Active (Fifth-Generation): Complicated skin and soft-tissue infections and community-acquired pneumonia (ceftaroline fosamil).
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:** Highly active against Gram-positive cocci (streptococci, staphylococci) in earlier generations, and Gram-negative bacilli (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, Enterobacteriaceae) in later generations. Cephamycins (cefoxitin, cefotetan) are active against anaerobes (Bacteroides fragilis). Ceftazidime and cefepime cover Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Ceftaroline covers MRSA. They are completely inactive against Listeria monocytogenes, enterococci (except minor ceftaroline activity), Legionella, Mycoplasma, Chlamydia, and Clostridium difficile.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    *   Ceftriaxone or cefotaxime used in combination with vancomycin for empirical therapy of suspected penicillin-resistant pneumococcal meningitis.
    *   Doxycycline is commonly combined with intravenous cefoxitin or cefotetan to cover anaerobes, Gram-negative bacilli, and Chlamydia trachomatis in patients with acute pelvic inflammatory disease.
    *   Ceftaroline fosamil used off-label to treat highly complicated or deep-seated infections such as MRSA bacteremia, endocarditis, and osteomyelitis, often in combination with other antimicrobial agents and at an increased dose of 600 mg every 8 hours.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:** Local injection site reactions (pain, tenderness, and phlebitis following parenteral administration), transient skin rash, drug fever, and mild gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea).
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:** Immediate hypersensitivity reactions (anaphylaxis, bronchospasm, urticaria), interstitial nephritis, granulocytopenia/leukopenia, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, Clostridium difficile-associated diarrhea / pseudomembranous colitis, and platelet-mediated bleeding/hypoprothrombinemia.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:** Severe disulfiram-like reactions when co-administered with alcohol or alcohol-containing medications (specifically associated with cefotetan due to its methylthiotetrazole (MTT) side chain).
*   **Black Box Warnings:** N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:** Prior history of immediate, severe hypersensitivity reactions (such as anaphylaxis, giant urticaria, or bronchospasm) to any cephalosporin or other beta-lactam antibiotics. Cephalosporins must be avoided in patients with a documented history of penicillin anaphylaxis.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:** Administer with caution in patients with a history of mild penicillin allergy (such as maculopapular rash) due to a low rate of cross-allergenicity (less than 10%, often around 1%), and in patients with pre-existing renal insufficiency. Ceftriaxone should be avoided in the treatment of Enterobacter infections—even if the clinical isolate appears susceptible in vitro—because of the rapid emergence of resistance.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    *   Probenecid: Decreases the renal tubular secretion of cephalosporins, resulting in elevated and prolonged serum drug concentrations (except cefoperazone, which is predominantly biliary).
    *   Alcohol / Ethanol: Co-administration with cefotetan results in a disulfiram-like reaction (avoid alcohol-containing beverages and medications).
    *   Aztreonam: Ceftazidime shares considerable structural similarity with aztreonam, leading to a risk of in vivo cross-allergenicity; aztreonam should be avoided in patients with immediate hypersensitivity to ceftazidime.
    *   Synergistic combination with aminoglycosides (such as ceftazidime plus an aminoglycoside for Pseudomonas aeruginosa meningitis).
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** Generally considered safe for use in pregnancy. They are commonly used as alternative agents to treat urinary tract infections and gonorrhea in pregnant patients when other first-line drugs are contraindicated.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Sardinian sewer outlet isolate; Giuseppe Brotzu (1948); 7-aminocephalosporanic acid nucleus; surgical prophylaxis timing; methylthiotetrazole (MTT) side chain; disulfiram-like reaction; cross-allergenicity with aztreonam; mixed clearance; AmpC beta-lactamase stability; PBP2a high-affinity binding.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   While cephalosporins as a general class require renal dosage adjustments, ceftriaxone possesses mixed renal and biliary clearance and therefore requires zero dosage adjustment in patients with renal failure.
    *   Although cephalosporins traditionally lack anaerobic activity, the cephamycin subgroup of second-generation cephalosporins (cefoxitin and cefotetan) features a unique 7-alpha-methoxy substitution on the beta-lactam ring that provides steric hindrance against beta-lactamase degradation and extends their spectrum to include Bacteroides fragilis.
    *   While cephalosporins historically lack activity against enterococci and MRSA, ceftaroline (fifth-generation) is specifically designed with high binding affinity for PBP2a, restoring bactericidal activity against MRSA and demonstrating minor activity against enterococci.
    *   Although aztreonam is highly useful for treating patients with severe penicillin/cephalosporin allergies because it typically lacks cross-allergenicity, it shares considerable structural similarity with ceftazidime, creating a unique exception where cross-allergenicity can occur in ceftazidime-allergic individuals.
    *   Traditional cephalosporins may appear susceptible to MRSA strains on in vitro disk-sensitivity testing, but resistant populations rapidly emerge during clinical therapy, leading to treatment failure; therefore, they must never be used to treat infections caused by MRSA (with the sole exception of ceftaroline).
    *   Ceftriaxone and other third-generation cephalosporins should be strictly avoided when treating Enterobacter infections, even if the isolate appears fully susceptible on in vitro testing, due to the high risk of rapid resistance emergence (via derepression of chromosomal AmpC beta-lactamases) during therapy; cefepime (fourth-generation) is highly stable to these enzymes and represents the preferred agent.
