### 4th Generation Cephalosporins
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:** Beta-lactam antibiotics; fourth-generation cephalosporins. The chemical nucleus is 7-aminocephalosporanic acid. Subclass members include cefepime and cefpirome.
*   **Target Organisms:** Gram-positive bacteria (including staphylococci and streptococci) and Gram-negative bacteria (including Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa).
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:** Inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis. These agents bind to and inhibit cell wall transpeptidases (penicillin-binding proteins, or PBPs) on the outer surface of the cell membrane, preventing the cross-linking of peptidoglycan chains composed of alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid residues, which weakens the peptidoglycan cell wall and leads to osmotic instability, lysis, and cell death.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:** Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) / cell wall transpeptidases.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):** Rapidly bactericidal (exhibiting time-dependent killing).
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:** Resistance is primarily mediated by enzymatic hydrolysis of the beta-lactam ring. Fourth-generation cephalosporins exhibit increased stability to hydrolysis by inducible, chromosomally encoded type I (AmpC) beta-lactamases (such as those produced by Enterobacter species) and some plasmid-encoded beta-lactamases (TEM-1, TEM-2, SHV-1). However, they remain susceptible to hydrolysis by extended-spectrum plasmid-mediated beta-lactamases (such as KPC, TEM-3, and TEM-10) and metallo-beta-lactamases. Resistance in methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and methicillin-resistant S. epidermidis is due to target site alterations, specifically the acquisition of a high-molecular-weight penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a) with low affinity for beta-lactam antibiotics. Intrinsic or acquired resistance is also present in enterococci, Listeria monocytogenes, Legionella pneumophila, L. micdadei, Clostridium difficile, Xanthomonas maltophilia, Campylobacter jejuni, and Acinetobacter species.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:** Systemic administration requires parenteral (intravenous) injection; they are not orally active. Oral bioavailability is N/A.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:** Widely distributed into body fluids and tissues. Cefepime penetrates well into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the presence of inflamed meninges.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:** N/A
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:** Cleared primarily by the kidneys via renal excretion. The elimination half-life of cefepime is approximately 2 hours. Active renal tubular secretion is slowed by the concurrent administration of probenecid.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:** Clearance is directly proportional to renal function and requires dosage modification in renal insufficiency.
    *   Cefepime (IV): Standard adult dose is 1 to 2 g intravenously every 12 hours (dosing is typically increased to 2 g intravenously every 8 hours for complicated Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections or in the setting of immunocompromise). Pediatric dose is 75 to 120 mg/kg/day in 2 or 3 divided doses.
    *   In the setting of renal failure, dosage is adjusted based on creatinine clearance (Clcr): reduce standard dose to 50% for Clcr of approximately 50 mL/min, and to 25% for Clcr of approximately 10 mL/min.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:** Indicated for the empirical treatment of serious nosocomial infections where antibiotic resistance owing to extended-spectrum beta-lactamases or chromosomally induced (AmpC) beta-lactamases is anticipated. It is also used empirically as monotherapy or in combination with other agents in patients presenting with febrile neutropenia.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:** Active against Gram-negative bacilli (including Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter, Citrobacter, Serratia, Haemophilus, and Neisseria species) and Gram-positive cocci (including methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Streptococcus pyogenes, and most penicillin-non-susceptible strains of streptococci). It is inactive against penicillin-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae (at high MICs), MRSA, methicillin-resistant S. epidermidis and other coagulase-negative staphylococci, Enterococcus faecalis, Enterococcus faecium, Listeria monocytogenes, Legionella species, Clostridium difficile, Xanthomonas (Stenotrophomonas) maltophilia, Campylobacter jejuni, KPC-producing Enterobacteriaceae, metallo-beta-lactamase expressing strains, and Acinetobacter species.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:** N/A

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:** Local injection site reactions (pain, tenderness, and phlebitis following parenteral administration), transient skin rash, and drug fever.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:** Immediate hypersensitivity reactions (anaphylaxis, bronchospasm, urticaria), interstitial nephritis, granulocytopenia, and autoimmune hemolytic anemia.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:** N/A
*   **Black Box Warnings:** N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:** Prior history of immediate, severe hypersensitivity reactions (such as anaphylaxis, giant urticaria, or bronchospasm) to any cephalosporin or other beta-lactam antibiotics. Avoid in patients with a documented history of penicillin anaphylaxis.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:** Administer with caution in patients with a history of mild penicillin allergy (such as maculopapular rash) due to a low rate of cross-reactivity (<10%), and in patients with pre-existing renal insufficiency.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:** Co-administration with probenecid decreases the renal tubular secretion of cephalosporins, resulting in elevated and prolonged serum drug concentrations.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** N/A

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Cefepime; Maxipime; fourth-generation cephalosporin; AmpC beta-lactamase stability; febrile neutropenia empirical backbone; 2-hour half-life; renal clearance; poor inducer of type I beta-lactamases.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   While third-generation cephalosporins (such as ceftazidime or ceftriaxone) are easily hydrolyzed by chromosomally encoded AmpC beta-lactamases (resulting in rapid resistance development during therapy of Enterobacter infections), the fourth-generation agent cefepime is a poor inducer of, and is relatively resistant to, type I chromosomally encoded and some extended-spectrum beta-lactamases, making it highly effective and useful for treating Enterobacter infections.
    *   Unlike ceftazidime, which possesses potent Gram-negative activity (including against Pseudomonas aeruginosa) but lacks significant Gram-positive activity, cefepime maintains excellent activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterobacteriaceae while simultaneously possessing potent Gram-positive activity against methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pneumoniae (including most penicillin-non-susceptible strains of streptococci).
