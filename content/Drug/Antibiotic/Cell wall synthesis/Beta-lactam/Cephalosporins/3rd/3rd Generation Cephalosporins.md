### 3rd Generation Cephalosporins
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:** Beta-lactam antibiotics; third-generation cephalosporins. The subclass includes parenteral agents (cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, ceftizoxime, cefoperazone, and moxalactam) and oral agents (cefixime, cefpodoxime proxetil, cefdinir, cefditoren pivoxil, and ceftibuten). Cefoperazone, ceftizoxime, and moxalactam are no longer commercially available in the USA.
*   **Target Organisms:** Gram-negative aerobic bacilli (including Enterobacteriaceae, Serratia marcescens, Providencia, Citrobacter, Haemophilus influenzae, and Moraxella catarrhalis), Gram-negative cocci (Neisseria meningitidis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae), and Gram-positive cocci (including Streptococcus pneumoniae, Streptococcus pyogenes, and methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus). Ceftazidime is also highly active against Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:** Inhibits bacterial cell wall peptidoglycan synthesis. These agents form stable covalent bonds with penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), which function as transpeptidases and carboxypeptidases on the outer surface of the bacterial cell membrane. By binding to PBPs, third-generation cephalosporins block the final transpeptidation reaction that cross-links the linear peptidoglycan polymer chains (composed of alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid residues). This blocks the cross-linking of peptide side-chains, leading to structural weakening of the cell wall, osmotic instability, cell membrane damage, lysis, and cell death.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:** Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) / cell wall transpeptidases located within the bacterial periplasmic space (for Gram-negative organisms) or outer membrane surface (for Gram-positive organisms).
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):** Rapidly bactericidal (exhibiting time-dependent killing).
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    *   Enzymatic hydrolysis: The most prevalent mechanism of resistance is enzymatic destruction of the cephalosporin core by hydrolysis of the beta-lactam ring. Third-generation cephalosporins are susceptible to hydrolysis by constitutively produced chromosomal AmpC beta-lactamases (such as those produced by Enterobacter species, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Serratia, Providencia, Acinetobacter, and Citrobacter when constitutively expressed or derepressed) and plasmid-mediated extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) produced by Escherichia coli and Klebsiella species. They are also hydrolyzed by carbapenemases (such as KPC class A beta-lactamases).
    *   Target site alteration: Resistance in methicillin-resistant staphylococci (MRSA and MRSE) is mediated by the acquisition of an altered high-molecular-weight penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a), encoded by the mecA gene, which features an extremely low binding affinity for all traditional beta-lactam antibiotics.
    *   Third-generation cephalosporins completely lack reliable activity against: penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae (at high MICs), MRSA, MRSE, Enterococcus, Listeria monocytogenes, Legionella pneumophila, L. micdadei, Clostridium difficile, Xanthomonas (Stenotrophomonas) maltophilia, Campylobacter jejuni, and KPC-producing Enterobacteriaceae.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:** Oral agents (cefixime, cefpodoxime proxetil, cefdinir, cefditoren pivoxil, ceftibuten) are well absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract. Cefditoren pivoxil is a prodrug that is hydrolyzed by esterases during absorption to the active drug, cefditoren. Cefpodoxime proxetil is also an inactive prodrug ester hydrolyzed during absorption to active cefpodoxime. Parenteral agents (cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, ceftizoxime, cefoperazone, moxalactam) are poorly absorbed from the gut and require intravenous or intramuscular administration. Intravenous infusion of 1 g of a parenteral cephalosporin produces serum levels of 60 to 140 mcg/mL.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:** Widely and extensively distributed into most body tissues and fluids. They achieve excellent penetration into the central nervous system (CNS) and cross the blood-brain barrier; intravenous agents (specifically ceftriaxone and cefotaxime) achieve levels in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) sufficient to inhibit most susceptible pathogens and treat bacterial meningitis when meninges are actively inflamed.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:** N/A (They do not undergo significant hepatic metabolism or interact with human cytochrome P450 enzyme systems).
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:** Most third-generation cephalosporins are excreted primarily unchanged by the kidneys via glomerular filtration and active tubular secretion. Exceptions are cefoperazone (and cefpiramide), which are excreted predominantly in the bile (nonrenal), and ceftriaxone, which has mixed clearance (both renal excretion and elimination in the bile/feces). Half-lives: cefotaxime (~1.1 h), ceftazidime (~1.8 h), ceftizoxime (~1.8 h), cefpodoxime (~2.2 h), cefditoren (~1.6 h), cefixime (~3-4 h), ceftriaxone (~6-8 h - mixed clearance with a long half-life). Active renal excretion is slowed by the concurrent administration of probenecid.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:** Clearance of most agents is directly proportional to renal function; dosages must be reduced in patients with renal impairment based on creatinine clearance (Clcr). Exceptions are ceftriaxone (no dosage adjustment required in renal failure due to mixed clearance) and cefoperazone (excreted predominantly in bile).
    *   Cefotaxime (IV): Standard adult dose is 1 to 2 g intravenously every 6 to 12 hours (up to 2 g every 4 to 8 hours for severe infections). Pediatric dose is 50 to 200 mg/kg/day in 4 to 6 divided doses. Neonatal dose is 100 mg/kg/day in 2 divided doses. In renal failure: reduce standard dose to 50% for Clcr of ~50 mL/min, and 25% for Clcr of ~10 mL/min.
    *   Ceftazidime (IV): Standard adult dose is 1 to 2 g intravenously every 8 to 12 hours (up to 2 g every 8 hours). Pediatric dose is 75 to 150 mg/kg/day in 3 divided doses. Neonatal dose is 100 to 150 mg/kg/day in 2 or 3 divided doses. In renal failure: reduce to 50% for Clcr of ~50 mL/min, and 25% for Clcr of ~10 mL/min.
    *   Ceftriaxone (IV): Standard adult dose is 1 to 4 g intravenously every 24 hours (or 2 g every 12 to 24 hours). Pediatric dose is 50 to 100 mg/kg/day in 1 or 2 divided doses. Neonatal dose is 50 mg/kg/day once daily. No dosage adjustments required for renal failure.
    *   Cefixime (PO): Standard adult dose is 400 mg/day (or 200 mg every 12 hours). In renal impairment: 300 mg/day for Clcr between 20 and 60 mL/min, and 200 mg/day for Clcr <20 mL/min.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    *   Serious infections: Septicemia, community-acquired pneumonia, osteomyelitis, and skin and soft-tissue infections caused by susceptible multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacilli (such as Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Proteus, Providencia, Serratia, and Haemophilus species).
    *   Bacterial Meningitis: Ceftriaxone and cefotaxime are the drugs of choice for initial empirical treatment of meningitis in adults and children >3 months of age (in combination with vancomycin and ampicillin pending identification). They are the drugs of choice for meningitis caused by H. influenzae, sensitive S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, and Gram-negative enteric bacilli. Ceftazidime (plus an aminoglycoside) is the treatment of choice for Pseudomonas meningitis.
    *   Gonorrhea: Ceftriaxone is the recommended first-line therapy of choice for all forms of gonorrhea, including uncomplicated urethritis (single 250 mg intramuscular injection), disseminated gonococcal infections, gonococcemia, gonococcal arthritis, and ophthalmia neonatorum.
    *   Lyme Disease: Ceftriaxone is the drug of choice for severe forms of Lyme disease (neurological, cardiac, or severe joint manifestations).
    *   Empirical therapy: Sepsis and febrile neutropenia (often combined with an aminoglycoside).
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:** Highly active against Gram-negative bacilli (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella species, Proteus species, indole-positive Proteus, Serratia marcescens, Citrobacter, Providencia, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis) and Gram-negative cocci (Neisseria meningitidis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae). Active against Gram-positive cocci (streptococci, including S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, S. agalactiae, and methicillin-susceptible S. aureus [MSSA] - though cefotaxime is the most active against S. aureus and S. pyogenes, whereas ceftazidime lacks significant Gram-positive activity). Ceftazidime is the only third-generation agent with useful activity against P. aeruginosa. Completely inactive against: penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae (with high MICs), MRSA, MRSE, Enterococcus, L. monocytogenes, Legionella, C. difficile, S. maltophilia, C. jejuni, KPC-producing Enterobacteriaceae, Acinetobacter species, and B. fragilis.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:** Ceftriaxone or cefotaxime used in combination with vancomycin for empirical therapy of suspected penicillin-resistant pneumococcal meningitis. Ceftriaxone used off-label at high doses for complicated Lyme disease.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:** Local injection site reactions (pain, tenderness, and phlebitis following parenteral administration), transient skin rash, drug fever, and mild gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea).
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:** Immediate hypersensitivity reactions (anaphylaxis, bronchospasm, urticaria), interstitial nephritis, granulocytopenia/leukopenia, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, Clostridium difficile-associated diarrhea / pseudomembranous colitis.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:** N/A
*   **Black Box Warnings:** N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:** Prior history of immediate, severe hypersensitivity reactions (such as anaphylaxis, giant urticaria, or bronchospasm) to any cephalosporin or other beta-lactam antibiotics. Avoid in patients with a documented history of penicillin anaphylaxis.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:** Administer with caution in patients with a history of mild penicillin allergy (such as maculopapular rash) due to cross-reactivity (<10%), and in patients with pre-existing renal insufficiency (requiring dose adjustments). Ceftriaxone should be avoided in treatment of Enterobacter infections—even if the clinical isolate appears susceptible in vitro—because of the rapid emergence of resistance.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    *   Probenecid: Decreases the renal tubular secretion of cephalosporins, resulting in elevated and prolonged serum drug concentrations (except cefoperazone, which is predominantly biliary).
    *   Aztreonam: Ceftazidime shares considerable structural similarity with aztreonam, leading to a risk of in vivo cross-allergenicity; aztreonam should be avoided in patients with immediate hypersensitivity to ceftazidime.
    *   Synergistic combination with aminoglycosides (such as ceftazidime plus an aminoglycoside for Pseudomonas aeruginosa meningitis).
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** Generally considered safe for use in pregnancy. Useful in pregnancy for treating urinary tract infections and gonorrhea when first-line penicillin agents are contraindicated.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Expanded Gram-negative coverage; ceftriaxone meningitis penetration; ceftazidime Pseudomonas activity; ceftriaxone gonorrhea drug of choice; mixed clearance; oral cefixime; aztreonam cross-allergenicity risk.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   While third-generation cephalosporins generally feature highly expanded Gram-negative coverage, ceftazidime is the sole agent of the class with useful activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, yet it concurrently lacks significant Gram-positive activity.
    *   Although cephalosporins generally require strict dosage adjustment in renal impairment, ceftriaxone possesses mixed renal and biliary clearance (with a prolonged half-life of 6-8 hours) and therefore requires zero dosage adjustment in patients with renal failure.
    *   While aztreonam is highly useful for treating patients with severe penicillin/cephalosporin allergies because it lacks cross-allergenicity, it shares considerable structural similarity with ceftazidime, creating a unique exception where cross-allergenicity can occur in ceftazidime-allergic individuals.
    *   Third-generation cephalosporins should be strictly avoided when treating Enterobacter infections, even if the isolate appears fully susceptible on in vitro testing, due to the high risk of rapid resistance emergence (via derepression of chromosomal AmpC beta-lactamases) during therapy.
