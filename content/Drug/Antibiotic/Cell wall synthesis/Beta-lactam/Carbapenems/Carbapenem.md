### Carbapenem
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:** Beta-lactam antibiotics; Carbapenem class.
*   **Target Organisms:** Broad spectrum of aerobic and anaerobic Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:** Inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to and inactivating penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), which are cell wall transpeptidases. This blocks the transpeptidation reaction that cross-links peptidoglycan polymer chains, thereby weakening the peptidoglycan cell wall lattice and causing osmotic instability, cell membrane damage, lysis, and rapid cell death.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:** Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) / cell wall transpeptidases.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):** Rapidly bactericidal.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:** Carbapenem resistance is mediated by hydrolysis via carbapenemases or metallo-beta-lactamases (such as KPC-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae strains). Methicillin-resistant staphylococci are resistant due to the acquisition of a high-molecular-weight PBP (PBP2a) with low affinity for beta-lactams. Resistance can also emerge rapidly in Pseudomonas aeruginosa during monotherapy. Anaerobes like Stenotrophomonas maltophilia are intrinsically resistant.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:** Carbapenems are not orally active and must be administered parenterally (intravenously or intramuscularly). Oral bioavailability is N/A.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:** Widely distributed into most tissues and body fluids. They penetrate well into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the presence of inflamed meninges. Meropenem features excellent CSF penetration and is specifically dosed at higher ranges for meningitis. Ertapenem is highly protein-bound (approximately 95%).
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:** Imipenem is rapidly degraded and inactivated by a dehydropeptidase-I enzyme in the renal tubules, resulting in low urinary concentrations; therefore, it is formulated and co-administered with cilastatin, a renal dehydropeptidase inhibitor. Meropenem, doripenem, and ertapenem are stable to renal dehydropeptidase-I and do not require co-administration with cilastatin. Hepatic metabolism and cytochrome P450 interactions are N/A.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:** Eliminated primarily unchanged by renal glomerular filtration. The elimination half-life of imipenem, meropenem, and doripenem is approximately 1 to 1.5 hours. Ertapenem has a significantly longer half-life (approximately 4 hours), which allows for once-daily dosing.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:** Clearance is renal and is directly proportional to creatinine clearance. Dosages must be adjusted in patients with renal insufficiency or renal failure. Imipenem is typically dosed at 0.25 to 0.5 g every 6 to 8 hours. Meropenem is dosed at 1 g every 8 hours (increased to 2 g every 8 hours for meningitis). Doripenem is dosed at 500 mg every 8 hours. Ertapenem is dosed at 1 g once daily (every 24 hours).

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:** Indicated for serious, complicated infections caused by susceptible multidrug-resistant organisms. Key indications include nosocomial pneumonia, sepsis, complicated intra-abdominal infections, mixed aerobic and anaerobic infections, and empirical therapy for febrile neutropenic patients (often in combination with an aminoglycoside). They are considered the treatment of choice for serious infections caused by extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae and are highly effective against AmpC beta-lactamase-producing Enterobacter infections.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:** Broadly active against streptococci (including penicillin-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae), Enterococcus faecalis (excluding Enterococcus faecium), staphylococci (methicillin-susceptible strains), Listeria, Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa (except ertapenem, which lacks useful activity), Acinetobacter (except ertapenem), and anaerobes (including Bacteroides fragilis). They are completely inactive against Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Enterococcus faecium, and Clostridium difficile.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:** N/A

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:** Local infusion-related reactions (pain and phlebitis at the injection site), nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, rash, and drug fever.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:** Seizures (convulsions), particularly in patients with pre-existing central nervous system lesions, renal insufficiency, or when high daily doses of imipenem (>2 g/day) are administered. Meropenem has a lower risk of inducing seizures (0.5%) compared to imipenem (1.5%). Lactic acidosis can also occur with nucleoside analogs but is N/A for carbapenems.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:** Myoclonic activity or seizures associated with high-dose imipenem in the setting of renal impairment.
*   **Black Box Warnings:** N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:** Prior history of immediate, severe hypersensitivity reactions (anaphylaxis) to carbapenems or other beta-lactam antibiotics.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:** Use with caution in patients with a history of seizure disorders, central nervous system disease, or renal insufficiency. Imipenem should not be used as monotherapy for Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections due to the risk of resistance developing during treatment. Cross-reactivity with penicillins is low (less than 10%), but caution should still be exercised in penicillin-allergic individuals.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:** Imipenem must be combined with the dehydropeptidase inhibitor cilastatin. Co-administration of carbapenems with valproic acid can result in a significant decrease in valproic acid therapeutic levels, potentially precipitating seizures.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** N/A

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Broadest spectrum beta-lactams; unsaturated carbon-substituted five-member ring; thienamycin derivative; renal dehydropeptidase degradation; imipenem-cilastatin pairing; meropenem meningitis utility; ertapenem Pseudomonas hole; ESBL treatment of choice; KPC resistance.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   While most beta-lactams are structurally characterized by a sulfur atom in their thiazolidine ring, carbapenems feature an unsaturated five-member ring containing a carbon atom instead of the sulfur atom, which imparts high resistance to most common beta-lactamases.
    *   Unlike imipenem, which is rapidly degraded by renal dehydropeptidase-I and requires the inhibitor co-drug cilastatin, next-generation carbapenems (meropenem, doripenem, and ertapenem) are stable to renal dipeptidases and are administered alone.
    *   Unlike imipenem, meropenem, and doripenem, which have potent activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter, ertapenem is completely inactive against these two pathogens and is restricted to once-daily dosing due to its long half-life.
