### Natural Penicillins
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:**
    - Beta-lactam class of antibacterial agents. The basic chemical structure consists of a thiazolidine ring connected to a four-membered beta-lactam ring that carries a secondary amino group. The structural integrity of the 6-aminopenicillanic acid nucleus (the thiazolidine and beta-lactam rings together) is absolutely essential for biological activity.
    - Subclass: Natural penicillins.
    - Class representatives include:
      - Penicillin G (benzylpenicillin): The only natural penicillin used clinically and the congener with the greatest antimicrobial activity. For penicillin G, the side chain is a phenyl-methyl substituent. It is formulated as crystalline sodium or potassium salts, or as repository forms.
      - Penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin): An acid-stable congener of penicillin G suitable for oral administration.
      - Repository forms of penicillin G designed for intramuscular injection: Procaine penicillin G and Benzathine penicillin G, which are repository salts that delay absorption to yield prolonged, low-level concentrations in the blood and tissues.
*   **Target Organisms:**
    - Highly active against susceptible Gram-positive cocci (including Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, viridans group streptococci, and penicillin-susceptible Streptococcus pneumoniae).
    - Gram-positive rods (including Listeria monocytogenes, Bacillus anthracis, Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae, and Actinomyces israelii).
    - Gram-negative cocci (including Neisseria meningitidis and susceptible Neisseria gonorrhoeae).
    - Non-beta-lactamase-producing anaerobes (including Clostridium species, Leptotrichia buccalis, oral spirochetes, Peptostreptococcus, and Peptococcus).
    - Spirochetes (exquisitely active against Treponema pallidum, Borrelia burgdorferi, and moderately active against Leptospira species).
    - Specific Gram-negative rods (including rat-bite fever pathogens Spirillum minor and Streptobacillus moniliformis, and Pasteurella multocida).
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    - Natural penicillins are bactericidal agents that prevent bacterial cell wall synthesis.
    - The bacterial cell wall peptidoglycan consists of long glycopeptide polymer chains made of alternating aminohexose backbones containing N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid residues. These linear polymers are cross-linked via peptide side-chains to form a polymeric murein lattice capable of resisting high internal osmotic pressure.
    - In Staphylococcus aureus, the cross-linking is catalyzed by a transpeptidase that links the terminal glycine residue of a pentaglycine bridge to the fourth residue of the pentapeptide (D-alanine), releasing the terminal D-alanine.
    - The chemical conformation of penicillin is stereochemically similar to the D-alanyl-D-alanine terminus of the peptidoglycan precursor.
    - Penicillins bind to and covalently acylate the transpeptidase enzyme, forming a stable penicilloyl-enzyme intermediate with cleavage of the beta-lactam ring's carbonyl-nitrogen bond.
    - This covalent blockade halts the transpeptidation reaction, preventing proper polymer cross-linking and weakening the cell wall murein lattice, which makes the bacterial cell highly susceptible to osmotic lysis.
    - The final bactericidal event is the inactivation of an endogenous inhibitor of autolytic enzymes (autolysins) in the cell wall, which triggers the autolysins to undergo uncontrolled self-digestion and cell lysis.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:**
    - Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), specifically bacterial cell wall transpeptidases and carboxypeptidases located outside the cell membrane of Gram-positive bacteria and within the periplasmic space of Gram-negative bacteria.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):**
    - Rapidly bactericidal against susceptible replicating microorganisms. They have no activity against non-replicating bacteria. They are only bacteriostatic against Enterococcus species when used as monotherapy. They are completely ineffective against amebae, plasmodia, rickettsiae, fungi, or viruses.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    - Resistance to natural penicillins occurs through four major mechanisms:
      1. Enzymatic inactivation of the drug by beta-lactamases (penicillinases): Cleavage of the reactive carbonyl-nitrogen bond of the beta-lactam ring by bacterial beta-lactamase enzymes yields penicilloic acid, which completely lacks antibacterial activity. Staphylococcus aureus produces a narrow-spectrum penicillinase. Enterobacter, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and other Enterobacteriaceae produce AmpC beta-lactamases or extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) that destroy natural penicillins.
      2. Alterations in target PBPs (reduced affinity): Modification or acquisition of novel penicillin-binding proteins. For example, methicillin-resistant staphylococci (MRSA and MRSE) express PBP2a, encoded by the mecA gene, which has extremely low affinity for all beta-lactams. Penicillin-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae also alters its PBPs.
      3. Impaired penetration: Reduced outer membrane permeability in Gram-negative bacteria (such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa) due to porin channel mutations or loss, preventing the drug from reaching the PBPs in the periplasmic space.
      4. Active efflux of the drug out of the cell.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:**
    - Oral absorption is highly variable and depends on stability in gastric acid and adsorption to food.
    - Penicillin G is highly acid-labile and destroyed by gastric secretions, making oral administration highly inefficient; it must be administered intravenously or intramuscularly.
    - Penicillin V is acid-stable and suitable for oral administration, but its absorption is significantly impaired by food; it should be given 1 to 2 hours before or after a meal to minimize binding to food proteins and acid inactivation.
    - Repository intramuscular formulations are designed to delay absorption, yielding prolonged low-level blood and tissue concentrations:
      - Benzathine penicillin G: A single intramuscular injection of 1.2 million units maintains clinically useful therapeutic serum levels above 0.02 mcg/mL for 10 days, and detectable levels above 0.003 mcg/mL persist for up to 3 weeks.
      - Procaine penicillin G: An intramuscular injection of 600,000 units yields peak concentrations of 1 to 2 mcg/mL and clinically useful levels for 12 to 24 hours.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:**
    - Widely distributed to most body organs, tissues, and fluids, including pleural, peritoneal, synovial, and joint fluids.
    - Protein binding: Benzylpenicillin is approximately 60% bound to plasma proteins.
    - Central Nervous System: Penetration into the central nervous system (CNS) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is extremely poor under normal conditions due to the blood-brain barrier. However, during active meningeal inflammation (bacterial meningitis), penetration increases significantly to therapeutic levels.
    - Lungs: Penicillins exhibit very poor penetration into lung epithelial lining fluid, achieving epithelial lining fluid-to-plasma concentration ratios of only 0.1 to 0.4:1.
    - Placenta: Penicillins readily cross the placenta and can be detected in fetal tissue and amniotic fluid.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    - Natural penicillins do not undergo significant hepatic metabolism and are not substrates, inhibitors, or inducers of the hepatic Cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzyme system. They do not exhibit CYP-mediated drug interactions.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    - Rapidly eliminated by the kidneys via both glomerular filtration (approximately 10%) and active tubular secretion (approximately 90%).
    - Probenecid competitively inhibits the active tubular secretion of weak acids like penicillins, which can be clinically exploited to double or triple penicillin blood levels and prolong the half-life.
    - Penicillin G has a very short plasma elimination half-life of approximately 30 to 40 minutes (about 0.5 hour) in adults with normal renal function.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - Renal Impairment: Significant dosage reductions or extensions of the dosing interval are required for natural penicillins in patients with severe renal insufficiency (creatinine clearance less than 50 mL/minute).
      - Penicillin G IV: Standard adult dose is 1 to 4 million units every 4 to 6 hours. Adjust dose to 50% to 75% of normal for moderate renal impairment (creatinine clearance approx 50 mL/minute), and to 25% of normal for severe renal failure (creatinine clearance approx 10 mL/minute).
      - Penicillin V: No dose adjustments listed for renal failure in the dosing tables.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    - Streptococcal Infections: Pharyngitis, cellulitis, erysipelas, pneumonia, arthritis, meningitis, and endocarditis caused by Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, or viridans group streptococci. Serious infections require 12 to 20 million units of penicillin G daily intravenously for 2 to 4 weeks (4 weeks for endocarditis).
    - Streptococcal Toxic Shock and Necrotizing Fasciitis: High-dose penicillin G is combined with clindamycin (to suppress toxin synthesis).
    - Meningococcal Infections: High-dose intravenous penicillin G is a first-line drug of choice for meningitis and septicemia caused by Neisseria meningitidis.
    - Syphilis: Drug of choice for all clinical stages of syphilis caused by Treponema pallidum. Intramuscular benzathine penicillin G is used for primary, secondary, and early latent syphilis. Neurosyphilis and cardiovascular syphilis are treated with high-dose intravenous penicillin G (12 to 24 million units daily, or 20 million units daily, for 10 to 14 days).
    - Actinomycosis: High-dose intravenous penicillin G (10 to 20 million units daily for 6 weeks) followed by oral penicillin V is the regimen of choice for pulmonary, cervicofacial, and abdominal actinomycosis.
    - Diphtheria Carrier State: Used to eradicate Corynebacterium diphtheriae pharyngeal carriage (with diphtheria antitoxin used to treat active clinical disease).
    - Anthrax: Treatment of infections caused by susceptible strains of Bacillus anthracis (using 12 to 20 million units daily).
    - Clostridial Infections: Penicillin G is the agent of choice for gas gangrene (caused by Clostridium perfringens) as an adjunct to surgical debridement and antitoxin, and for eradicating vegetative Clostridium tetani in tetanus.
    - Fusospirochetal Infections: Vincent's gingivostomatitis (trench mouth) caused by the synergistic action of oral spirochetes and Leptotrichia buccalis (treated with oral penicillin V, 500 mg every 6 hours for several days).
    - Rat-Bite Fever: First-line agent of choice for infections caused by Spirillum minor or Streptobacillus moniliformis (using 12 to 15 million units daily parenterally for 3 to 4 weeks).
    - Listeria monocytogenes Infections: Penicillin G is a first-line drug of choice (15 to 20 million units parenterally daily for at least 2 weeks, or 4 weeks if endocarditis is present; ampicillin ± gentamicin is also a preferred regimen).
    - Erysipeloid: Infections caused by Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae (uncomplicated cases respond to a single IM dose of 1.2 million units of benzathine penicillin G; endocarditis is treated with 12 to 20 million units of penicillin G daily for 4 to 6 weeks).
    - Pasteurella multocida Wound Infections: Uniformly susceptible to penicillin G following cat or dog bites.
    - Lyme Disease: Amoxicillin is preferred for early localized Borrelia burgdorferi disease, but severe or late-stage disease with neurological or cardiac manifestations is treated with up to 20 million units of intravenous penicillin G daily for 10 to 14 days.
    - Prophylaxis of Recurrent Rheumatic Fever: Long-term monthly intramuscular benzathine penicillin G (1.2 million units) or daily oral penicillin V is highly effective in preventing recurrent streptococcal pharyngitis in patients with a history of rheumatic heart disease.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:**
    - Susceptible: Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, viridans group streptococci, susceptible Streptococcus pneumoniae, Listeria monocytogenes, Bacillus anthracis, Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae, Actinomyces israelii, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Clostridium perfringens, Clostridium tetani, Peptostreptococcus, Peptococcus, Leptotrichia buccalis, Spirillum minor, Streptobacillus moniliformis, Treponema pallidum, Borrelia burgdorferi, Leptospira species, Neisseria meningitidis, non-beta-lactamase-producing Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Pasteurella multocida.
    - Resistant: Methicillin-resistant staphylococci (MRSA, MRSE), penicillin-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae, Enterococcus faecium, Bacteroides fragilis, Prevotella melaninogenica (some strains), Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia, rickettsiae, fungi, viruses, amebae, plasmodia.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    - Intravenous penicillin G is used off-label for the treatment of severe, early disseminated Lyme disease with neurologic (meningitis) or cardiac manifestations.
    - High-dose continuous intravenous infusion of penicillin G to maximize the time above the minimum inhibitory concentration (T > MIC) for serious endovascular infections.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:**
    - Local: Pain, local tenderness, induration, and sterile abscesses at the site of intramuscular injections (particularly with repository benzathine or procaine salts). Thrombophlebitis, venous irritation, and local pain at the site of intravenous infusions.
    - Dermatological: Maculopapular or urticarial skin rashes, pruritus, and drug fever.
    - Gastrointestinal: Nausea, vomiting, mild diarrhea, and abdominal distress (less common with parenteral formulations).
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    - Anaphylaxis: Immediate, IgE-mediated type I hypersensitivity reaction manifesting as bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, severe hypotension, cardiovascular collapse, and death (occurs in approximately 0.05% of treatment courses).
    - Severe Cutaneous Adverse Reactions (SCARs): Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, and exfoliative dermatitis.
    - Neurotoxicity: Convulsions, hyperreflexia, myoclonus, hallucinations, and seizures. This is a severe, concentration-dependent toxic effect caused by high doses of penicillin G (exceeding 20 million units daily) or drug accumulation in patients with severe renal failure.
    - Cation Overload (Hyperkalemia): Potassium penicillin G contains about 1.7 mEq of potassium per million units, which can cause severe, life-threatening hyperkalemia in patients with severe renal insufficiency.
    - Hematological: Platelet-mediated bleeding, impaired platelet aggregation, granulocytopenia, neutropenia, and Coombs-positive hemolytic anemia (typically occurring with prolonged high-dose therapy).
    - Pseudomembranous Colitis: Overgrowth of toxin-producing Clostridium difficile in the colon, causing copious watery diarrhea, fever, and abdominal pain.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:**
    - Jarisch-Herxheimer Reaction: An acute febrile reaction characterized by fever, chills, headache, myalgias, tachycardia, and transient exacerbation of skin lesions, occurring within 2 to 12 hours after starting penicillin G therapy for primary, secondary, or neurosyphilis, or leptospirosis. It is caused by the rapid destruction of spirochetes and subsequent release of endotoxin-like pyrogens into the bloodstream.

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    - Documented history of immediate IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reaction (anaphylaxis, angioedema, bronchospasm, or urticaria) to any penicillin or other beta-lactam class drug.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - History of mild delayed hypersensitivity skin rash (maculopapular) to penicillins: Cephalosporins can be administered with caution (cross-reactivity is less than 10%), but must be avoided if immediate IgE-mediated hypersensitivity was present. Aztreonam can be safely administered as it has no cross-allergenicity.
    - Preexisting renal failure or severe renal impairment (requires strict dosing adjustment and monitoring to prevent neurotoxicity and seizures).
    - Congestive heart failure, fluid retention, or severe renal disease (with respect to high-potassium or high-sodium penicillin G formulations to avoid cation overload).
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - Probenecid: Competitively inhibits the renal active tubular secretion of penicillins, which decreases their renal clearance, significantly elevates peak serum levels, and prolongs their half-lives.
    - Aminoglycosides (e.g., gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin): High concentrations of penicillins can physically bind and inactivate aminoglycosides in solution; they must never be mixed in the same intravenous bottle or administered through the same IV line. In vivo, they are highly synergistic and should be administered separately.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:**
    - Penicillins readily cross the placenta and enter fetal tissue and amniotic fluid.
    - They are considered highly safe for use in all trimesters of pregnancy. There is no documented risk of teratogenicity or fetal harm in humans. Penicillin G is the absolute first-line drug of choice for treating maternal syphilis to prevent congenital transmission to the fetus.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "Bedpans and the Oxford Police Force," referring to the historical milestone where the Oxford investigators under Florey, Chain, and Abraham grew crude Penicillium cultures in bedpans and recycled/purified penicillin from the urine of treated patients (including an Oxford policeman) to preserve their limited supply.
    - "Thiazolidine ring connected to a four-membered beta-lactam ring carrying a secondary amino group," describing the exact chemical structure of the penicillin nucleus.
    - "Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction," the classic, spirochete-lysis-induced febrile reaction seen during syphilis treatment.
    - "Probenecid boosting," highlighting the pharmacological manipulation of active renal tubular secretion.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - Although natural penicillins are rapidly bactericidal cell wall synthesis inhibitors, they are strictly bacteriostatic when used as single agents against Enterococcus species (e.g., E. faecalis), requiring synergistic combination with an aminoglycoside to achieve bactericidal activity.
    - While aztreonam is a beta-lactam (monobactam), it exhibits zero cross-allergenicity with penicillins, allowing it to be safely administered to patients with a history of penicillin-induced anaphylaxis.
    - While the vast majority of Gram-positive pathogens are susceptible to natural penicillins, the vast majority of clinical isolates of Staphylococcus aureus produce a highly effective penicillinase, rendering them completely resistant to penicillin G.
    - Although Listeria monocytogenes is a Gram-positive rod, it is completely resistant to all cephalosporins, but remains highly susceptible to penicillin G and ampicillin.
    - Bacteroides fragilis is a Gram-negative anaerobe but is highly resistant to natural penicillins due to its expression of a broad-spectrum cephalosporinase.
