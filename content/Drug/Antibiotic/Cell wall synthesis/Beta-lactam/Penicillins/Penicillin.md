### Penicillins
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:**
    - Beta-lactam class of antibacterial agents. The basic structure consists of a thiazolidine ring connected to a four-membered beta-lactam ring that carries a secondary amino group.
    - Subclasses are classified based on their spectra of antimicrobial activity:
      1. Natural Penicillins: Penicillin G (benzylpenicillin; the only natural penicillin used clinically) and Penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin). Penicillin G is formulated as sodium or potassium salts, as well as repository forms for intramuscular injection (procaine salts and benzathine salts).
      2. Antistaphylococcal Penicillins (penicillinase-resistant penicillins): Nafcillin, oxacillin, cloxacillin, and dicloxacillin (and methicillin, which is no longer used clinically due to high rates of nephrotoxicity).
      3. Extended-Spectrum Penicillins:
         - Aminopenicillins: Ampicillin and amoxicillin. Often combined with a beta-lactamase inhibitor (e.g., amoxicillin/potassium clavulanate, ampicillin/sulbactam).
         - Carboxypenicillins: Carbenicillin (discontinued in the US) and its indanyl ester carbenicillin indanyl, and ticarcillin (marketed only in combination with clavulanate in the US).
         - Ureidopenicillins: Piperacillin, mezlocillin (discontinued in the US), and azlocillin (discontinued in the US). Piperacillin is marketed in combination with tazobactam.
*   **Target Organisms:**
    - Natural Penicillins: Highly active against sensitive strains of Gram-positive cocci (including Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, viridans group streptococci, and penicillin-susceptible Streptococcus pneumoniae), Gram-positive rods (including Listeria monocytogenes, Bacillus anthracis, Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae, and Actinomyces israelii), Gram-negative cocci (including Neisseria meningitidis and susceptible Neisseria gonorrhoeae), and non-beta-lactamase-producing anaerobic microorganisms (including Clostridium species, Leptotrichia buccalis, and oral spirochetes). Exquisitely active against Treponema pallidum and Borrelia burgdorferi.
    - Antistaphylococcal Penicillins: Active against penicillinase-producing staphylococci (Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis) that are not methicillin-resistant.
    - Extended-Spectrum Penicillins: Retain the spectrum of natural penicillins with improved activity against Gram-negative rods, including Escherichia coli, Proteus mirabilis, Haemophilus influenzae, Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter jejuni, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (specifically piperacillin and ticarcillin).
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    - Penicillins are bactericidal agents that prevent bacterial cell wall synthesis.
    - The bacterial cell wall peptidoglycan consists of long glycopeptide polymer chains made of alternating aminohexose backbones (N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid) linked via bridges between amino acid side chains.
    - This cross-linking reaction is a transpeptidation that links the terminal glycine residue of the pentaglycine bridge to the fourth residue of the pentapeptide (D-alanine), releasing the terminal D-alanine.
    - The chemical conformation of penicillin is stereochemically similar to the D-alanyl-D-alanine terminus of the peptidoglycan precursor.
    - Penicillin binds to and acylates the transpeptidase enzyme, forming a stable penicilloyl-enzyme intermediate with cleavage of the beta-lactam ring's carbonyl-nitrogen bond.
    - This covalent blockade halts the transpeptidation reaction, preventing proper polymer cross-linking and weakening the cell wall murein lattice, which makes the cell susceptible to osmotic lysis.
    - The final bactericidal event is the inactivation of an endogenous inhibitor of autolytic enzymes in the cell wall, which triggers the autolysins to undergo uncontrolled self-digestion and cell lysis.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:**
    - Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), specifically bacterial cell wall transpeptidases and carboxypeptidases located outside the cell membrane of Gram-positive bacteria and within the periplasmic space of Gram-negative bacteria.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):**
    - Rapidly bactericidal against susceptible replicating microorganisms. They have no activity against non-replicating bacteria, and are completely ineffective against amebae, plasmodia, rickettsiae, fungi, or viruses. They are only bacteriostatic against Enterococcus species when used as monotherapy.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    - Resistance to penicillins is mediated by four major mechanisms:
      1. Enzymatic inactivation of the drug by beta-lactamases (the most common mechanism): Cleavage of the reactive carbonyl-nitrogen bond of the beta-lactam ring by bacterial beta-lactamase enzymes yields penicilloic acid, which completely lacks antibacterial activity. Staphylococcus aureus, Haemophilus influenzae, and Escherichia coli produce narrow-spectrum penicillinases. Enterobacter, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacteriaceae produce extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) or AmpC beta-lactamases that destroy both penicillins and cephalosporins.
      2. Alterations in target PBPs: Modification or acquisition of novel penicillin-binding proteins with drastically reduced binding affinity for beta-lactams. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis (MRSE) express a high-molecular-weight PBP (PBP2a) encoded by the mecA gene, which has very low affinity for all beta-lactam antibiotics.
      3. Impaired penetration of the drug to its target: Reduced outer membrane permeability in Gram-negative bacteria prevents the drug from reaching the PBPs within the periplasmic space.
      4. Antibiotic efflux: Active export of the drug out of the cell by multidrug efflux pumps.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:**
    - Oral absorption is highly variable and depends on stability in gastric acid and adsorption to food proteins.
    - Acid-stable oral formulations include penicillin V, dicloxacillin, and amoxicillin.
    - Absorption of oral penicillins (with the exception of amoxicillin) is significantly impaired by food; they must be administered 1 to 2 hours before or after a meal to minimize acid inactivation and binding to food proteins.
    - Amoxicillin is completely stable in gastric acid, is highly bioavailable, and can be administered without regard to meals.
    - Natural penicillin G is highly acid-labile and is destroyed by gastric secretions, making oral administration inefficient; it must be administered intravenously or intramuscularly.
    - Repository IM formulations (procaine and benzathine salts of penicillin G) are designed to delay absorption, yielding prolonged low-level blood and tissue concentrations. A single IM injection of 1.2 million units of benzathine penicillin G maintains clinically useful therapeutic serum levels above 0.02 mcg/mL for 10 days, and detectable levels above 0.003 mcg/mL persist for up to 3 weeks. Procaine penicillin G (600,000 units) yields peak concentrations of 1 to 2 mcg/mL and clinically useful levels for 12 to 24 hours.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:**
    - Widely distributed to most body organs, tissues, and fluids, including pleural, peritoneal, synovial, and joint fluids.
    - Penetration into the central nervous system (CNS) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is extremely poor under normal conditions due to the blood-brain barrier. However, during active meningeal inflammation (bacterial meningitis), penetration increases significantly to therapeutic levels.
    - Most penicillins exhibit very poor penetration into lung epithelial lining fluid, achieving epithelial lining fluid-to-plasma concentration ratios of only 0.1 to 0.4:1.
    - Antistaphylococcal penicillins are highly bound to plasma proteins (exceeding 90%). Penicillins readily cross the placenta and can be detected in fetal tissue and amniotic fluid.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    - Do not undergo significant hepatic metabolism by mammalian enzymes and are not substrates, inhibitors, or inducers of the Cytochrome P450 system. They do not exhibit CYP-mediated drug interactions.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    - Eliminated rapidly by the kidneys via both glomerular filtration and active renal tubular secretion.
    - Probenecid competitively inhibits the active tubular secretion of weak acids like penicillins, which can be clinically exploited to double or triple penicillin blood levels and prolong the half-life.
    - Penicillin G has a very short elimination half-life of approximately 30 to 40 minutes in adults with normal renal function.
    - Exceptions to renal clearance: Nafcillin, oxacillin, and cloxacillin undergo primary hepatic clearance via biliary excretion; their elimination half-lives are virtually unchanged by renal failure.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - Renal Impairment: Significant dosage reductions or extensions of the dosing interval are required for renal-cleared penicillins (penicillin G, penicillin V, ampicillin, amoxicillin, and piperacillin) in patients with severe renal insufficiency (creatinine clearance less than 50 mL/minute).
      - Penicillin G: Reduce dose to 50% to 75% for moderate renal failure, and to 25% for severe renal failure (creatinine clearance less than 10 mL/minute).
      - Amoxicillin: Reduce dose to 66% for moderate renal impairment, and to 33% for severe renal impairment.
    - Hepatic Impairment: Nafcillin, oxacillin, and cloxacillin do not require dosage adjustments in patients with renal failure but may require dosage reductions in severe hepatic failure or active liver disease.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    - Streptococcal Infections: Pharyngitis, cellulitis, erysipelas, pneumonia, arthritis, meningitis, and endocarditis caused by S. pyogenes or S. agalactiae.
    - Streptococcal Toxic Shock and Necrotizing Fasciitis: High-dose penicillin G is combined with clindamycin (to suppress toxin synthesis).
    - Meningococcal Infections: High-dose intravenous penicillin G is a first-line drug of choice for meningitis and septicemia caused by Neisseria meningitidis.
    - Syphilis: Drug of choice for all clinical stages of syphilis caused by Treponema pallidum. Intramuscular benzathine penicillin G is used for primary, secondary, and early latent syphilis. Neurosyphilis and cardiovascular syphilis are treated with high-dose intravenous penicillin G (20 million units daily for 10 days).
    - Actinomycosis: High-dose intravenous penicillin G (10 to 20 million units daily for 6 weeks) followed by oral penicillin V is the regimen of choice.
    - Diphtheria Carrier State: Used to eradicate Corynebacterium diphtheriae from the pharynx (specific antitoxin is the only treatment for active clinical diphtheria).
    - Anthrax: Treatment of infections caused by susceptible strains of Bacillus anthracis.
    - Clostridial Infections: Penicillin G is the agent of choice for gas gangrene (caused by Clostridium perfringens) as an adjunct to surgical debridement and antitoxin, and for eradicating vegetative Clostridium tetani in tetanus.
    - Fusospirochetal Infections: Trench mouth (gingivostomatitis) caused by the synergistic action of oral spirochetes and Leptotrichia buccalis.
    - Rat-Bite Fever: First-line agent of choice for infections caused by Spirillum minor or Streptobacillus moniliformis.
    - Listeria monocytogenes Infections: Sepsis, meningitis, or endocarditis (ampicillin is the drug of choice, often combined with an aminoglycoside in immunocompromised hosts).
    - Lyme Disease: Amoxicillin is a drug of choice for early localized Borrelia burgdorferi disease; severe or late-stage disease is treated with high-dose IV penicillin G or ceftriaxone.
    - Erysipeloid: Infections caused by Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae (uncomplicated cases respond to a single IM dose of benzathine penicillin G).
    - Pasteurella multocida Wound Infections: Uniformly susceptible to penicillin G and ampicillin after dog or cat bites.
    - Surgical Prophylaxis: Cefazolin is generally preferred, but broad-spectrum penicillin combinations (e.g., piperacillin/tazobactam) are used for polymicrobial abdominal or pelvic surgeries.
    - Endocarditis Prophylaxis: Oral amoxicillin is the standard drug of choice for at-risk patients undergoing dental procedures to prevent viridans streptococcal bacteremia.
    - Prophylaxis of Rheumatic Fever: Long-term monthly intramuscular benzathine penicillin G or daily oral penicillin V is highly effective in preventing recurrent streptococcal pharyngitis in patients with a history of rheumatic heart disease.
    - Urinary Tract Infections: Uncomplicated UTIs caused by susceptible Enterobacteriaceae (E. coli, P. mirabilis).
    - Serious Pseudomonas aeruginosa Infections: Piperacillin or ticarcillin is combined with an aminoglycoside or a beta-lactamase inhibitor.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:**
    - Susceptible: Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, viridans group streptococci, susceptible Streptococcus pneumoniae, Listeria monocytogenes, Bacillus anthracis, Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae, Actinomyces israelii, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Clostridium perfringens, Clostridium tetani, Peptostreptococcus, Peptococcus, Leptotrichia buccalis, Spirillum minor, Streptobacillus moniliformis, Treponema pallidum, Borrelia burgdorferi, Leptospira species, Neisseria meningitidis, non-beta-lactamase-producing Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Pasteurella multocida, Escherichia coli, Proteus mirabilis, Haemophilus influenzae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Citrobacter, Enterobacter, Serratia, Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter jejuni.
    - Resistant: Methicillin-resistant staphylococci (MRSA, MRSE), penicillin-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae, Enterococcus faecium, Bacteroides fragilis, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia, rickettsiae, fungi, viruses, amebae, plasmodia.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    - Intravenous penicillin G or ampicillin is used off-label for the treatment of severe, early disseminated Lyme disease with neurologic or cardiac manifestations.
    - High-dose continuous intravenous infusion of penicillin G to maximize the time above the minimum inhibitory concentration (T > MIC) for serious endovascular infections.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:**
    - Gastrointestinal: Nausea, vomiting, mild diarrhea, and abdominal distress (more pronounced with oral formulations of ampicillin and amoxicillin).
    - Local: Pain, local tenderness, induration, and sterile abscesses at the site of intramuscular injections (particularly with repository benzathine or procaine salts). Thrombophlebitis, venous irritation, and local pain at the site of intravenous infusions.
    - Dermatological: Maculopapular or urticarial skin rashes, pruritus, and drug fever.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    - Anaphylaxis: Immediate, IgE-mediated type I hypersensitivity reaction manifesting as bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, severe hypotension, cardiovascular collapse, and death (occurs in approximately 0.05% of treatment courses).
    - Severe Cutaneous Adverse Reactions (SCARs): Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, and exfoliative dermatitis.
    - Neurotoxicity: Convulsions, hyperreflexia, myoclonus, hallucinations, and seizures. This is a severe, concentration-dependent toxic effect caused by high doses of penicillin G (exceeding 20 million units daily) or drug accumulation in patients with severe renal failure.
    - Renal: Acute interstitial nephritis (manifests as fever, eosinophilia, hematuria, and renal insufficiency; classically associated with methicillin, but can occur with any penicillin congener).
    - Cation Overload: Potassium penicillin G contains about 1.7 mEq of potassium per million units, which can cause severe, life-threatening hyperkalemia in patients with severe renal insufficiency. Carbenicillin and ticarcillin are disodium salts containing high sodium loads (approximately 5 mEq of sodium per gram), which can precipitate fluid overload, pulmonary edema, or congestive heart failure.
    - Hematological: Platelet-mediated bleeding, impaired platelet aggregation, granulocytopenia, neutropenia, and Coombs-positive hemolytic anemia (classically occurring with prolonged high-dose therapy).
    - Pseudomembranous Colitis: Overgrowth of toxin-producing Clostridium difficile in the colon, causing copious watery diarrhea, fever, and abdominal pain.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:**
    - Jarisch-Herxheimer Reaction: An acute febrile reaction characterized by fever, chills, headache, myalgias, tachycardia, and transient exacerbation of skin lesions, occurring within 2 to 12 hours after starting penicillin therapy for primary, secondary, or neurosyphilis, or leptospirosis. It is caused by the rapid destruction of spirochetes and subsequent release of endotoxin-like pyrogens into the bloodstream.
    - Ampicillin Rash in Infectious Mononucleosis: Virtually 95% to 100% of patients with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infectious mononucleosis develop a characteristic, non-allergic, maculopapular, intensely pruritic skin rash when given ampicillin or amoxicillin.

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    - Documented history of immediate IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reaction (anaphylaxis, angioedema, bronchospasm, or urticaria) to any penicillin or other beta-lactam class drug.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - History of mild delayed hypersensitivity skin rash (maculopapular) to penicillins: Cephalosporins can be administered with caution (cross-reactivity is less than 10%), but must be avoided if immediate IgE-mediated hypersensitivity was present. Aztreonam can be safely administered as it has no cross-allergenicity.
    - Epstein-Barr virus infectious mononucleosis (due to the extremely high risk of inducing a severe, non-allergic ampicillin rash).
    - Preexisting renal failure or severe renal impairment (requires strict dosing adjustment and monitoring to prevent neurotoxicity and seizures).
    - Congestive heart failure, fluid retention, or severe renal disease (with respect to high-sodium or high-potassium penicillin formulations).
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - Probenecid: Competitively inhibits the renal active tubular secretion of penicillins, which decreases their renal clearance, significantly elevates peak serum levels, and prolongs their half-lives.
    - Aminoglycosides (e.g., gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin): High concentrations of penicillins (such as carbenicillin, ticarcillin, or piperacillin) can physically bind and inactivate aminoglycosides in solution; they must never be mixed in the same intravenous bottle or administered through the same IV line. In vivo, they are highly synergistic and should be administered separately.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:**
    - Penicillins readily cross the placenta and enter fetal tissue and amniotic fluid.
    - They are considered highly safe for use in all trimesters of pregnancy. There is no documented risk of teratogenicity or fetal harm in humans. Penicillin G is the absolute first-line drug of choice for treating maternal syphilis to prevent congenital transmission to the fetus.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "Bedpans and the Oxford Police Force," referring to the historical milestone where the Oxford investigators under Florey, Chain, and Abraham grew crude Penicillium cultures in bedpans and recycled/purified penicillin from the urine of treated patients (including an Oxford policeman) to preserve their limited supply.
    - "Thiazolidine ring connected to a four-membered beta-lactam ring carrying a secondary amino group," describing the exact chemical structure.
    - "Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction," the classic, spirochete-lysis-induced febrile reaction seen during syphilis treatment.
    - "PBP2a encoded by the mecA gene," describing the molecular basis of methicillin resistance in S. aureus.
    - "Probenecid boosting," highlighting the pharmacological manipulation of active renal tubular secretion.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - Although penicillins are rapidly bactericidal cell wall synthesis inhibitors, they are strictly bacteriostatic when used as single agents against Enterococcus species (e.g., E. faecalis), requiring synergistic combination with an aminoglycoside to achieve bactericidal activity.
    - While the vast majority of penicillins undergo rapid renal excretion via active tubular secretion and require strict dosage adjustments in renal failure, nafcillin, oxacillin, and cloxacillin are cleared primarily via biliary excretion (hepatic clearance) and require no dosage adjustments in the setting of renal insufficiency.
    - Although aztreonam is a beta-lactam (monobactam), it exhibits zero cross-allergenicity with penicillins (except ceftazidime), allowing it to be safely administered to patients with a history of penicillin-induced anaphylaxis.
    - While the vast majority of Gram-positive pathogens are susceptible to natural penicillins, the vast majority of clinical isolates of Staphylococcus aureus produce a highly effective penicillinase, rendering them completely resistant to penicillin G.
    - Although Listeria monocytogenes is a Gram-positive rod, it is completely resistant to all cephalosporins, but remains highly susceptible to ampicillin and penicillin G.
    - Bacteroides fragilis is a Gram-negative anaerobe but is highly resistant to natural penicillins due to its expression of a broad-spectrum cephalosporinase.
