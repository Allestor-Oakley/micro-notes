### Glycopeptides
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:**
    - Glycopeptides: Tricyclic glycopeptide subclass, which includes vancomycin and teicoplanin.
    - Lipoglycopeptides: Semisynthetic lipoglycopeptides derived from either vancomycin or teicoplanin, which include telavancin, dalbavancin, and oritavancin.
*   **Target Organisms:**
    - Active exclusively against Gram-positive bacteria. This includes aerobic and anaerobic Gram-positive cocci and bacilli such as Staphylococcus aureus (including methicillin-susceptible MSSA, methicillin-resistant MRSA, and vancomycin-intermediate/resistant strains), Staphylococcus epidermidis (including methicillin-resistant MRSE), Streptococcus species (including Streptococcus pneumoniae, Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, and viridans group streptococci), Enterococcus species (including Enterococcus faecalis and some strains of Enterococcus faecium), Bacillus species (including Bacillus anthracis), Corynebacterium species (diphtheroids), Actinomyces species, and Clostridium species (including Clostridium difficile and Clostridium perfringens).
    - Virtually all species of Gram-negative bacilli and mycobacteria are completely resistant.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    - Inhibition of Peptidoglycan Polymerization and Cross-Linking: Glycopeptides and lipoglycopeptides inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding with exceptionally high affinity and firmness to the D-alanyl-D-alanine (D-Ala-D-Ala) terminus of cell wall precursor units (such as UDP-N-acetylmuramyl-pentapeptide precursors attached to the lipid carrier bactoprenol). This binding sterically blocks the transglycosylase (or transglycosylation) reaction, preventing the addition of the disaccharide-pentapeptide monomer to the growing peptidoglycan polymer chain. It also sterically blocks transpeptidases from accessing the precursor, preventing the final transpeptidation (cross-linking) reaction. This weakens the cell wall murein lattice, making the bacterial cell susceptible to osmotic lysis.
    - Secondary Membrane Depolarization (Lipoglycopeptides): Telavancin, dalbavancin, and oritavancin contain lipophilic alkyl side-chains that allow them to anchor directly into the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane. This calcium-dependent insertion disrupts bacterial cell membrane potential (depolarization), increases membrane permeability, and leaks intracellular contents, leading to rapid cell death.
    - Secondary Inhibition of RNA Synthesis: Oritavancin also exhibits a secondary mechanism of action involving the direct inhibition of bacterial RNA synthesis.
    - Direct Cell Membrane Damage: Vancomycin also direct damages the bacterial cell membrane, which contributes to its overall antibacterial effect.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:**
    - The D-alanyl-D-alanine (D-Ala-D-Ala) terminus of nascent peptidoglycan pentapeptide precursors. By binding to the substrate itself, these drugs prevent transglycosylase and transpeptidase enzymes (penicillin-binding proteins) from executing their respective cell wall elongation and cross-linking reactions.
    - For telavancin, dalbavancin, and oritavancin: also target the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane bilayer (disrupting membrane potential and permeability).
    - For oritavancin: also targets bacterial RNA synthesis.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):**
    - Generally bactericidal against susceptible Gram-positive pathogens, though they exhibit slower killing kinetics than beta-lactam antibiotics.
    - Bacteriostatic against Enterococcus species, requiring combination with an aminoglycoside to achieve reliable bactericidal activity.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    - Intrinsic Resistance:
      - All Gram-negative bacteria are intrinsically resistant because their outer membrane acts as an impermeable barrier to the large molecular size of glycopeptide and lipoglycopeptide molecules, preventing access to the cell wall precursor units.
      - Certain Gram-positive genera (e.g., Lactobacillus, Leuconostoc, Pediococcus) are naturally resistant because they synthesize cell wall precursors that naturally terminate in D-alanine-D-lactate or D-alanine-D-serine instead of D-Ala-D-Ala, preventing drug binding.
    - Acquired Resistance:
      - Enterococcal Resistance (VRE): Major nosocomial threat, primarily in Enterococcus faecium and Enterococcus faecalis. Resistance is plasmid- or transposon-mediated (often located on conjugative plasmids carrying transposons like Tn1546), enabling rapid horizontal transmission. The main resistance mechanism (such as VanA-type resistance) involves a cluster of plasmid-encoded genes that synthesize enzymes modifying cell wall precursors. Specifically, the terminal D-alanine is substituted with D-lactate (forming D-alanyl-D-lactate), which reduces glycopeptide binding affinity by 1000-fold.
      - Staphylococcal Resistance:
        - Vancomycin-Intermediate S. aureus (VISA): Results from gradual alterations in cell wall structure and metabolism. Resistant strains synthesize a thickened, poorly cross-linked peptidoglycan layer containing excess D-Ala-D-Ala residues. This thickened wall acts as a physical decoy, trapping and binding vancomycin molecules at the outer periphery of the cell and preventing them from reaching the active synthesis sites on the cytoplasmic membrane.
        - Vancomycin-Resistant S. aureus (VRSA): Results from the horizontal transfer of the enterococcal VanA gene cluster from a co-infecting VRE strain to S. aureus, converting the terminal precursor to D-lactate.
      - Lipoglycopeptide Resistance: Dalbavancin is inactive against most vancomycin-resistant enterococcal strains. Oritavancin retains some in vitro activity against VanA VRE, but its clinical utility remains unestablished.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:**
    - Oral administration of vancomycin results in negligible, extremely poor absorption from the gastrointestinal tract, and the drug is excreted unchanged in the feces. Therefore, oral vancomycin is useful only for localized infections within the bowel lumen (such as Clostridium difficile colitis) and cannot be used for systemic infections.
    - For systemic infections, glycopeptides must be administered parenterally.
    - Vancomycin must be given intravenously (IV) and is not administered intramuscularly due to tissue irritation.
    - Teicoplanin is highly unique as it can be administered both intravenously (IV) and intramuscularly (IM).
    - Telavancin, dalbavancin, and oritavancin are administered strictly by intravenous (IV) infusion.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:**
    - Parenteral glycopeptides are widely distributed into most body tissues and fluids, achieving therapeutic levels in synovial, pleural, and peritoneal fluids, as well as in bone and joint tissues.
    - Penetration into the central nervous system (CNS) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is poor under normal conditions, but increases to therapeutic levels when the meninges are actively inflamed (bacterial meningitis).
    - Volume of distribution is large for the lipoglycopeptides due to extensive intracellular and tissue penetration.
    - Plasma protein binding: Vancomycin has moderate protein binding (approximately 50% or less). Tedizolid is 70% to 90% protein bound. Teicoplanin and lipoglycopeptides (dalbavancin, oritavancin) are highly bound to plasma proteins.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    - Vancomycin does not undergo hepatic metabolism and is excreted unchanged.
    - These drugs do not interact with or undergo biotransformation by the hepatic Cytochrome P450 enzyme system, and they do not inhibit or induce CYP450 enzymes.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    - Parenteral glycopeptides are eliminated primarily unchanged by the kidneys via glomerular filtration. Elimination clearance is directly proportional to creatinine clearance.
    - Plasma Elimination Half-Lives:
      - Vancomycin: Approximately 6 hours in patients with normal renal function, but prolongs to 6 to 10 days in functionally anephric or severe renal failure patients.
      - Teicoplanin: Long half-life of 45 to 70 hours, which permits once-daily dosing.
      - Telavancin: Half-life of approximately 8 hours, supporting once-daily dosing.
      - Dalbavancin: Extremely long half-life of greater than 10 days, permitting once-weekly or single-dose administration.
      - Oritavancin: Extremely long half-life of greater than 10 days, permitting once-weekly or single-dose administration.
    - Hemodialysis: Vancomycin is not efficiently cleared by conventional low-flux dialysis membranes, but a significant amount is removed during standard hemodialysis when utilizing high-flux membranes, requiring careful therapeutic drug monitoring.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - Renal Impairment: Strict dosage adjustments (either reducing the dose or extending the dosing interval) are mandatory for all systemically administered glycopeptides in the presence of renal insufficiency to prevent drug accumulation and severe toxicity.
      - Vancomycin: Adults with normal renal function receive a standard starting dose of 30 mg/kg/day in two or three divided doses. In renal impairment (such as a creatinine clearance of 10-50%), a loading dose of 15 mg/kg is recommended, followed by a reduced maintenance dose of 20 mg/kg/day in two doses, with subsequent adjustments guided by serum trough concentration measurements.
      - Telavancin: Requires dose reduction and careful monitoring in renal impairment.
      - Dalbavancin and Oritavancin: Require dose reductions or interval adjustments in patients with significant renal impairment.
    - Hepatic Impairment: N/A

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    - Severe Infections caused by Gram-positive Pathogens: Treatment of sepsis, bacteremia, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, and bone/joint infections caused by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) or methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis (MRSE).
    - Prosthetic Valve or Foreign Body Infections: Serious staphylococcal infections involving foreign devices (often combined with rifampin to prevent resistance).
    - Bacterial Meningitis: Empiric treatment of meningitis in pediatric and adult patients, administered intravenously in combination with a third-generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone or cefotaxime) to cover potentially penicillin-resistant strains of Streptococcus pneumoniae.
    - Clostridium difficile-Associated Diarrhea: Oral administration of vancomycin is a first-line treatment of choice for severe or mild-to-moderate C. difficile-associated colitis and pseudomembranous colitis.
    - Serious Infections in Penicillin-Allergic Patients: Alternative treatment of pneumonia, arthritis, or cellulitis in patients with a history of immediate hypersensitivity, rash, or anaphylaxis to beta-lactam antibiotics.
    - Surgical Prophylaxis: Prophylaxis in patients undergoing cardiothoracic or orthopedic surgeries involving foreign implants who have severe allergies to penicillins and cephalosporins, or in institutional settings with highly prevalent MRSA.
    - Telavancin Specific Indications: Approved for treating complicated skin and soft tissue infections (cSSTIs) and hospital-acquired/ventilator-associated bacterial pneumonia (HABP/VABP) caused by susceptible Gram-positive pathogens.
    - Dalbavancin and Oritavancin Specific Indications: Approved for treating acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections (ABSSSI) caused by susceptible Gram-positive pathogens.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:**
    - Susceptible Gram-positive pathogens: Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA, MRSA, VISA), Staphylococcus epidermidis (MRSE), Streptococcus pneumoniae (including penicillin-resistant strains), Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A), Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B), viridans group streptococci, Enterococcus faecalis (some strains), Enterococcus faecium (some strains), Bacillus anthracis, Corynebacterium species, Actinomyces species, Clostridium perfringens, and Clostridium difficile.
    - Resistant pathogens: All Gram-negative bacilli (including Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter), all Gram-negative cocci, and all mycobacteria are completely resistant. Most VanA-type enterococci are resistant to vancomycin, teicoplanin, and dalbavancin.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    - Empirical combination therapy: Combined with rifampin for life-threatening MRSA endocarditis or osteomyelitis, particularly in the presence of orthopedic or cardiac prosthetic devices.
    - Oritavancin is investigated for its potential in vitro activity against vancomycin-resistant enterococci, though its definitive clinical utility against VRE remains unproven.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:**
    - Local injection site reactions: Local pain, tissue irritation, inflammation, and phlebitis at the intravenous infusion site (minimized by diluting the drug and rotating infusion sites).
    - Gastrointestinal: Nausea, vomiting, abdominal discomfort, and diarrhea (primarily with oral vancomycin).
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    - Nephrotoxicity: Reversible, dose-dependent damage to the renal tubules, which manifests as rising serum creatinine or decreased GFR. The risk of renal injury is significantly enhanced with prolonged therapy, high trough levels, advanced age, or when co-administered with other nephrotoxic drugs (such as aminoglycosides).
    - Ototoxicity: Injury to the auditory and vestibular branches of cranial nerve VIII, presenting as high-frequency hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo, and permanent deafness. This is highly associated with elevated serum concentrations, prolonged therapy, or concurrent use of other ototoxic agents.
    - Red Man Syndrome (or Red Neck Syndrome): An infusion-rate-dependent, non-immunologic anaphylactoid reaction characterized by intense pruritus, flushing, and an erythematous rash on the face, neck, and upper torso, occasionally progressing to hypotension and tachycardia. This is caused by the rapid intravenous infusion of vancomycin, which directly triggers mast cell degranulation and histamine release.
    - Telavancin-Induced Nephrotoxicity and Mortality: Telavancin is associated with substantial nephrotoxicity and an increased risk of mortality compared to vancomycin in patients with preexisting moderate-to-severe renal impairment (creatinine clearance <= 50 mL/min) treated for hospital-acquired pneumonia.
    - Teratogenicity: Telavancin is potentially teratogenic and must be avoided during pregnancy.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:**
    - "Red Man Syndrome" induced specifically by the rapid intravenous infusion of vancomycin.
    - Auditory ototoxicity presenting initially as high-frequency hearing loss (outside conversational range) or tinnitus.
    - Telavancin-associated substantial nephrotoxicity and increased mortality in patients with moderate-to-severe renal impairment.
*   **Black Box Warnings:**
    - Telavancin: Issued a black box warning by the FDA due to substantial nephrotoxicity and an observed increased risk of mortality in patients with preexisting moderate-to-severe renal impairment (creatinine clearance <= 50 mL/min) treated for hospital-acquired/ventilator-associated bacterial pneumonia. It also carries warnings regarding its potential teratogenic risk, necessitating pregnancy testing prior to initiation.

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    - Documented history of immediate hypersensitivity, anaphylaxis, or severe allergic reactions to vancomycin, teicoplanin, or any other glycopeptide/lipoglycopeptide class drug.
    - Use of telavancin in pregnant women (due to potential teratogenic risks and fetal harm).
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - Preexisting renal impairment or renal insufficiency (requires strict dosing adjustment and therapeutic drug monitoring of serum trough levels).
    - Preexisting vestibular or auditory impairment (hearing loss or deafness).
    - Advanced age (heightened risk of subclinical renal insufficiency).
    - Concomitant use of other ototoxic or nephrotoxic medications.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - Aminoglycosides (e.g., tobramycin, gentamicin, amikacin, streptomycin): Concomitant administration significantly potentiates and enhances both nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity, increasing the risk of permanent deafness and acute kidney injury.
    - Other Nephrotoxic Agents (e.g., amphotericin B, cisplatin, cyclosporine, colistin): Concurrent use increases the risk and severity of renal impairment.
    - Inhalation Anesthetics: Co-administration with vancomycin can increase the risk of anesthetic-induced cardiovascular depression and infusion-related reactions (erythema, flushing, and anaphylactoid reactions) in pediatric patients.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:**
    - Vancomycin should be used during pregnancy only if the potential therapeutic benefits clearly outweigh the potential risks to the fetus.
    - Telavancin is absolutely contraindicated or must be strictly avoided in pregnant women due to its documented potential for teratogenicity and fetal harm. Pregnancy testing is mandatory in women of childbearing potential before initiating telavancin.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "Amycolatopsis orientalis" (formerly known as Streptococcus orientalis), the soil actinomycete from which vancomycin was first isolated.
    - "D-alanyl-D-alanine (D-Ala-D-Ala) substrate binding," highlighting that glycopeptides target the cell wall substrate itself rather than an enzyme.
    - "D-lactate precursor substitution," describing the molecular mechanism behind VanA-type vancomycin resistance.
    - "Red Man Syndrome," describing the histamine-mediated flushing associated with rapid drug infusion.
    - "High-flux membrane hemodialysis," indicating the specific dialysis membranes capable of clearing vancomycin in renal failure.
    - "Extremely long half-lives (>10 days)," highlighting the unique pharmacokinetics of next-generation lipoglycopeptides (dalbavancin and oritavancin) that allow for once-weekly dosing.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - Slower Killing Kinetics: While cell-wall active beta-lactam antibiotics are rapidly bactericidal against almost all susceptible pathogens, glycopeptides (vancomycin) exhibit slower killing kinetics and require several days to clear bacteremia.
    - Enterococcal Bacteriostasis: Although glycopeptides are bactericidal against most Gram-positive pathogens, they are strictly bacteriostatic against Enterococcus species and require the addition of an aminoglycoside to achieve bactericidal synergy.
    - Substrate-Binding Mechanism: Unlike beta-lactam antibiotics, which physically mimic D-Ala-D-Ala to bind and covalently inhibit the transpeptidase enzyme, glycopeptides do not bind to the enzyme; instead, they bind directly to the D-Ala-D-Ala substrate itself, sterically hindering the transglycosylase and transpeptidase enzymes from accessing it.
    - Gut-Localized Efficacy: Although glycopeptides have zero systemic absorption when given orally, oral vancomycin is highly effective for treating Clostridium difficile colitis because its lack of absorption keeps the drug concentrated entirely within the bowel lumen, exactly where the pathogen resides.
