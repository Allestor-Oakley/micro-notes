### Isoniazid
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:** Synthetic analog of pyridoxine; core first-line antimycobacterial agent.
*   **Target Organisms:** Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (including Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Mycobacterium bovis) and Mycobacterium kansasii.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:** Isoniazid is a pro-drug that enters mycobacterial bacilli via passive diffusion. Once inside, it is metabolically activated to its toxic form by KatG, a multifunctional mycobacterial catalase-peroxidase. KatG catalyzes the production of an isonicotinoyl radical that spontaneously reacts with mycobacterial NAD+ and NADP+ to form a dozen distinct adducts.
    1. One major adduct, a nicotinoyl-NAD isomer, binds covalently to and inhibits the activities of enoyl acyl carrier protein reductase (InhA) and beta-ketoacyl acyl carrier protein synthase (KasA). This blocks fatty acid synthase II (FASII), halting the biosynthesis of mycolic acids (essential structural components of the mycobacterial cell wall) and causing bacterial cell death.
    2. Another adduct, a nicotinoyl-NADP isomer, potently inhibits mycobacterial dihydrofolate reductase (with a Ki of less than 1 nM), thereby directly interfering with nucleic acid synthesis.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:** Targets mycobacterial enoyl-ACP reductase (encoded by inhA), beta-ketoacyl-ACP synthase (KasA), acyl carrier protein (AcpM), and dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR). Metabolic activation requires the mycobacterial catalase-peroxidase KatG.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):** Halts the growth of resting bacilli (bacteriostatic activity) but is rapidly bactericidal against actively growing and dividing mycobacteria.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:** Spontaneous resistance occurs at a rate of approximately 1 in 10^6 bacilli. Clinical resistance emerges rapidly if isoniazid is administered as monotherapy. Resistance is associated with several distinct genetic mutations:
    1. Overexpression of inhA (due to promoter mutations) or structural mutations within the inhA gene, which encode an NADH-dependent acyl carrier protein reductase. This confers low-level isoniazid resistance and cross-resistance to ethionamide.
    2. Mutation or deletion of the katG gene, which encodes the catalase-peroxidase required for pro-drug activation. KatG mutants express high-level isoniazid resistance and are typically not cross-resistant to ethionamide.
    3. Promoter mutations resulting in the overexpression of ahpC, a gene involved in protecting the cell from oxidative stress.
    4. Mutations in the kasA structural gene.
    5. Enhanced efflux pump activity, such as that mediated by the iniA gene (which encodes an efflux pump that confers multi-drug tolerance to both isoniazid and ethambutol).
    There is no cross-resistance between isoniazid and other first-line antimycobacterial agents (such as rifampin, pyrazinamide, or ethambutol).

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:** Readily and rapidly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract. Absorption is optimal under fasting conditions; peak plasma concentrations may be decreased by up to 50% when administered with a high-fat meal. Following a standard 300 mg oral dose (or 5 mg/kg in children), peak plasma concentrations (Cmax) of 3 to 5 mcg/mL are achieved within 1 to 2 hours. Both slow and fast acetylators reach Cmax at approximately 1 hour.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:** Diffuses rapidly and widely into all body fluids and tissues. It achieves excellent penetration into the central nervous system, with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) concentrations ranging from 20% to 100% of simultaneous serum concentrations. It also penetrates exceptionally well into caseous, necrotic tuberculous lesions. Isoniazid is taken up by host macrophages and is highly active against both intracellular and extracellular organisms. Plasma protein binding is low, ranging from 20% to 38%.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:** Metabolism is genetically determined and exhibits a multi-modal distribution based on host N-acetyltransferase type 2 (NAT2) polymorphisms. Isoniazid is N-acetylated in the liver to N-acetylisoniazid (the principal metabolite) using acetyl-CoA. This reaction is catalyzed by NAT2, encoded by various NAT2* alleles (the number of NAT2*4 alleles accounts for 88% of clearance variability). N-acetylisoniazid is either excreted renally or converted to acetylhydrazine. Acetylhydrazine can be oxidized to highly hepatotoxic intermediates by CYP2E1, or further acetylated by NAT2 to diacetylhydrazine (which is nontoxic). Patients are classified into three phenotypic clearance groups:
    1. Rapid Acetylators: Possess fast NAT2 alleles, clearing the drug with a half-life of less than 1.5 hours (mean of 1.2 hours). They rapidly clear acetylhydrazine via diacetylation, reducing hepatotoxicity risk, but may achieve subtherapeutic levels during intermittent or once-weekly dosing.
    2. Slow Acetylators: Possess defective NAT2 alleles, clearing the drug with a half-life of greater than 2 hours (mean of 3.3 hours). They achieve higher plasma concentrations and are at a significantly higher risk of adverse effects (hepatotoxicity and peripheral neuropathy) due to drug accumulation and slower clearance of acetylhydrazine.
    3. Intermediate Acetylators: Possess codominant fast and slow NAT2 alleles.
    CYP450 Dynamics: Isoniazid is a potent inhibitor of CYP2C19 and CYP3A, and a weak inhibitor of CYP2D6. Conversely, it is an inducer of CYP2E1.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:** Eliminated primarily by the kidneys. Metabolites (N-acetylisoniazid) and a small amount of unchanged active drug are excreted in the urine. The elimination half-life ranges from 1 hour (in rapid acetylators) to 3 hours (in slow acetylators). Approximately 73% of a dose is excreted within 24 hours. It is cleared from the systemic circulation by hemodialysis.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - Renal Impairment: No dosage adjustments are required in renal failure.
    - Hepatic Impairment: No clear guidelines exist for dosage adjustments in patients with severe pre-existing hepatic insufficiency; dosing should be carefully guided by monitoring serum concentrations if a reduction is contemplated.
    - Dialysis: Cleared by hemodialysis; supplemental dosing may be considered post-session.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    1. Active Tuberculosis: Used as a core first-line agent. It is administered as part of a four-drug initial intensive phase (isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol) for the first 2 months, followed by a 4-month continuation phase of dual therapy with isoniazid and rifampin.
    2. Latent Tuberculosis Infection (LTBI) Prophylaxis: Indicated as monotherapy to prevent reactivation of latent disease. The typical regimen is 300 mg daily (or 900 mg twice weekly under direct observation) for 9 months in children and 6 to 9 months in adults.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:** Strictly restricted to the genus Mycobacterium. It is highly active against Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Mycobacterium bovis, and moderately active against Mycobacterium kansasii. It displays poor activity against Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) and has zero activity against any other microbial genus.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:** N/A

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:** Asymptomatic minor elevations in serum liver aminotransferases (transaminases up to 3 to 4 times the upper limit of normal, occurring in 10% to 20% of patients), transient skin rashes, drug fever, and mild gastrointestinal discomfort.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    1. Clinical Hepatitis: Hepatocellular damage and hepatic necrosis presenting with loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, jaundice, and right upper quadrant pain. It occurs in approximately 1% of recipients and can be fatal if the drug is not discontinued immediately. The risk is strongly age-dependent: rare under age 20, 0.3% in those aged 21 to 35, 1.2% in those aged 36 to 50, and 2.3% in those aged 50 and older.
    2. Peripheral Neuropathy: Manifests as symmetrical numbness, tingling, and paresthesias of the extremities, occurring in 10% to 20% of patients given doses greater than 5 mg/kg/day (uncommon at standard 300 mg daily doses).
    3. Central Nervous System Toxicity: Memory loss, acute psychosis, delirium, myoclonus, ataxia, lowered seizure threshold, cerebral overexcitability, and convulsions/seizures.
    4. Hematologic Abnormalities: Provocation of pyridoxine deficiency anemia, and hemolytic anemia in patients with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency.
    5. Drug-Induced Systemic Lupus Erythematosus.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:** Symmetrical peripheral neuropathy caused by drug-promoted excretion and relative depletion of pyridoxine (vitamin B6). Isoniazid binds to pyridoxal 5'-phosphate to form isoniazid-pyridoxal hydrazones, depleting neuronal pyridoxal 5'-phosphate and blocking reactions requiring this co-factor, including the synthesis of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA. Asymptomatic minor transaminase elevations up to 3-4x normal in up to 20% of recipients.
*   **Black Box Warnings:** N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:** Pre-existing history of severe isoniazid-associated hypersensitivity or clinical isoniazid-induced hepatitis.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - Use with extreme caution in patients with active liver disease, pre-existing hepatic insufficiency, chronic alcohol dependence, during pregnancy, and the postpartum period (due to heightened hepatitis risks).
    - To prevent peripheral neuropathy, co-administration of pyridoxine (vitamin B6) at a dose of 10 to 50 mg/day (or 25 to 50 mg/day) is recommended for patients predisposed to neuropathy (including the malnourished, elderly, pregnant women, diabetic patients, HIV-infected individuals, alcoholic patients, and uremic patients).
    - Clinical monitoring is mandatory: if clinical symptoms of hepatitis or significant transaminase elevations occur, the drug must be stopped immediately and permanently.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - CYP450 Inhibition: By inhibiting CYP2C19 and CYP3A, isoniazid significantly increases the plasma concentrations and toxicities of phenytoin, carbamazepine, ethosuximide, diazepam, and other benzodiazepines.
    - CYP450 Induction: Induces CYP2E1, which can increase the hepatotoxic potential of acetaminophen.
    - Rifampin Interaction: Rifampin is a potent CYP enzyme inducer (specifically of CYP2E1). When co-administered with isoniazid, rifampin induces CYP2E1, which converts the acetylhydrazine metabolite of isoniazid into highly hepatotoxic intermediates, thereby significantly potentiating isoniazid-induced hepatotoxicity. Concurrently, the overall enzyme induction by rifampin can decrease the concentrations of co-administered antiepileptics.
    - Overdose Antidote: In acute isoniazid overdose, pyridoxine (vitamin B6) acts as a specific antidote to replenish GABA levels. It must be administered intravenously gram-for-gram with the ingested isoniazid (or at a dose of 70 mg/kg if the ingested quantity is unknown). Intravenous benzodiazepines are utilized for seizures.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** Isoniazid has been used safely during pregnancy, but there is a heightened risk of maternal hepatitis during pregnancy and the postpartum period. Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) supplementation must be given to all pregnant women receiving isoniazid to prevent fetal/maternal neuropathy.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** INH; Nydrazid; KatG pro-drug activation; mycolic acid synthesis inhibitor; InhA and KasA target; caseous lesion penetration; NAT2 acetylator phenotype (fast vs. slow); pyridoxine deficiency neuropathy; gram-for-gram pyridoxine antidote.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - Unlike broad-spectrum antimicrobials, isoniazid is an ultra-selective agent with zero activity against any non-mycobacterial genus.
    - Unlike drugs with uniform clearance profiles, isoniazid exhibits a distinct multi-modal elimination distribution strictly governed by host NAT2 genetic polymorphisms, directly dictating both therapeutic success and toxic susceptibility.
    - While most neurotoxic drugs require complex symptomatic management, isoniazid's major neurotoxicities (peripheral neuropathy and seizures) are caused by a direct chemical depletion of pyridoxal 5'-phosphate and are completely prevented or cured by simple pyridoxine supplementation, with overdose seizures treated with a unique gram-for-gram intravenous pyridoxine regimen.
