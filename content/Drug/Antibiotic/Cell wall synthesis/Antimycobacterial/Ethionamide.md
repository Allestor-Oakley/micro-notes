### Ethionamide
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:** Congener of thioisonicotinamide; second-line antimycobacterial agent.
*   **Target Organisms:** Mycobacterium tuberculosis and other nontuberculous mycobacteria.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:** Ethionamide is a pro-drug. It is converted within the mycobacterium by the enzyme EthA (an NADPH-specific, FAD-containing monooxygenase) to a sulfoxide, and then subsequently to 2-ethyl-4-aminopyridine. While these specific end products are not toxic to mycobacteria, a closely related and transient chemical intermediate formed during this activation process acts as the active antibiotic. This active intermediate inhibits mycobacterial growth by blocking the inhA gene product (enoyl-ACP reductase of fatty acid synthase II). The inhibition of enoyl-ACP reductase halts the biosynthesis of mycolic acids, leading to a consequent impairment of mycobacterial cell-wall synthesis and cell death.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:** Targets the inhA gene product, which is the enoyl-ACP reductase of fatty acid synthase II. It requires metabolic activation by the mycobacterial EthA monooxygenase.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):** It halts the growth of resting organisms (exhibiting bacteriostatic activity) but can kill dividing bacteria (bactericidal activity against actively dividing mycobacteria).
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:** Bacterial resistance occurs mainly via genetic changes in the EthA enzyme that activates ethionamide. Mutations are frequently encountered in etaR, a transcriptional repressor gene that controls EthA expression. Mutations in the inhA gene (either structural mutations or promoter mutations resulting in overexpression of inhA) lead to resistance to both ethionamide and isoniazid. Overexpression of inhA (but not kasA) confers resistance in Mycobacterium smegmatis, Mycobacterium bovis BCG, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Some low-level cross-resistance can occur between isoniazid and ethionamide. KatG mutants, which express high-level isoniazid resistance, are often not cross-resistant to ethionamide. Resistance to ethionamide as a single agent develops rapidly in vitro and in vivo.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:** Poorly water-soluble and available only for oral use. Oral bioavailability is high, approaching 100%. Following a single oral administration of 500 mg of ethionamide, a maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) of 1.4 mg/L is achieved in approximately 2 hours.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:** Widely and evenly distributed. The drug concentrations in the blood and various organs are approximately equal. It penetrates exceptionally well into the central nervous system, achieving cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) concentrations that are equal to those in the serum (100% CSF penetration).
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:** Extensively metabolized in the liver, with six distinct metabolites identified. Within mycobacteria, it undergoes metabolic activation via a nitro-reduction step mediated by the FAD-containing monooxygenase EthA.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:** Metabolites are eliminated in the urine, with less than 1% of the administered ethionamide excreted in its active, unchanged form. The elimination half-life is approximately 2 hours. Its pharmacokinetics are adequately explained by a one-compartment model with first-order absorption and elimination. It is cleared from the systemic circulation by hemodialysis.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:** Listed among drugs requiring dosage adjustments in the setting of renal impairment due to the clearance of accumulated metabolites. It is cleared during hemodialysis.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:** Indicated as a second-line antituberculosis agent for the treatment of active tuberculosis caused by strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis resistant to first-line agents. It must always be administered in combination with other active antimycobacterial drugs to prevent the rapid emergence of resistance.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:** Suppresses the multiplication of Mycobacterium tuberculosis at concentrations ranging from 0.6 to 2.5 mg/L (or 2.5 mcg/mL or less). A concentration of 10 mg/L or less will inhibit approximately 75% of photochromogenic mycobacteria (such as Mycobacterium kansasii, where it is utilized as an alternative agent). Scotochromogenic mycobacteria are more resistant to the drug.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:** N/A

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:** Severe gastric irritation, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and abdominal pain.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:** Hepatotoxicity (it is hepatotoxic) and neurological symptoms (including peripheral neuritis and other neurologic toxicity).
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:** Intense gastric irritation and gastrointestinal intolerance that frequently limit patient adherence.
*   **Black Box Warnings:** N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:** N/A
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:** Use with caution in patients with pre-existing hepatic insufficiency or liver disease due to its hepatotoxic potential. Gastrointestinal irritation and neurologic symptoms often make the theoretically desirable 1-g/day dosage poorly tolerated, typically limiting the tolerable daily dose to 500–750 mg/day. Dosing is typically initiated at 250 mg once daily and increased in 250 mg increments to the recommended dosage of 1 g/day (or 15 mg/kg/day), if tolerated.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:** Concomitant administration of pyridoxine (vitamin B6) may be utilized to help prevent or alleviate the neurological symptoms and peripheral neuritis associated with ethionamide therapy. It exhibits cross-resistance with isoniazid in strains carrying inhA mutations.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** N/A

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Congener of thioisonicotinamide; Trecator; EthA-activated pro-drug; inhA inhibitor; 100% CSF penetration; severe gastric irritation; second-line TB agent; cleared by hemodialysis.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike isoniazid, which is a pro-drug activated by the KatG catalase-peroxidase enzyme, ethionamide is activated by the EthA monooxygenase enzyme, even though both active drugs ultimately target the identical enoyl-ACP reductase (inhA) structural target to inhibit mycolic acid biosynthesis. Additionally, despite being a polar molecule, ethionamide penetrates the blood-brain barrier without restriction, achieving CSF concentrations that are 100% equal to those in the serum.
