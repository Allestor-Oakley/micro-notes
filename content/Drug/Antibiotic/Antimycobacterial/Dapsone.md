### Dapsone
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:**
    - Sulfone class of antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory agents. Dapsone (diamino-diphenylsulfone) is the class prototype.
*   **Target Organisms:**
    - Mycobacteria: Mycobacterium leprae, Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), and Mycobacterium kansasii.
    - Protozoan parasites: Plasmodium falciparum and Toxoplasma gondii.
    - Fungi: Pneumocystis jirovecii.
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:**
    - Dapsone is a structural analog of para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA). It acts by competitively inhibiting the enzyme dihydropteroate synthase in the folate pathway. This competitive blockade inhibits the synthesis of dihydropteroic acid, preventing the subsequent production of folic acid. Folic acid is a critical cofactor required for the synthesis of purines, pyrimidines, and nucleic acids, meaning its depletion blocks DNA synthesis and cell division in susceptible microorganisms.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:**
    - Dihydropteroate synthase (specifically folP1/folP2 in the folate biosynthesis pathway).
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):**
    - Bacteriostatic against Mycobacterium leprae (active at concentrations of 1 to 10 mg/L).
    - Fungistatic/static against Pneumocystis jirovecii (active at concentrations of 0.1 mg/L).
    - Parasitostatic/static against Toxoplasma gondii tachyzoites (active at an inhibitory concentration of 0.55 mg/L).
    - Blood schizonticide against Plasmodium falciparum (highly active with an IC50 of 0.006 to 0.013 mg/L).
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:**
    - Acquired resistance in Plasmodium falciparum, Pneumocystis jirovecii, and Mycobacterium leprae results primarily from point mutations in the chromosomal genes encoding the target enzyme dihydropteroate synthase, which decreases dapsone's binding affinity.
      - In Mycobacterium leprae: Point mutations are encountered at codons 53 and 55. Resistance can emerge readily in large populations of Mycobacterium leprae (such as in lepromatous leprosy), particularly if low doses are administered as monotherapy.
      - In Plasmodium falciparum: Point mutations occur at positions 436, 437, 540, 58, and 613.
      - In Pneumocystis jirovecii: Mutations are often amino acid substitutions at positions 55 and 57 of the dihydropteroate synthase enzyme.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:**
    - Rapidly and nearly completely absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract following oral administration.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:**
    - Widely distributed throughout body water and into all tissues and fluids, including the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).
    - Tends to be retained in the skin, muscle, liver, and kidneys.
    - Skin heavily infected with Mycobacterium leprae may contain drug concentrations several times higher than normal uninfected skin.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:**
    - Extensively metabolized in the liver, where a major portion of the drug undergoes acetylation.
    - Undergoes clinically significant enterohepatic recycling: the drug is excreted into the bile and subsequently reabsorbed in the intestine.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:**
    - Highly persistent with a long plasma elimination half-life of 24 to 48 hours (or 1 to 2 days).
    - Excreted slowly into the urine, with a portion excreted unchanged and the majority excreted as acetylated metabolites. Some drug persists in tissues (kidney, liver, skin, muscle) for much longer periods.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:**
    - Renal Impairment: Dose may have to be adjusted in patients with renal failure.
    - Hepatic Impairment: N/A

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:**
    - Leprosy (Hansen's disease): Administered as a core component of multidrug treatment (MDT) regimens to prevent resistance.
      - Paucibacillary Leprosy: The WHO regimen consists of oral rifampin (600 mg once monthly) and dapsone (100 mg once monthly) administered under direct supervision for 6 months, with daily unsupervised dapsone (100 mg/day) in between. In the U.S., the regimen is daily dapsone (100 mg) and daily rifampin (600 mg) for 6 months, followed by dapsone monotherapy for 3 to 5 years.
      - Multibacillary Leprosy: The WHO regimen consists of monthly supervised rifampin (600 mg), dapsone (100 mg), and clofazimine (300 mg), combined with daily unsupervised dapsone (100 mg) and clofazimine (50 mg) for 12 months. In the U.S., the regimen consists of daily dapsone, rifampin, and clofazimine for 3 years, followed by dapsone monotherapy for 10 years.
    - Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP): Approved for the treatment and prophylaxis of PCP in high-risk immunocompromised patients (such as those with AIDS, leukemia, or transplant recipients) who are intolerant of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.
    - Toxoplasmosis: Used for the prophylaxis and alternative treatment of Toxoplasma gondii infections (such as acute toxoplasmic encephalitis) in immunocompromised patients, typically in combination with pyrimethamine and folinic acid (leucovorin) or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.
    - Dermatitis herpetiformis: First-line standard therapy for this chronic blistering skin condition associated with celiac disease due to dapsone's potent anti-inflammatory properties.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:**
    - Highly active against: Mycobacterium leprae (MIC of 1 to 10 mg/L), atypical mycobacteria (such as Mycobacterium avium complex and Mycobacterium kansasii, where more than 90% of clinical isolates have an MIC of <=8 mg/L), Plasmodium falciparum (highly active even against sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine-resistant strains), Toxoplasma gondii tachyzoites, and Pneumocystis jirovecii.
    - Inactive / Resistant: It has little to no activity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis and most other aerobic or anaerobic bacteria.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:**
    - Malaria: Combined with chlorproguanil for the treatment of Plasmodium falciparum malaria.
    - Anti-inflammatory therapy: Used in the treatment of pemphigoid, linear IgA bullous disease, relapsing chondritis, and skin ulcers caused by the bite of the brown recluse spider.

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:**
    - Anorexia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal discomfort, headache, and fever.
    - Pruritus and various mild skin rashes.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:**
    - Hemolysis of red blood cells: Predictable, dose-related effect that can lead to frank hemolytic anemia, particularly in patients with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency.
    - Methemoglobinemia.
    - Peripheral neuropathy and motor/sensory deficits.
    - Psychosis and mental changes.
    - Severe allergic dermatitis and drug rashes.
    - Agranulocytosis, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia.
    - Reversal reactions (lepra reactions): Acute immunological inflammatory reactions (delayed-type hypersensitivity or erythema nodosum leprosum) triggered by the massive release of bacterial antigens during therapy.
    - Dapsone Hypersensitivity Syndrome (or sulfone syndrome): A potentially fatal syndrome resembling infectious mononucleosis that occurs occasionally within the first 6 weeks of therapy, characterized by fever, chills, lymphadenopathy, skin rashes, and hepatic involvement.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:**
    - Predictable dose-dependent methemoglobinemia and red blood cell hemolysis.
    - Dapsone hypersensitivity syndrome (infectious mononucleosis-like syndrome).
    - Immune-mediated lepra reactions (reversal reactions) triggered during the treatment of leprosy.
*   **Black Box Warnings:**
    - N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:**
    - Documented history of immediate hypersensitivity, severe allergic reactions, or anaphylaxis to dapsone, sulfones, or any component of the formulation.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:**
    - Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency (requires mandatory pre-treatment screening due to the high risk of severe, life-threatening hemolysis).
    - Preexisting severe anemia, bone marrow suppression, or active blood dyscrasias.
    - Impaired renal function (may require dosage adjustment).
    - Active liver disease or severe hepatic insufficiency.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:**
    - Clofazimine: Concomitant administration of dapsone may inhibit the anti-inflammatory effects of clofazimine.
    - Other neuropathic or myelosuppressive drugs: Avoid concurrent administration of other neuropathic drugs (such as stavudine, didanosine, or isoniazid) due to an increased risk of severe peripheral neuropathies, or myelosuppressive drugs (such as ganciclovir or ribavirin) due to additive hematological toxicity.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:**
    - Dapsone readily crosses the placenta. In pregnant women with leprosy, multidrug treatment (rifampin, clofazimine, and dapsone) should be continued because the clinical benefits of treating maternal leprosy far outweigh the potential risks to the developing fetus. However, for non-urgent indications (such as cosmetic or mild inflammatory conditions), therapy should be postponed until after pregnancy is complete.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "Competitive inhibitor of dihydropteroate synthase"
    - "Enterohepatic recycling of sulfones"
    - "Skin heavily infected with Mycobacterium leprae retains several times more drug"
    - "Dapsone hypersensitivity syndrome / mononucleosis-like syndrome"
    - "Dermatitis herpetiformis first-line treatment"
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - Non-Antimicrobial Utility: Although classified as an antimicrobial agent targeting folate synthesis in pathogens, dapsone is used clinically as a first-line drug in dermatology for non-infectious blistering diseases (like dermatitis herpetiformis, pemphigoid, and linear IgA bullous disease) solely because of its potent anti-inflammatory properties.
    - Spectrum Discrepancy: Despite having outstanding activity against several mycobacterial species (such as Mycobacterium leprae, MAC, and Mycobacterium kansasii), dapsone has extremely high MICs and is completely ineffective against Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
    - Tissue Persistence: Unlike most systemically administered water-soluble antibiotics that are cleared rapidly from the body, dapsone displays an extremely long half-life (24 to 48 hours) and accumulates heavily in tissues, allowing active drug to persist in the liver, kidney, skin, and muscle for several months after the discontinuation of a treatment course.
