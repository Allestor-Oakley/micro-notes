### Pyrazinamide
#### Classification & Mechanism of Action
*   **Drug Class & Subclass:** Synthetic pyrazine analog of nicotinamide; first-line antimycobacterial agent.
*   **Target Organisms:** Mycobacterium tuberculosis (specifically Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex).
*   **Exact Mechanism of Action:** Pyrazinamide is a pro-drug that requires activation by the bacteria under acidic conditions. It enters bacilli via passive diffusion. Once inside, it is deaminated by Mycobacterium tuberculosis nicotinamidase (or pyrazinamidase) to its active form, pyrazinoic acid. Pyrazinoic acid is then transported to the extracellular milieu by an efflux pump. In an acidic extracellular environment (such as at the edges of necrotic cavities where inflammatory cells produce lactic acid), a fraction of pyrazinoic acid is protonated to POAH (or HPOA), a more lipid-soluble form that easily enters the bacillus. As the pH of the extracellular medium declines toward the pKa of pyrazinoic acid (2.9), POAH formation and equilibration across membranes are progressively favored, which enhances microbial killing. Within the bacillus, the drug disrupts the cell membrane potential, cell membrane metabolism, and transport functions. Three proposed specific mechanisms of microbial kill include: 1) inhibition of fatty acid synthase type I (FASI), leading to interference with mycolic acid synthesis; 2) reduction of intracellular pH; and 3) disruption of membrane transport by HPOA.
*   **Target Structure / Enzyme:** Targets and is activated by mycobacterial pyrazinamidase (nicotinamidase), encoded by the pncA gene. Proposed targets within the cell include eukaryotic-like fatty acid synthase I (FASI), cell membrane metabolism, and transport functions.
*   **Activity Type (-cidal vs. -static):** Exhibits bacteriostatic activity against susceptible strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, but can be bactericidal against actively dividing organisms. It is also broadly categorized under bactericidal agents. It possesses a potent "sterilizing" effect against residual intracellular organisms residing in acidic environments that can cause relapse.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Gene Mutations:** Pyrazinamide-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis possess pyrazinamidase with a reduced affinity for pyrazinamide, which decreases the conversion of the pro-drug to active pyrazinoic acid. Single point mutations in the pncA gene are encountered in up to 70% of resistant clinical isolates. The mechanisms contributing to resistance in the remaining 30% of resistant isolates are unclear. Impaired uptake of pyrazinamide also contributes to resistance. Resistant strains exhibit no cross-resistance with isoniazid or other first-line antimycobacterial drugs.

#### Pharmacokinetics (ADME)
*   **Absorption & Bioavailability:** Rapidly and well-absorbed after oral administration under fasting conditions. It has an absorption constant of 3.56 h^-1.
*   **Distribution & Tissue Penetration:** Widely distributed throughout the tissues and body fluids, including excellent penetration into the cerebrospinal fluid (meninges) and lung tissue. It has a volume of distribution of 29.2 L (for adults) or 0.5 L/kg. It is taken up by macrophages and exerts activity within the acidic environment of lysosomes.
*   **Metabolism & CYP450 Dynamics:** Converted by mycobacterial enzymes to its active metabolite, pyrazinoic acid. Human metabolism is primarily hepatic, with a systemic clearance of 3.4 L/h. Human clearance is highly impacted by patient weight, which affects both AUC and half-life.
*   **Excretion & Half-Life:** Metabolites are cleared renally, primarily by glomerular filtration. The mean terminal plasma elimination half-life of pyrazinamide is approximately 9 hours.
*   **Dosage Adjustments:** Because metabolites are renally cleared, the dosing frequency must be reduced to three doses weekly in patients with renal insufficiency/impairment if the creatinine clearance is less than 30 mL/min.

#### Clinical Indications & Spectrum
*   **Primary Clinical Indications:** Indicated as a core first-line agent in combination therapy for the treatment of active tuberculosis caused by susceptible strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It is used during the initial intensive phase (the first 2 months) of "short-course" 6-month regimens to eradicate residual intracellular organisms and prevent relapse.
*   **Specific Pathogen Spectrum:** Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (including Mycobacterium tuberculosis typus humanus, Mycobacterium canettii, Mycobacterium africanum, Mycobacterium bovis, and Mycobacterium microti). It is inactive against other mycobacterial genera (e.g., completely inactive against Mycobacterium kansasii). In vitro activity is seen only at acidic pH; at pH 5.5, it inhibits tubercle bacilli at concentrations of approximately 20 mcg/mL, and at pH 5.8–5.95, 80% to 90% of clinical isolates have an MIC of <= 100 mg/L.
*   **Off-Label / Niche Uses:** N/A

#### Adverse Effects & Boxed Warnings
*   **Common Side Effects:** Hyperuricemia (occurs uniformly in nearly all patients due to inhibition of urate excretion, though not a reason to halt therapy if asymptomatic), nausea, vomiting, anorexia, arthralgias, malaise, drug fever, and photosensitivity.
*   **Severe / Life-Threatening Adverse Effects:** Hepatotoxicity (injury to the liver is the most serious side effect). Historically, with older high-dose regimens (40–50 mg/kg), hepatic disease occurred in approximately 15% of patients, with jaundice in 2-3% and rare deaths due to hepatic necrosis. Current lower-dose regimens (15–30 mg/kg) are much safer but still carry a risk of hepatotoxicity (hepatotoxic in 1–5% of patients). It can also cause acute episodes of gout secondary to hyperuricemia.
*   **Pathognomonic / Highly Specific Side Effects:** Uniform hyperuricemia due to inhibited renal excretion of urate.
*   **Black Box Warnings:** N/A

#### Contraindications & Drug Interactions
*   **Absolute Contraindications:** Contraindicated in patients with pre-existing significant hepatic dysfunction or liver failure.
*   **Relative Contraindications & Cautions:** Should be avoided or used with extreme caution in patients with hepatic dysfunction, and only when absolutely unavoidable. Must be used with caution in patients with a history of gout or hyperuricemia. Prior to and during administration, hepatic function studies must be performed at frequent intervals, and therapy must be stopped if significant hepatic damage is detected.
*   **Major Drug-Drug Interactions:** Co-administration with isoniazid and/or rifampin is highly synergistic and has led to a one-third reduction in the duration of anti-tuberculosis therapy and a two-thirds reduction in TB relapse. Pyrazinamide inhibits the excretion of urate, which may interfere with drugs used for gout.
*   **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** Not approved for use during pregnancy in the United States due to inadequate data on teratogenicity.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Pyrazinecarboxamide; PZA; relative of nicotinamide; pro-drug activated by pyrazinamidase (encoded by pncA); acidic environment activation; lysosomal sterilizing agent; short-course therapy enabler; uniform hyperuricemia.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike most antibacterial agents which are tested and active at physiological pH, pyrazinamide is completely inactive at a neutral pH and exhibits antimicrobial activity in vitro only under acidic conditions (specifically pH 5.5 to 5.95), targeting slow-growing bacilli in necrotic cavity edges or within acidic macrophage lysosomes.
