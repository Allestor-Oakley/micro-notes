### Soft ticks

#### Arthropod Anatomy & Physical Morphology
*   **Adult Physical Features & Size:** Adult argasid ticks are dorsoventrally flattened, oval or roundish in outline, measuring 8.0 mm to 13 mm in length. Body shape is broadly rounded anteriorly. The integument is soft, leathery, wrinkled, and covered with fine tubercles (mammillae), granulations, or ridge structures protruding above the cuticle surface. Soft ticks completely lack a hard chitinous dorsal scutum (shield) in all post-larval developmental stages (adults and nymphs). Inornate with subcircular depressions termed discs (muscle attachment sites) arranged in characteristic patterns dorsally and ventrally. The cuticle expands during blood feeding to accommodate ingested blood but does not grow.
*   **Mouthparts & Feeding Apparatus:** The mouthparts, collectively termed the capitulum, gnathosoma, or false head, are situated ventrally and are completely invisible from above (dorsal view) in nymphs and adults, positioned in an anterior ventral groove called the camerostome. Palps are 4-segmented and leg-like (not club-shaped as in [[hard ticks]]). Powerful cutting chelicerae possess smooth, non-denticulate sheaths with teeth at their tips. The elongate central hypostome possesses rows of backward-projecting, recurved denticles. Both chelicerae and hypostome penetrate host skin during blood feeding. Salivary glands secrete digestive enzymes, anticoagulants, and local anesthetics, but soft ticks generally do not form a prominent proteinaceous cement cone. Specialized coxal organs (coxal glands) located between the bases of the coxae of the first and second pairs of legs act as osmoregulatory structures to filter excess fluid and salts from ingested blood meals, excreting coxal fluid during or immediately after feeding. Female possesses Gené's organ in front of the mid-gut, extruded above the capitulum during oviposition to coat each egg with a protective waxy waterproofing substance.
*   **Egg / Nit Morphology & Attachment:** Eggs are small, spherical, dark brown or translucent structures. Females lay several small egg batches (typically 4 to 6 batches during their lifetime following separate blood meals), each batch containing 15 to 100 eggs (occasionally up to 300 to 500 eggs per batch, totaling hundreds to thousands over a lifetime). Eggs are coated with a protective waxy secretion from Gené's organ, enabling them to withstand desiccation, immersion in water, and adverse environmental conditions for months. Deposited off-host in dark resting places near host sleeping sites—such as cracks and crevices in walls, floorboards, and furniture of houses, mud huts, rodent burrows, bird nests, bat caves, and animal shelters—never attached as external nits to host hair or skin.
*   **Immature Stages:** Hemimetabolous lifecycle involving incomplete metamorphosis:
    *   *Larva:* Minute, 6-legged stage possessing 3 pairs of legs. In most soft tick species, larvae have the capitulum projecting anteriorly and visible dorsally (unlike nymphs and adults). Larvae are active and search for hosts, blood-feeding for 20 to 30 minutes (or several days in certain species) before dropping off to molt. Exception: Larvae of Ornithodoros moubata do not take blood meals; they remain inside eggshells after hatching and molt directly into first-instar nymphs.
    *   *Nymph:* Eight-legged stage possessing 4 pairs of legs, resembling adults but lacking a genital aperture. Soft ticks pass through multiple nymphal instars—typically 4 to 5 nymphal stages (ranging from 4 to 7 instars depending on species and blood meal availability). Each nymphal instar requires a separate blood meal before molting to the next stage.

#### Lifecycle, Habitat & Host Attachment
*   **Primary Host Range:** Multi-host / many-host parasites (usually feeding on approximately 6 or more separate individual hosts throughout their lifecycle). Primary natural hosts include wild mammals (rodents, insectivores, bats), birds (poultry, pigeons), livestock (pigs, sheep, goats, cattle, camels), and humans (accidental or opportunistic hosts). Host specificity is low.
*   **Developmental Stages:** Complete lifecycle consists of: Egg -> 6-Legged Larva (or larva retained in eggshell in O. moubata) -> 8-Legged Nymphs (4 to 7 instars) -> Adult (Male / Female). Duration from egg to adult is typically 6 to 12 months under favorable temperature and host availability. Adult soft ticks are extraordinarily long-lived, surviving 12 to 20 years in the laboratory (record up to 25 years). In the absence of suitable hosts, adults can survive complete starvation for up to 10 years.
*   **Attachment Mechanics & Burrowing Behavior:** Nidicolous lifestyle: soft ticks reside in the nests, burrows, caves, crevices, mud huts, village houses, animal shelters, and chicken sheds of their hosts. Mating occurs off-host away from vegetation (e.g., in soil or wall cracks). Soft ticks do not burrow into skin. They emerge at night (nocturnal feeders) to attack sleeping hosts, attach externally to exposed skin, feed rapidly (taking 20 to 35 minutes or 30 to 60 minutes for a complete blood meal; increasing body weight up to 12-fold), and then detach immediately and drop off to return to hidden crevices.
*   **Environmental Survival Off-Host:** High environmental resistance due to waxy egg coatings, leathery cuticle, and efficient coxal gland osmoregulation. Inhabit dark, dry or humid cracks, mud walls, thatch, floor dust, rodent burrows, bat caves, and bird nests. Capable of surviving years without feeding under adverse climatic conditions.

#### Pathogenesis & Vector Dynamics
*   **Direct Dermatologic Damage:** Puncture wound from chelicerae and denticulate hypostome causes focal tissue damage, localized pain, erythematous macules, indurated papules, and local hemorrhagic spots at the bite site.
*   **Hypersensitivity & Immune Response:** Bite sites elicit immediate or delayed hypersensitivity reactions, presenting with intense itching, skin rashes, local edema, and occasional systemic allergic symptoms (fever, vomiting, [[diarrhea]]).
*   **Pathogens Transmitted as a Vector:**
    *   *Spirochetes:* Borrelia duttonii and over 15 other Borrelia species causing tick-borne (endemic) relapsing fever (TBRF). Transmitted primarily by Ornithodoros species (e.g., Ornithodoros moubata species complex in sub-Saharan Africa; O. hermsi, O. turicata, O. parkeri in North America).
        *   *Pathogen Transmission Mechanism:* Spirochetes ingested with a blood meal multiply in the mid-gut, penetrate the stomach wall into the hemocoel within 24 hours, and invade nearly all tick tissues/organs within 3 days (salivary glands, coxal organs, ovaries). Nymphs transmit spirochetes primarily via infected saliva injected during bites. Adults transmit spirochetes via both infected saliva and coxal fluid (excreted from coxal organs between legs 1 and 2 during/after feeding), which penetrates bite wounds or intact skin.
        *   *Transovarial & Transstadial Transmission:* Spirochetes invade female ovaries, passing transovarially to eggs and emerging larvae. Passed transstadially across all nymphal instars to adults for 3 to 4 generations. Soft ticks serve as both vector and primary reservoir host.
    *   *Arboviruses:* Over 30 arboviruses isolated from soft ticks, including Alkhurma hemorrhagic fever virus (Flavivirus, transmitted by Ornithodoros savignyi in Saudi Arabia and Egypt, primarily in butchers and camel handlers).
    *   *Bacteria:* [[Coxiella burnetii]] (Q fever).
*   **Host Defense & Predisposing Factors:** Exposure occurs in infested mud huts, poorly constructed rural dwellings, sleeping on floors/mats, animal shelters, caves, and rustic mountain cabins inhabited by wild rodents.

#### Clinical Syndromes & Presentations
*   **Primary Infestation Presentation:**
    *   *Uncomplicated Soft Tick Bite:* Painful or pruritic erythematous papule or wheal with a central puncture mark, resolving after several days.
    *   *Tick-Borne Relapsing Fever (TBRF):* Abrupt onset of high fever, severe headache, shaking chills, myalgia, arthralgia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, and macular or petechial rash. Characterized by recurring febrile episodes (fever lasting 3 to 5 days, followed by afebrile period of 4 to 9 days, then relapse) driven by antigenic variation of Borrelia outer surface proteins.
    *   *Alkhurma Hemorrhagic Fever:* Fever, headache, joint pain, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, elevated liver enzymes, and hemorrhagic manifestations.
*   **Pathognomonic Lesions & Distribution Pattern:** Bites occur on exposed skin of sleeping individuals (limbs, trunk, neck, face). No permanent eschar like scrub typhus, but multiple bite marks may be present. TBRF features characteristic relapsing fever curves.
*   **Secondary Complications:** Secondary bacterial infection of bite sites from scratching. TBRF complications include severe [[myocarditis]], hepatitis, central nervous system involvement ([[meningitis]], facial palsy, deafness), iridocyclitis, spontaneous abortion in pregnant women, Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction following initial antibiotic treatment, and death in untreated cases.
*   **Laboratory & Dermoscopic Findings:** TBRF diagnosis: microscopic visualization of motile spirochetes (Borrelia) in darkfield microscopy or Giemsa/Wright-stained thick and thin peripheral blood smears prepared during febrile episodes. [[PCR]] amplification of Borrelia DNA or serological assays. Dermoscopy of bite site shows a central hemorrhagic punctum with surrounding erythema.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Geographic Distribution:** Worldwide in tropical, subtropical, and warm temperate regions (Americas, Africa, Europe, Asia, Middle East). Absent from Australia and New Zealand. Ornithodoros moubata complex is prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa. Ornithodoros hermsi, O. turicata, O. parkeri in Western United States, Mexico, and Central/South America.
*   **Transmission Route:** Transmission occurs through the bite (salivary injection) or coxal fluid contamination of bite wounds/intact skin during short nocturnal blood meals by infected larvae, nymphs, or adults. No direct person-to-person transmission.
*   **Institutional & Crowding Risks:** High risk in rural mud-plastered villages, native dwellings, overcrowded shelters with dirt/cracked floors, animal pens, bat caves, and rustic mountain cabins.
*   **Public Health & Outbreak Management:**
    *   Housing improvements: plastering walls, sealing cracks/crevices, replacing dirt floors, raising beds off floor level (sleeping on elevated beds significantly reduces tick contact).
    *   Residual chemical spraying of indoor floors, walls, cracks, and furniture with carbamates (carbaryl, propoxur), organophosphates (malathion), or synthetic pyrethroids (deltamethrin, cypermethrin, [[permethrin]]).

#### Advanced Diagnostics
*   **Direct Examination & Skin Scrapings:** Identification of soft tick adults, nymphs, or larvae using low-power microscopy. Key features: flattened leathery body without scutum, mammillated/granulated cuticle, ventral capitulum (in nymphs/adults), leg-like palps, non-denticulate cheliceral sheaths, presence of coxal organs between coxae I and II, and absence of festoons.
*   **Dermoscopy Features:** Dermoscopy of bite site demonstrates central puncture/hemorrhagic crust with surrounding inflammatory ring. Ticks are rarely seen attached on dermoscopy because they feed rapidly for 20 to 30 minutes and detach.
*   **Biopsy Findings:** Histopathological examination of bite site shows dermal hemorrhage, focal epidermal necrosis, edema, and a perivascular inflammatory infiltrate composed of neutrophils, lymphocytes, histiocytes, and eosinophils. Skin biopsy is not performed for TBRF spirochetes; blood smears or lymph/tissue aspirates are evaluated instead.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Eradication
*   **Topical Scabicides & Pediculicides:** Topical [[permethrin]] or scabicides are not used for soft ticks because ticks feed briefly and detach. [[Permethrin]] 0.5% clothing sprays or impregnation of bedding/nets function as contact insecticides/repellents.
*   **Oral Systemic Treatments:**
    *   *Tick-Borne Relapsing Fever (TBRF) Antibiotic Therapy:*
        *   [[Doxycycline]]: First-line agent (100 mg PO/IV twice daily for 7 to 10 days).
        *   [[Erythromycin]] or [[Azithromycin]]: Alternative first-line agents for young children (<8 years) and pregnant women ([[Erythromycin]] 500 mg PO four times daily for 7 to 10 days).
        *   [[Penicillin G]] / [[Ceftriaxone]]: Indicated for severe disease or cases with central nervous system involvement.
        *   *Jarisch-Herxheimer Reaction:* Acute systemic inflammatory reaction (fever, chills, hypotension, tachycardia, leukopenia) occurring within 1 to 4 hours after initial antibiotic administration due to rapid lysis of spirochetes; requires close clinical monitoring and supportive fluid resuscitation.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Re-application Schedules:** Prompt treatment of TBRF halts relapses. For tick bites, washing skin and applying antiseptic/topical corticosteroids relieves itching and prevents secondary infection.
*   **Environmental & Laundry Decontamination Protocols:** Personal protection: applying DEET (N,N-diethyl-m-toluamide), picaridin, or dibutyl phthalate repellents to skin/clothing. Sleeping under [[permethrin]]-treated bed nets elevated off the floor. Laundering bedding and clothing in hot water (>60°C). Indoor environmental residual insecticide spraying (deltamethrin, [[permethrin]], carbaryl, propoxur) targeting floor cracks, wall crevices, and furniture.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Absence of scutum", "Ventral capitulum / gnathosoma" (hidden from dorsal view in nymphs/adults), "Mammillated / granulate leathery cuticle", "Coxal organs / coxal fluid transmission", "Leg-like palps", "Many-host / multihost ticks", "Nidicolous habit", "Nocturnal rapid feeding" (20–35 minutes), "Long lifespan" (up to 12–20+ years, 10 years starvation survival), "Tick-borne relapsing fever" (Borrelia duttonii), "Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction", "African tampan tick" (Ornithodoros moubata).
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   Soft ticks (Argasidae) completely LACK a dorsal scutum (shield) in all developmental stages, unlike [[hard ticks]] (Ixodidae) which possess a scutum.
    *   Capitulum (mouthparts) is located ventrally and invisible from above in argasid nymphs and adults, BUT in argasid larvae, the capitulum projects anteriorly and IS visible dorsally (like [[hard ticks]]).
    *   Larvae of Ornithodoros moubata do NOT take blood meals; they remain inside eggshells after hatching and molt directly into first-instar nymphs.
    *   Soft ticks undergo 4 to 5 (up to 7) nymphal instars, requiring a blood meal before each molt, whereas [[hard ticks]] have only ONE nymphal stage.
    *   Female soft ticks feed rapidly (20 to 35 minutes), lay several small batches of eggs (15 to 100 eggs per batch), and survive for years; female [[hard ticks]] feed slowly (days to weeks), lay a single massive egg batch (thousands of eggs), and die.
    *   Spirochetes (Borrelia duttonii) are transmitted not only through saliva during biting, but also through infected coxal fluid excreted by coxal organs during feeding, which can penetrate intact skin or bite wounds.
