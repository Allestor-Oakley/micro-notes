### Horse flies

#### Arthropod Anatomy & Physical Morphology
*   **Adult Physical Features & Size:** Medium to very large, robust, heavily built true flies measuring 6–30 mm in length (wingspan up to 65 mm in large Tabanus species). Body colouration varies from dark brown or black to lighter reddish-brown, yellow, or greenish; the thorax and abdomen frequently feature stripes, patches, or triangular markings of contrasting colours. Head is large and broad, appearing semicircular or semilunar when viewed from above. Eyes: Conspicuous, large compound eyes marked in life with brilliant iridescent colours (greens, reds, purples, gold) arranged in bands, spots, or zigzags. Sexes are easily differentiated by eye posture: females are dichoptic (eyes separated on top of the head by a distinct space called the frons or dichoptic space); males are holoptic (eyes are so large that they touch each other on top of the head or are separated by a very narrow line). Antennae: Relatively small but stout, 3-segmented, with the third segment subdivided into 3 or 4 small divisions by annulations; antennal arista is completely absent (distinguishing Tabanidae from Muscidae, Glossinidae, Calliphoridae). Genus differentiation by antennae and wings:
    - Chrysops (deer flies / mango flies): Medium-sized (6–12 mm). Antennal segment 2 is long, slender, and cylindrical without dorsal projections; segment 3 has 4 subdivisions. Hind tibiae possess apical spurs. Wings held in an open scissor-like posture, marked with dark brown transverse bands.
    - Tabanus (horse flies / greenheads): Medium to very large (10–30 mm). Both antennal segments 2 and 3 bear small but distinct dorsal projections/teeth; segment 3 is curved upward with 4 subdivisions. Antennae are shorter and less conspicuous than Chrysops. Hind tibiae lack apical spurs. Wings held open in a scissor-like posture, usually clear or with minor dark spots.
    - Haematopota (clegs / stouts): Dark grey, medium-sized (8–25 mm). Antennae longer than Tabanus; segment 3 is straight, lacks a dorsal projection, and has only 3 subdivisions. Wings folded roof-like over the abdomen at rest; wings are dusty grey, speckled or mottled with greyish patches.
    - Thorax & Wings: Thorax is stout, bearing 1 pair of functional membranous fore-wings and 1 pair of knob-like halteres. Wing venation is characteristic, possessing 2 submarginal cells, 5 posterior cells, and a completely closed discal cell near the center of the wing. R4+R5 vein encloses the wing tip.
    - Abdomen: Broad, stout, and dorsoventrally flattened in unfed flies.
*   **Mouthparts & Feeding Apparatus:** Stout, blade-like / knife-like piercing-sucking mouthparts hanging downwards from the head (unlike the rigid, forward-projecting proboscis of tsetse flies or Stomoxys). Adapted for telmophagous / pool feeding (slicing and lapping). Female mouthparts consist of sharp, blade-like mandibles and maxillae (laciniae) that act like scissors to slice through host skin and rupture fine dermal blood capillaries, creating a bleeding dermal blood pool (haematoma). The labellum then laps up the pooled blood. Saliva contains potent anticoagulants and antihaemostatic enzymes that prevent blood clotting and facilitate blood uptake. Only female tabanids take blood meals (required for egg maturation); male tabanids lack functional cutting mandibles and feed exclusively on plant juices, nectar, and honeydew (some species have a long proboscis adapted for drinking from deep flowers).
*   **Egg / Nit Morphology & Attachment:** Eggs are small, elongated, cylindrical, cigar-shaped or banana-shaped, measuring 1–2.5 mm in length. Laid in dark, compact, multi-layered masses or clusters of 100 to over 1000 eggs glued onto overhanging aquatic vegetation, leaves, rocks, or sticks directly above moist soil, mud, or water edges. Initially white or cream-coloured when laid, turning dark grey or shiny black upon exposure to air. Eggs hatch in 5–14 days; newly hatched young larvae wriggle out of the egg mass and drop directly down into underlying mud, wet soil, or shallow water.
*   **Immature Stages:** Larvae: Cylindrical, legless (apodous), tapered and pointed at both ends. Creamy white, brown, or greenish, often with dark pigmented bands near segment borders. Head is small, black, sclerotized, and fully retractile into the thorax. Consists of 11–12 well-differentiated body segments. Diagnostic larval features include prominent raised tyre-like muscular rings encircling body segments, and 6 roundish protuberances called pseudopods (1 lateral pair + 2 ventral pairs) on abdominal segments 1–7 used for crawling through mud. The last abdominal segment features a short, retractile respiratory siphon dorsally and a pear-shaped sensory structure termed Graber's organ, which contains 15 or fewer black globular bodies (function unclear). Larvae live in mud, damp soil, rotting vegetation, edges of swamps, ditches, and shallow streams. Larvae of Tabanus and Haematopota are predaceous, carnivorous, or cannibalistic (feeding on earthworms, insect larvae, mollusks, injecting a paralyzing venom); Chrysops larvae are largely herbivorous or saprophagous in mud. Larvae undergo 6–13 moults over several months to a year (or longer). Pupa: Cylindrical, obtect pupa, dark brown, featuring thoracic spiracles and a caudal aster (group of 6 sclerotized terminal spines) on the last abdominal segment used for wriggling through damp soil/mud to drier ground prior to adult emergence. Pupal stage lasts 1–3 weeks.

#### Lifecycle, Habitat & Host Attachment
*   **Primary Host Range:** Domestic and wild mammals (cattle, horses, deer, pigs, dogs, sheep, goats, rodents, wild game) and humans. Chrysops silaceus and C. dimidiatus regularly attack humans as primary hosts in rainforest clearings.
*   **Developmental Stages:** Complete metamorphosis (holometabolous): Egg -> Larva (6–13 instars) -> Pupa -> Adult. Total lifecycle duration ranges from 3–4 months in warm tropical climates to 1–3 years in cold temperate zones (overwintering occurs in the larval stage in damp soil or mud). Adult lifespan is typically 2–6 weeks.
*   **Attachment Mechanics & Burrowing Behavior:** Free-living flyers; adults do not burrow or permanently attach to host tissue. Female lands on exposed skin or dark clothing, anchors with tarsal claws, slices the skin with blade-like mandibles, and feeds rapidly on pooled blood (taking 20–25 mg blood for Chrysops, up to 100–500 mg for large Tabanus). Because bites inflict sharp pain, flies are frequently swatted or disturbed during feeding, leading to interrupted/intermittent feeding on multiple host individuals (which significantly enhances their mechanical vector capacity).
*   **Environmental Survival Off-Host:** Larvae inhabit wet mud, damp soil, rotting vegetation, edges of swamps, ditches, streams, low-lying marshy scrub, and tropical rainforest floors. Adults are powerful flyers capable of flying several kilometers (2–10+ km). Adults are daytime feeders (diurnal) active in bright sunshine, warm weather, and high humidity; some species are crepuscular. Chrysops silaceus and C. dimidiatus rest in the high forest canopy and descend to feed when attracted by movement, dark colours, carbon dioxide, or wood smoke.

#### Pathogenesis & Vector Dynamics
*   **Direct Dermatologic Damage:** Mechanical slicing of host skin by blade-like mandibles causes deep, painful lacerations, focal tissue destruction, and persistent oozing of blood from the bite wound (due to salivary anticoagulants). Bites result in painful, swollen, erythematous papules or nodules, local induration, and intense itching. Multiple bites lead to significant blood loss and distress in humans and livestock.
*   **Hypersensitivity & Immune Response:** Injection of saliva containing antihaemostatic enzymes and allergenic proteins triggers immediate wheal-and-flare reactions, local edema, and delayed-type cell-mediated hypersensitivity. Repeated bites cause severe allergic dermatitis, systemic allergic reactions, generalized urticaria, or anaphylaxis in sensitized individuals.
*   **Pathogens Transmitted as a Vector:**
    - Loa loa (African eye worm): Biological vector (Chrysops silaceus, C. dimidiatus, C. distinctipennis, C. longicornis). Microfilariae ingested during daytime blood meals penetrate the fly gut, migrate to the fat body, undergo 2 moults into infective L3 larvae in 8–12 days, and then migrate to the labium/proboscis to infect humans during subsequent blood meals.
    - Francisella tularensis (Tularemia / Deer fly fever): Mechanical vector (Chrysops discalis, Tabanus spp.). Contaminated mouthparts transfer bacteria from infected rabbits, rodents, or horses to humans.
    - Bacillus anthracis (Anthrax): Mechanical vector (Tabanus, Chrysops, Haematopota) transferring spores or vegetative bacilli from infected animal carcasses or livestock to humans.
    - Trypanosoma evansi (Surra) & Trypanosoma vivax (Souma): Mechanical vector in livestock (Tabanus spp., Stomoxys).
    - Anaplasma marginale: Mechanical vector in cattle (Tabanus fusciostatus).
    - Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme disease agent): Possible mechanical vector (unconfirmed role).
*   **Host Defense & Predisposing Factors:** Outdoor occupational or recreational exposure in endemic areas (forest workers, rubber plantation workers, wood cutters, farmers, hunters, fishermen, travelers in Central/West African rainforests). Attraction factors: movement, dark shiny objects, dark clothing, carbon dioxide, body heat, and wood smoke (Chrysops silaceus and C. dimidiatus are uniquely attracted to wood fires in forest settlements).

#### Clinical Syndromes & Presentations
*   **Primary Infestation Presentation:**
    - Tabanid Bite Reaction: Immediately painful, bleeding bite site followed by pruritic erythematous papules, localized edema, and wheals.
    - Loiasis (Loa loa infection):
        - Calabar Swellings (Fugitive swellings): Transient, painful, pruritic subcutaneous angioedema (nodular swellings) lasting several days, commonly on wrists, hands, forearms, or legs, caused by hypersensitivity to migrating adult worms or metabolic products.
        - Eye Worm: Visible, active subconjunctival migration of adult Loa loa across the eye, causing severe ocular irritation, pain, tearing, foreign body sensation, and eyelid edema.
        - Peripheral blood microfilaremia (diurnal periodicity, peaking during daytime hours matching Chrysops feeding habits).
    - Tularemia (Deer fly fever): Ulceroglandular presentation with a painful necrotic ulcer at the deer fly bite site, regional lymphadenopathy, high fever, chills, and headache.
*   **Pathognomonic Lesions & Distribution Pattern:**
    - Deep, persistent bleeding puncture/laceration on exposed skin or dark-clothed areas.
    - Loiasis: Transient non-pitting Calabar swellings on limbs; thread-like adult worm (30–70 mm) visible migrating beneath the bulbar conjunctiva.
*   **Secondary Complications:** Excoriation from scratching bite wounds leading to secondary bacterial pyoderma (Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes), impetigo, cellulitis, or abscess formation. High Loa loa microfilarial loads (>20,000 mf/mL) can lead to encephalopathy or renal failure if treated aggressively with diethylcarbamazine (DEC) or ivermectin.
*   **Laboratory & Dermoscopic Findings:**
    - Marked peripheral eosinophilia (50%–70%) and elevated serum IgE in loiasis.
    - Microscopic detection of sheathed Loa loa microfilariae (230–300 µm x 6–8 µm) in thick/thin Giemsa-stained daytime blood smears (nuclei extending continuously to the very tip of the tail; sheath does not stain with Giemsa).
    - Tularemia: Positive culture, PCR, or serology for Francisella tularensis.
    - Dermoscopy of bite site: Lacerated hemorrhagic lesion with central crust.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Geographic Distribution:** Worldwide (Cosmopolitan). Tabanus and Chrysops occur globally in temperate and tropical zones. Haematopota occurs in Europe, Asia, Africa, but is absent from South America and Australasia, and uncommon in North America. Loa loa vector Chrysops species are strictly localized to equatorial rainforests of West and Central Africa (Ghana, Nigeria, Cameroon, Equatorial Guinea, Gabon, Republic of Congo, DRC, Angola, South Sudan, Uganda).
*   **Transmission Route:**
    - Day-biting female flies attack humans outdoors (or indoors near wood fires for C. silaceus).
    - Biological transmission of Loa loa L3 larvae via bite wound puncture.
    - Mechanical transmission of bacterial/protozoal pathogens via mouthpart blood contamination during interrupted/intermittent feeding.
*   **Institutional & Crowding Risks:** High risk among outdoor agricultural, forestry, and rubber plantation workers, woodcutters, hunters, researchers, and rural village dwellers in African rainforest clearings.
*   **Public Health & Outbreak Management:** Personal protection against fly bites; vector control is notoriously difficult due to inaccessible breeding sites in vast forest mud/swamps. Mass drug administration (MDA) of ivermectin or DEC for loiasis in endemic populations (with pre-screening for high microfilarial burdens).

#### Advanced Diagnostics
*   **Direct Examination & Skin Scrapings:** Gross identification of adult tabanid flies captured off host/clothing. Microscopic mounting of fly specimens to examine antennal segment structure, hind tibial apical spurs, wing venation (discal cell, submarginal/posterior cells), and eye posture (dichoptic vs holoptic). Loa loa: Surgical/forceps extraction of active adult worm from subconjunctival space or subcutaneous tissue. Daytime blood film examination (10:00 AM – 2:00 PM) for sheathed microfilariae.
*   **Dermoscopy Features:** Hemorrhagic puncture mark with surrounding blanching or erythema and localized dermal edema.
*   **Biopsy Findings:** Histopathology of bite lesion demonstrates acute epidermal laceration, dermal hemorrhage, capillary thrombosis, and intense perivascular infiltrate containing abundant neutrophils, eosinophils, histiocytes, and lymphocytes. Histopathology of Calabar swelling reveals deep subcutaneous edematous tissue with chronic eosinophilic and lymphocytic granulomatous inflammation surrounding Loa loa metabolic products or adult worm tracks.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Eradication
*   **Topical Scabicides & Pediculicides:** N/A
*   **Oral Systemic Treatments:**
    - Oral antihistamines (cetirizine, diphenhydramine) for pruritus and wheals.
    - Treatment of Loa loa infection: Diethylcarbamazine (DEC, 6 mg/kg/day for 14–21 days) or Albendazole (400 mg twice daily for 21 days to reduce microfilarial load slowly). Caution: In patients with high microfilarial loads (>20,000 mf/mL), DEC or Ivermectin can trigger severe encephalopathy or fatal Mazzotti-like reactions; albendazole or plasmapheresis is preferred initially.
    - Antibiotics (flucloxacillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate) for secondary bacterial cellulitis.
    - Systemic antibiotics (streptomycin, gentamicin, or doxycycline) for Tularemia.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Re-application Schedules:** DEC kills both adult Loa loa worms and microfilariae; multiple courses may be required. Chemoprophylaxis for loiasis in long-term visitors: DEC 300 mg orally once weekly.
*   **Environmental & Laundry Decontamination Protocols:** Environmental vector control of Chrysops and Tabanus is generally impractical and ineffective due to widespread, inaccessible larval breeding sites (swamps, riverbanks, forest mud). Insecticide space spraying with pyrethroids provides temporary local suppression. Personal protection is key: wearing thick, light-coloured long-sleeved clothing and trousers (tabanids are attracted to dark colours and dark clothing), applying DEET-based or picaridin insect repellents to exposed skin and clothing, using fine-mesh window screens, avoiding wood smoke/fires in endemic forest clearings, and avoiding outdoor activity during peak daytime biting hours.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Pool feeder / Telmophagy" (blade-like slicing mouthparts), "Discal cell" (closed central wing cell), "Holoptic vs Dichoptic eyes" (male touching eyes vs female separated eyes), "Iridescent eyes" (green, red, purple bands and zigzags in life), "Hatchet-like or open scissor wing posture" (Chrysops vs Tabanus vs Haematopota roof-like wings), "Graber's organ" (larval caudal sensory structure with black globular bodies), "Caudal aster" (pupa terminal spines), "Calabar swellings / Fugitive swellings" (Loa loa angioedema), "Mango fly / Red fly" (Chrysops silaceus / C. dimidiatus), "Interrupted feeding" (frequent host switching enhancing mechanical vectoring), "Wood smoke attraction" (Chrysops silaceus attracted to wood fires), "Deer fly fever" (Tularemia transmitted by Chrysops discalis).
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - While most blood-sucking insects are repelled by wood smoke, African loiasis vectors Chrysops silaceus and C. dimidiatus are uniquely attracted to wood smoke from forest fires and village cooking hearths.
    - While most Diptera have aristae on their antennae, Tabanidae completely lack an antennal arista.
    - While male mosquitoes and midges have plumose antennae, male tabanids have non-plumose antennae and are distinguished from females primarily by their holoptic eyes.
    - While Haematopota is widespread in the Old World (Europe, Asia, Africa), it is completely absent from South America and Australasia, and uncommon in North America.
    - While Loa loa microfilariae circulate in blood during the day (diurnal periodicity), matching the day-biting Chrysops flies, they retreat to pulmonary capillaries at night.
    - While DEET is highly effective against mosquitoes and ticks, horse flies and deer flies are notoriously robust, requiring high concentrations of DEET or physical barrier clothing, and repellents are sometimes less effective against aggressive tabanid swarms.
