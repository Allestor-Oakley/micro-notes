### Tsetse flies

#### Arthropod Anatomy & Physical Morphology
*   **Adult Physical Features & Size:** Adult tsetse flies (family Glossinidae, genus *Glossina*) are robust, yellowish to dark brown-black flies measuring 6.0 mm to 15.0 mm in length (specifically 6.5 mm to 15.5 mm depending on group and species; approximately the size of a [[Houseflies|housefly]] or slightly larger). Abdominal segments may be uniformly colored or display lighter-colored transverse stripes with a distinct median longitudinal line. The body is divided into head, thorax, and an 8-segmented abdomen. Two diagnostic morphological features distinguish tsetse flies from all other Diptera:
    1.  *Scissored Wing Posture:* At rest, the wings are folded flat over the dorsum of the abdomen, resting one directly on top of the other like closed blades of a pair of scissors.
    2.  *Hatchet Cell:* The wing venation contains a closed, cleaver-shaped or axe-shaped cell situated between wing veins 4 and 5, universally known as the "hatchet cell" (or cleaver cell), which conclusively identifies a tsetse fly.
    Antennae are 3-segmented; the first two segments are small and inconspicuous, whereas the third segment is relatively large, cylindrical, and banana-shaped. Arising near the base of the third antennal segment is a specialized sensory arista bearing plumose (branched) hairs exclusively on its upper (dorsal) surface. Sexual dimorphism is absent in blood-feeding habits (both sexes bite and ingest blood). Morphologically, adult males possess a prominent raised, button-like ventral structure at the abdominal tip called the hypopygium, which folds forward and when unfolded reveals a pair of genital claspers. Adult females lack a button-like hypopygium; pregnant females containing a fully developed third-instar larva exhibit a distended, stretched abdomen through which the dark posterior larval respiratory structures are visible ventrally.
*   **Mouthparts & Feeding Apparatus:** The mouthparts consist of a rigid, needle-like proboscis that projects horizontally forward from the anterior of the head. The proboscis features a distinctive swollen, bulbous base (bulbous haustellum). A pair of long, narrow, hair-covered palps (pedipalps) arise dorsally at the base of the proboscis and lie closely alongside it, encasing and protecting it at rest. During feeding, the proboscis swings downward at a right angle to pierce the skin, while the palps remain projecting straight forward. The proboscis comprises an upper labrum, lower labium, and a central internal hypopharynx containing the salivary duct. Tsetse flies are pool/capillary feeders; muscular pharyngeal pumping draws blood up the labrum into the alimentary canal.
    *   *Alimentary Canal & Salivary Glands:* Blood passes down the esophagus, proventriculus, and crop (a large diverticulum/storage sac) before entering the midgut, which is lined by a chitinous peritrophic membrane (peritrophic matrix). The junction of the 4 Malpighian tubules marks the transition from midgut to hindgut. Paired, thread-like salivary glands originate in the head, are enormously long and convoluted, and extend backward through the thorax deep into the posterior abdomen. The salivary ducts unite in the head into a common salivary duct that traverses the hypopharynx, through which saliva containing potent anticoagulants and vasodilators is continuously pumped during feeding.
*   **Egg / Nit Morphology & Attachment:** Tsetse flies do not lay eggs in the environment (they are strictly non-oviparous). Tsetse flies reproduce via **adenotrophic viviparity** (larviparous reproduction). A female mates only once in her lifetime, storing viable spermatozoa in paired chitinous spermathecae. A single egg completes maturation in one of the two ovaries at a time, passes into the common oviduct, and enters the uterus, where it is fertilized. The egg measures ~1.5 mm, is translucent, and hatches inside the mother's uterus after 3 to 4 days into a first-instar larva. Eggs are never deposited externally on hosts, hair, skin, soil, or vegetation.
*   **Immature Stages:** Complete internal and external development:
    *   *Intrauterine Larval Instars:* The larva remains inside the female's uterus throughout its entire development, progressing through 3 larval instars over approximately 9 days. The uterus is supplied with a conspicuous pair of highly branched secretory uterine glands called **milk glands**. These glands secrete a nutrient-rich fluid ("uterine milk" composed of lipids and proteins) that the larva ingests through its mouth. A female must take regular blood meals every 2 to 3 days to maintain uterine milk production; if starved, the developing larva is aborted. The mature third-instar larva is 5.0 mm to 7.0 mm long, creamy white, composed of 12 visible segments, and bears a pair of prominent, dark, heavily sclerotized posterior respiratory protuberances called **polypneustic lobes**.
    *   *Larviposition:* The female gives birth to a single, fully grown third-instar larva at a time, deposited posterior-end first ("breech birth"). The female selects shaded, cool microclimates with loose, friable soil, sand, or humus under bushes, trees, fallen logs, rocks, tree buttress roots, riverbanks, or tree rot-holes (up to 4–5 meters above ground).
    *   *Puparium & Pupal Stage:* Immediately upon deposition, the active larva buries itself 2 cm to 5 cm deep into the soil (requiring ~15 minutes). Within 15 to 30 minutes, the third-instar larval skin contracts, hardens, and turns dark reddish-brown to black, forming a barrel-shaped **puparium** (5.0 mm to 8.0 mm long) that retains the distinctive paired posterior polypneustic lobes. Inside this puparial case, true pupation occurs. The pupal stage lasts 3 to 13 weeks (typically 3 to 4 weeks depending on ambient soil temperature).

#### Lifecycle, Habitat & Host Attachment
*   **Primary Host Range:** Obligate blood-feeders on terrestrial vertebrates. Primary hosts include domestic and wild suids (warthogs *Phacochoerus africanus*, domestic pigs, wild pigs), wild bovids (bushbuck, buffalo, hartebeest, kob, waterbuck), domestic cattle, sheep, goats, horses, reptiles (monitor lizards *Varanus*, crocodiles), birds, hippopotami, and humans.
*   **Developmental Stages:** Complete metamorphosis with intrauterine larval development: Fertilized Egg (in utero) -> 1st Instar Larva (in utero) -> 2nd Instar Larva (in utero) -> 3rd Instar Larva (in utero) -> Larviposition -> Puparium / Pupa (buried in soil) -> Adult. Adult lifespan averages 3 months (females live up to 3 to 4 months; males have shorter lifespans). A female produces a single larva every 9 to 10 days, yielding 8 to 10 (rarely up to 12) total offspring over her lifetime (~4 generations per year).
*   **Attachment Mechanics & Burrowing Behavior:** Tsetse flies do not burrow or remain permanently attached to host skin. Both adult males and females are aggressive, daytime-biting bloodsuckers that land on hosts, penetrate skin with the downward-swung proboscis, inject saliva, engorge on blood within 1 to 2 minutes, and fly off. Feeding periodicity is strictly diurnal, peaking during daylight hours between 18°C and 32°C (flies become inactive at temperatures above 36°C or during cold nights). Host location utilizes visual cues (attracted to movement, dark moving objects, and blue or black colors) and long-range olfactory cues (attracted to carbon dioxide, acetone, octanol, 1-octen-3-ol, 3-n-propylphenol, and 4-methylphenol present in host breath and urine). On pale-skinned humans, flies preferentially bite through dark clothing (such as socks, shorts, or trousers) rather than bare skin.
*   **Environmental Survival Off-Host:** Adult tsetse flies spend approximately 23 hours a day resting in dark, humid, shaded vegetation microclimates (twigs, branches, tree trunks, undersides of woody stems, and leaves below 4 meters in height; in West Africa, 50% rest between ground level and 30 cm). At night, flies rest on the upper surfaces of leaves. Flies reside within defined ecological geographical zones known as "fly belts". Adult flies and puparia cannot survive desiccation or ambient temperatures exceeding 36°C.

#### Pathogenesis & Vector Dynamics
*   **Direct Dermatologic Damage:** Puncture of host dermal capillaries by the proboscis and injection of salivary fluid cause immediate local tissue trauma, vascular dilation, focal edema, and pain. Within 1 to 2 weeks post-bite, an infective bite produces a **trypanosomal chancre**—a painful, indurated, red erythematous nodule or ulcerated lesion at the inoculation site, accompanied by localized lymphadenopathy, which heals spontaneously over 2 to 3 weeks.
*   **Hypersensitivity & Immune Response:** Bite sites elicit localized immediate and [[Type IV Hypersensitivity|delayed-type hypersensitivity]] reactions (pruritus, erythema, edema). Systemic pathology of transmitted pathogens is driven by parasite Variant Surface Glycoprotein (VSG) antigenic variation, triggering waves of parasitemia, massive polyclonal B-cell activation, immune complex deposition, vasculitis, tissue necrosis, severe anemia, and CNS neuroinflammation.
*   **Pathogens Transmitted as a Vector:** Tsetse flies are primary biological vectors and definitive hosts for hemoflagellate protozoa of the genus *Trypanosoma* (family Trypanosomatidae, subgenus *Trypanozoon*):
    *   *[[Trypanosoma brucei|Trypanosoma brucei gambiense]]:* Causative agent of Gambian / West African human African trypanosomiasis (chronic sleeping sickness). Transmitted principally by riverine tsetse flies of the **Palpalis group** (*Glossina palpalis palpalis*, *G. palpalis gambiensis*, *G. tachinoides*, *G. fuscipes fuscipes*, *G. fuscipes quanzensis*, *G. fuscipes martini*).
    *   *[[Trypanosoma brucei|Trypanosoma brucei rhodesiense]]:* Causative agent of Rhodesian / East African human African trypanosomiasis (acute fulminant sleeping sickness). Transmitted principally by savanna tsetse flies of the **Morsitans group** (*Glossina morsitans morsitans*, *G. morsitans centralis*, *G. pallidipes*, *G. swynnertoni*, and riverine *G. fuscipes fuscipes* around Lake Victoria).
    *   *[[Trypanosoma brucei|Trypanosoma brucei brucei]]:* Causative agent of Nagana (animal African trypanosomiasis) in cattle and domestic livestock. Non-pathogenic to humans because human serum apolipoprotein L1 (trypanosome lytic factor) lyses the parasite.
    *   *Other Animal Trypanosomes:* *Trypanosoma vivax*, *Trypanosoma congolense*, *Trypanosoma simiae* (causing severe Nagana in livestock and fatal disease in domestic pigs).
    *   *Developmental Cycle of [[Trypanosoma brucei]] in Glossina:*
        1. *Ingestion:* Unfed adult male or female tsetse fly ingests short, stumpy bloodstream trypomastigotes during a blood meal on an infected host.
        2. *Midgut Development:* Inside the fly's midgut/ectoperitrophic space, stumpy forms transform into long, slender procyclic trypomastigotes, multiplying by binary fission.
        3. *Proventricular Migration:* Parasites penetrate the anterior peritrophic membrane, pass through the proventriculus, ascend the esophagus and food channel of the proboscis, and enter the paired salivary glands via the hypopharynx and salivary ducts.
        4. *Salivary Gland Maturation:* Trypanosomes attach to salivary gland epithelial cells, transform into **epimastigotes**, multiply enormously, and differentiate into infective **metacyclic trypomastigotes** (metatrypanosomes) covered by a dense VSG coat.
        5. *Extrinsic Incubation Period (EIP):* The full developmental cycle in the fly takes 18 to 35 days (average 20 days; 12 to 30 days). Once infected, the tsetse fly remains contagious for life, injecting infective metacyclic trypomastigotes in its saliva during every subsequent blood meal.
        6. *Infection Rates:* Mature salivary gland infection rates in wild tsetse populations are extremely low, rarely exceeding 0.1% to 1% even in hyperendemic foci. Facultative sexual recombination of *T. brucei* occurs within the tsetse fly.
    *   *Mechanical Transmission:* Tsetse flies can also mechanically transmit *T. brucei*, *T. vivax*, and *T. evansi* via blood-contaminated mouthparts if interrupting a feeding session and immediately re-feeding on an adjacent host within a few hours.
*   **Host Defense & Predisposing Factors:** Risk is strictly tied to human-fly contact in tsetse-infested habitats: rural agricultural workers, fishermen, water collectors, laundry washers at riverbanks (*G. palpalis* group); hunters, poachers, honey gatherers, firewood collectors, game wardens, cattle herders, and tourists visiting game reserves (*G. morsitans* group). Human host immune defenses fail to clear *T. brucei* due to continuous VSG gene switching (antigenic variation among ~1,000 VSG pseudogenes).

#### Clinical Syndromes & Presentations
*   **Primary Infestation Presentation:** Painful tsetse bite followed by an acute, localized erythematous papule or indurated **trypanosomal chancre** (developing 5 to 15 days post-bite in 20% to 50% of non-endemic or East African cases), associated with local heat, edema, and regional lymphadenopathy.
*   **Pathognomonic Lesions & Distribution Pattern:** Primary bite lesions occur on exposed body areas (legs, arms, neck, head). Pathognomonic clinical features of transmitted Human African Trypanosomiasis (HAT):
    *   *Winterbottom's Sign:* Painless, soft, rubbery enlargement of the posterior cervical [[lymph nodes]], pathognomonic for Stage I *[[Trypanosoma brucei|Trypanosoma brucei gambiense]]* (West African) HAT.
    *   *Stage I (Hemolymphatic Stage):* Irregular, paroxysmal high fever, shaking chills, severe headache, generalized lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, pruritus, transient circinate rash, tachycardia, and edema.
    *   *Stage II (Meningoencephalitic / CNS Stage):* Trypanosomes breach the blood-brain barrier into the central nervous system and cerebrospinal fluid. Presents with profound sleep disturbances (daytime somnolence, nocturnal insomnia, inversion of sleep-wake cycle), apathy, fatigue, severe headache, tremors, ataxia, muscle weakness, speech impairment, logorrhea, hyperreflexia, behavioral alterations, psychosis, mania, confusion, seizures, progressive marasmus/wasting, coma, and death.
*   **Secondary Complications:** Secondary bacterial infection of bite sites. Systemic HAT complications include severe [[myocarditis]], pericardial effusion, congestive heart failure, cerebral edema, meningoencephalitis, malnutrition, endocrine dysfunction (amenorrhea, impotence), fetal abortion, and near 100% mortality if untreated.
*   **Laboratory & Dermoscopic Findings:**
    *   *Entomological Identification:* Demonstration of hatchet cell in wing venation, rigid forward-pointing proboscis with bulbous base, and arista with dorsal-only plumose hairs.
    *   *Parasitological Confirmation:* Microscopic visualization of motile trypomastigotes in wet mounts or Giemsa-stained smears of chancre aspirate, cervical lymph node aspirates (sensitivity 40%–80% for *gambiense*), or peripheral blood (thick/thin films, buffy coat microhematocrit centrifugation, mini-anion exchange chromatography technique [mAECT]). Parasitemia is high in *rhodesiense* and low/fluctuating in *gambiense*.
    *   *Serology:* Card Agglutination Test for Trypanosomiasis (CATT/T.b. gambiense) or rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) for screening.
    *   *Disease Staging:* Lumbar puncture with CSF examination; >5 WBC/µL or presence of trypanosotes in CSF indicates Stage II CNS disease.
    *   *Molecular Methods:* [[PCR]] or loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) detecting SRA gene (*T. b. rhodesiense*) or TgsGP gene (*T. b. gambiense*).

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Geographic Distribution:** Restricted strictly to sub-Saharan Africa between latitudes 14°N (Senegal to Somalia) and 20°S (fringes of Kalahari/Namibian deserts, extending to 30°S in eastern coastal South Africa), encompassing 36 countries over 10 million km² ("fly belts"). Two species occur in southwest Arabia.
    *   *Gambiense HAT (West & Central Africa):* Democratic Republic of the Congo (highest burden), Angola, Central African Republic, Guinea, Gabon, Congo, Cameroon, Chad, South Sudan, Uganda, Ivory Coast.
    *   *Rhodesiense HAT (East & Southern Africa):* Southeast Uganda, Tanzania, Malawi, Zambia, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Kenya.
*   **Transmission Route:** Biological transmission via the bite (salivary inoculation of metacyclic trypanosomes) of infected male or female tsetse flies during daytime feeding. Additional minor routes include congenital (transplacental) transmission, blood transfusion, accidental laboratory needle-stick inoculation, and sexual transmission.
*   **Institutional & Crowding Risks:** High risk in rural farming villages along river crossings, lakeshores, and water collection points (*G. palpalis* group); savanna woodland settlements, livestock markets, game reserves, and refugee camps (*G. morsitans* group).
*   **Public Health & Outbreak Management:** Integrated control combining active case detection/treatment with vector control:
    1.  *Active Case Finding & Mass Screening:* Mobile diagnostic teams screening rural populations using CATT or RDTs, followed by parasitological confirmation and stage-specific treatment to eliminate the human reservoir (critical for *T. b. gambiense*).
    2.  *Vector Suppression via Traps & Targets:* Deploying blue and black cloth traps (blue color attracts flies; black cloth induces settling/entry). Traps for *G. morsitans* are baited with synthetic mammalian odorants (acetone, 1-octen-3-ol, 3-n-propylphenol, 4-methylphenol, CO2, or cow urine).
    3.  *Tiny Targets:* Small insecticide-impregnated screens (50 cm x 25 cm) composed of blue cloth and black netting treated with synthetic pyrethroids (deltamethrin), positioned along riverbanks to decimate *G. palpalis* populations at low cost.
    4.  *Insecticide-Treated Livestock:* Applying pour-on pyrethroid formulations to the belly and legs of cattle ("live traps") in East Africa, simultaneously killing tsetse vectors and treating the animal reservoir for *T. b. rhodesiense*.
    5.  *Sterile Insect Technique (SIT):* Mass rearing, gamma-irradiation, and aerial release of sterile male tsetse flies following initial population suppression with traps or chemical sprays (successfully eradicated *Glossina austeni* from Unguja Island, Zanzibar).
    6.  *Sequential Aerosol Technique (SAT):* Ultra-low-volume (ULV) aerial spraying of low-dose pyrethroids along fly belts.

#### Advanced Diagnostics
*   **Direct Examination & Skin Scrapings:** Stereomicroscopic or hand lens examination of captured adult flies:
    *   *Wing Venation:* Verify presence of the closed, cleaver-shaped "hatchet cell" between veins 4 and 5.
    *   *Mouthparts & Head:* Confirm rigid, forward-projecting proboscis with a bulbous base, paired palps equal in length to proboscis, and 3rd antennal segment bearing an arista with plumose hairs strictly on its upper surface.
    *   *Abdomen:* Examine ventral abdominal tip for male hypopygium or female genital orifice; examine for presence of posterior polypneustic lobes in mature larvae or dark brown barrel-shaped puparia recovered from soil scrapings.
*   **Dermoscopy Features:** Dermoscopy (x10 to x20 magnification) of an acute tsetse bite or early chancre demonstrates a central hemorrhagic puncture mark surrounded by intense dermal erythema, induration, microvascular dilation, and localized edema.
*   **Biopsy Findings:** Histopathological examination (H&E stain) of a trypanosomal chancre demonstrates marked epidermal edema, focal necrosis, ulceration, and a dense, deep dermal and subcutaneous polymorphonuclear and lymphohistiocytic infiltrate containing abundant [[Plasma Cell & Memory B Cell|plasma cells]], [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]], and extravasated erythrocytes. Interstitial extracellular trypomastigotes may be visualized using Giemsa, Wright, or silver stains. Brain histopathology in Stage II HAT reveals diffuse meningoencephalitis, perivascular cuffing composed of lymphocytes and [[Plasma Cell & Memory B Cell|plasma cells]] (Mott cells / morular cells containing intracellular [[Antibodies, Immunoglobulins|immunoglobulins]]), microglial proliferation, demyelination, and cerebral edema.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Eradication
*   **Topical Scabicides & Pediculicides:** Topical scabicides or pediculicides are not used on human skin for tsetse fly bites. Personal protection relies on topical insect repellents containing DEET (N,N-diethyl-m-toluamide, 20% to 50%) or picaridin applied to exposed skin, though repellents are less effective against tsetse flies than against [[mosquitoes]]. [[Permethrin]] 0.5% spray applied to heavy clothing or uniforms provides additional protection.
*   **Oral Systemic Treatments:**
    *   *Symptomatic Bite Relief:* Local antiseptics and topical hydrocortisone 1% cream for local bite reactions.
    *   *Systemic Antitrypanosomal Chemotherapy for Transmitted HAT:*
        *   *Stage I T. b. gambiense (Hemolymphatic):* [[Fexinidazole]] (oral tablet; 1,800 mg once daily for 4 days, then 1,200 mg once daily for 6 days with food) OR [[Pentamidine]] isethionate (4 mg/kg IM daily for 7 days).
        *   *Stage II T. b. gambiense (CNS):* [[Fexinidazole]] (first-line for mild/moderate CNS disease) OR NECT ([[Nifurtimox]]-[[Eflornithine]] Combination Therapy: oral [[nifurtimox]] 15 mg/kg/day in 3 divided doses for 10 days PLUS IV [[eflornithine]] 400 mg/kg/day in 2 infusions for 7 days).
        *   *Stage I T. b. rhodesiense (Hemolymphatic):* [[Suramin]] sodium (IV slow infusion; 20 mg/kg, max 1 gram per dose, on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 21).
        *   *Stage II T. b. rhodesiense (CNS):* [[Melarsoprol]] (IV slow infusion; 2.2 mg/kg daily for 10 consecutive days). [[Melarsoprol]] causes acute reactive encephalopathy in 5% to 10% of treated patients (half of which are fatal); co-administration of oral prednisolone is recommended.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Re-application Schedules:** All HAT-treated patients require systematic post-treatment follow-up with clinical evaluation, blood examination, and lumbar puncture CSF checks every 6 months for 2 years to confirm cure and detect relapses.
*   **Environmental & Laundry Decontamination Protocols:** Wearing medium-weight, light-colored long-sleeved shirts and long trousers (tsetse flies are attracted to dark colors, especially blue and black, and easily bite through lightweight fabrics). Avoiding dense riverine bush, thickets, and known fly belts during peak daylight hours. Using vehicle window screening and insect traps around field camps. Vector resistance to insecticides has not been reported in wild Glossinidae populations.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Hatchet cell / Cleaver cell" (diagnostic wing venation), "Scissors wings posture" (resting wings folded flat over abdomen), "Arista with plumose hairs on upper side only", "Adenotrophic viviparity" (larviparous reproduction with intrauterine milk glands), "Polypneustic lobes" (paired dark posterior respiratory structures on larva and puparium), "Breech birth" (larva born posterior end first), "Barrel-shaped puparium" (buried in soil), "Hypopygium" (button-like male abdominal structure enclosing claspers), "Fly belts" (endemic geographic zones), "Trypanosomal chancre" (inoculation site ulcer), "Winterbottom's sign" (posterior cervical lymphadenopathy in Gambian HAT), "Tiny targets" (50 x 25 cm pyrethroid-treated blue/black screens), "Sterile Insect Technique (SIT)" (eradication on Zanzibar), "Mott cells / Morular cells" ([[Plasma Cell & Memory B Cell|plasma cells]] in CNS sleeping sickness).
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   Tsetse flies do NOT lay eggs in the environment; females undergo adenotrophic viviparity, giving birth to a single fully developed 3rd-instar larva at a time that buries itself in soil within minutes to form a puparium.
    *   Both male and female tsetse flies are obligate blood-feeders and both act as competent disease vectors (unlike [[mosquitoes]], sandflies, or tabanids where only females feed on blood).
    *   Arista hairs in *Glossina* are branched (plumose) strictly on the upper (dorsal) surface only, whereas aristae in other muscoid flies bear hairs on both upper and lower surfaces or lack branching.
    *   Tsetse flies have never established populations outside of the African continent (and small areas of Arabia) due to their unique, slow adenotrophic viviparous reproduction and low lifetime reproductive rate (~8 to 10 offspring per female).
    *   Salivary gland infection rates in wild tsetse populations are extremely low (<0.1% to 1%), yet a single infected fly remains contagious for its entire lifetime (~3 months) and transmits trypanosomes with every blood meal.
    *   Insecticide resistance has never been documented in wild Glossinidae species, making pyrethroid-impregnated "tiny targets" and traps highly effective.
