### Biting midges

#### Arthropod Anatomy & Physical Morphology
*   **Adult Physical Features & Size:** Adult biting midges (family Ceratopogonidae, primarily genera *Culicoides* and *Leptoconops*) are minute, slender, blood-sucking Diptera measuring 1.0 mm to 2.5 mm in length (ranging from 0.5 mm to 4.0 mm). Along with phlebotomine [[sand flies]], they represent the smallest biting flies attacking humans and animals. The body color is dark brown, grey, or black. The head possesses a prominent pair of large compound eyes, a pair of short, 5-segmented drooping maxillary palps, and a pair of relatively long, filamentous 15-segmented antennae. Sexual dimorphism is marked: males do not feed on blood and possess feathery or plumose antennae, whereas blood-sucking females have non-plumose antennae with short sparse hairs. The thorax in some species is covered dorsally with distinct small black spots and species-specific decorative patterns. A critical diagnostic morphological feature of the genus *Culicoides* is a pair of small, elongate, shiny black depressions situated dorsally on the anterior part of the thorax, termed **humeral pits** (the presence of humeral pits distinguishes *Culicoides* from *Leptoconops*). Wings are short, relatively broad, and held flat over the abdomen when at rest; the wing membrane is covered with fine microtrichia and frequently displays species-specific patterns of dark and pale spots or blotches. Wing venation is faint except for the anterior main veins. Wings are non-hairy on their surfaces (unlike phlebotomine [[sand flies]], which have densely hairy wings). The three pairs of legs are slender, lacking specialized scale paddlings or heavy spines.
*   **Mouthparts & Feeding Apparatus:** The biting mouthparts are very small, short, inconspicuous, and hang straight downward from the ventral surface of the head (not projecting forward like [[mosquitoes]]). The structure and arrangement of mouthparts resemble those of black flies (Simuliidae): short, broad, lancet-like stylets composed of the labrum, mandibles, maxillae, and hypopharynx. Teeth on the labrum stretch host skin, while the rasping/sawing action of the maxillae and mandibles cuts through epidermal tissue and ruptures superficial dermal capillaries. Biting midges are pool feeders (telmophages), drawing blood up from the small pool of lacerated capillary blood via the muscular pharyngeal pump into the digestive tract. Saliva containing potent anticoagulants, antihemostatic enzymes, vasodilators, and local irritant/allergenic proteins is injected into the wound during feeding. Because their mouthparts are extremely short, biting midges are unable to bite through clothing (unlike [[mosquitoes]], tabanids, or [[tsetse flies]]).
*   **Egg / Nit Morphology & Attachment:** Eggs are small, measuring 250 µm to 500 µm (0.25 mm to 0.5 mm) in length. They are dark, cylindrical, or curved and banana-shaped. Females deposit eggs in batches of 30 to 450 directly on moist or wet environmental substrates: mud, wet soil near freshwater or saltwater swamps and marshes, decaying leaf litter, humus, manure, semi-rotting vegetation, or partially submerged aquatic plants. Specific species select specialized oviposition sites; for example, African species such as *Culicoides milnei*, *Culicoides austeni*, and *Culicoides grahamii* lay eggs specifically in the rotting cut stumps of banana and plantain plants. Eggs hatch after 2 to 7 days depending on ambient temperature and species; some temperate species overwinter as eggs. Eggs cannot withstand complete desiccation.
*   **Immature Stages:** Complete metamorphosis (holometabolous lifecycle) with 4 larval instars:
    *   *Larvae:* Fully grown 4th-instar larvae are cylindrical, whitish, 3.0 mm to 6.0 mm long, and nematode-like / worm-like in appearance. The body consists of a small, dark conical head (unpigmented head in *Leptoconops*) followed by 12 smooth body segments. The body is completely devoid of legs, prolegs, or obvious lateral appendages. The last abdominal segment terminates in a pair of four-lobed, retractile anal papillae (respiratory/osmoregulatory organs) that can be extruded or completely withdrawn within the final segment. Live larvae exhibit a pathognomonic, active serpentine or eel-like wriggling swimming motion in water or liquid mud.
    *   *Pupae:* Measure 2.0 mm to 4.0 mm in length, colored golden-brown to dark brown. Pupae are non-feeding and rest at the water or mud surface. They are distinguished by three key morphological features: (1) a pair of respiratory trumpets on the cephalothorax that appear two-segmented; (2) abdominal segments bearing small but conspicuous tubercles, each ending in a fine hair; and (3) a prominent pair of horn-like terminal processes on the final abdominal segment. The pupal period lasts 2 to 3 days.

#### Lifecycle, Habitat & Host Attachment
*   **Primary Host Range:** Adults of both sexes feed on naturally occurring plant sugar solutions, nectar, and fruit juices. In addition, female biting midges require a protein blood meal from terrestrial vertebrates (humans, domestic livestock including cattle, sheep, goats, horses, dogs, wild mammals, and birds) or small insects for ovarian egg development.
*   **Developmental Stages:** Complete holometabolous lifecycle: Egg -> 4 Larval Instars -> Pupa -> Adult (Male / Female). Total developmental duration from egg to adult requires 2 to 4 weeks under warm tropical conditions, but may extend over winter in temperate zones. Approximately 30 species are autogenous (capable of maturing and laying their first batch of eggs without taking a blood meal). Adult females are short-lived, surviving for 1 to 3 weeks (rarely up to 4 weeks).
*   **Attachment Mechanics & Burrowing Behavior:** Biting midges do not burrow into skin or remain permanently attached to host tissues. Females land on exposed, bare skin, cut superficial dermal capillaries with lancet-like stylets, feed rapidly on pooled blood for 2 to 5 minutes, and fly off. Because short mouthparts cannot penetrate fabric or clothing, midges swarm around uncovered areas—frequently biting the forehead, scalp, face, ears, neck, hands, arms, ankles, and legs. Swarming and biting activity occurs at all hours of the day and night, but peaks during crepuscular hours (dusk and dawn) or on warm, humid, still days. Certain species exhibit specific feeding schedules; for example, *Culicoides grahamii* bites predominantly in the early morning.
*   **Environmental Survival Off-Host:** Adults inhabit outdoor natural vegetation, moist leaf litter, tree-holes, marsh margins, and agricultural plantations. Midges are weak fliers that usually fly only a few hundred meters from their larval breeding sites, although some *Culicoides* and *Leptoconops* species can fly 2 km to 3 km without wind assistance, or be carried tens to hundreds of kilometers by strong wind currents. Most species feed strictly outdoors (exophagic), but a few species (e.g., *C. milnei*, *C. austeni*, *C. grahamii*) readily enter human dwellings to feed (endophagic).

#### Pathogenesis & Vector Dynamics
*   **Direct Dermatologic Damage:** Mechanical cutting of dermal tissue by lancet-like mandibles and maxillae produces immediate focal tissue trauma, localized burning pain, microvascular laceration, and small hemorrhagic spots (puncta) at feeding sites.
*   **Hypersensitivity & Immune Response:** Salivary proteins and digestive enzymes injected during blood feeding elicit immediate (IgE-mediated) and delayed-type (cell-mediated) cutaneous hypersensitivity reactions. Bites produce small, intensely pruritic, red erythematous welts, papules, or indurated allergic lesions ("scrub itch", "autumnal itch", "punkie itch"). Persistent scratching can lead to excoriations and secondary bacterial skin infections.
*   **Pathogens Transmitted as a Vector:** Biting midges are biological vectors for human filarial nematodes, arboviruses, and major veterinary pathogens:
    *   *[[Mansonella perstans]]:* Filarial nematode widely distributed in sub-Saharan Africa and Central/South America. Vector species include *Culicoides austeni*, *Culicoides inornatipennis*, *Culicoides grahamii*, and *Culicoides milnei* in Africa (which breed in rotting banana and plantain tree stumps). Female midges ingest unsheathed, non-periodic microfilariae during blood meals on infected hosts. Microfilariae exsheath, penetrate the midge gut wall, invade thoracic flight muscles, undergo two molts to become L3 infective larvae over 8 to 12 days, and migrate to the head and proboscis. L3 larvae exit the proboscis onto host skin during subsequent blood feeding. Midge salivary glands are not involved in transmission.
    *   *[[Mansonella streptocerca]]:* Filarial nematode endemic to rainforests of West and Central Africa (from Ivory Coast to DRC and western Uganda). Transmitted primarily by *Culicoides grahamii* (and secondarily *C. milnei*, *C. austeni*). Unsheathed microfilariae residing in host skin snips are ingested during feeding; L3 infective larvae develop in thoracic muscles over 9 days and migrate to the proboscis.
    *   *[[Mansonella ozzardi]]:* Filarial nematode endemic to Central America, South America, and the Caribbean. Transmitted by *Culicoides* species (primarily *Culicoides furens* and *Culicoides [[Sand flies|phlebotomus]]*) and *Leptoconops becquaerti* in Caribbean islands and coastal regions (whereas *Simulium* [[blackflies]] serve as vectors in the Amazon basin).
    *   *Oropouche Virus (Bunyavirus):* Causative agent of Oropouche fever in Central and South America (Brazil, Colombia, Panama, Peru, Trinidad and Tobago). Biologically transmitted to humans by the bite of *Culicoides paraensis*, a forest and peri-urban midge that breeds in tree-holes and decaying plant matter. Sloths and monkeys serve as natural sylvatic reservoir hosts.
    *   *Veterinary Pathogens:* *Culicoides* species are major vectors of Bluetongue virus (Orbivirus) in sheep and cattle, and African Horse Sickness virus (Orbivirus) in equines.
*   **Host Defense & Predisposing Factors:** Exposure occurs during outdoor recreational, agricultural, or harvesting activities in wetland areas, salt marshes, riverbanks, forest margins, and banana/plantain plantations at dusk, dawn, or humid daytime hours.

#### Clinical Syndromes & Presentations
*   **Primary Infestation Presentation:** Biting midge dermatitis presents as multiple, highly pruritic, small red erythematous papules, wheals, or burning welts on exposed skin (forehead, scalp, face, neck, arms, hands, legs). Attacks by large swarms ("no-see-ums") cause severe local pain, intense irritation, and skin burning sensations.
*   **Pathognomonic Lesions & Distribution Pattern:** Multiple small pruritic papules or welts clustered on uncovered, exposed skin areas (scalp, forehead, face, neck, limbs). Clinical presentations of vector-borne infections:
    *   *Mansonelliasis perstans:* Infection is frequently asymptomatic. When symptomatic, presents with transient, subcutaneous Calabar-like swellings, generalised pruritus, fever, arthralgia, severe right upper quadrant abdominal pain, periorbital edema, eyelid swelling, proptosis, conjunctival nodules, high filaria-specific IgG4, and marked peripheral eosinophilia (or hypereosinophilia).
    *   *Mansonelliasis streptocerca:* Chronic cutaneous filariasis presenting with pruritic dermatitis over the upper trunk, chest, and shoulders, characterized by hypopigmented macules, papules, thickened lichenified skin, and regional lymphadenopathy (resembling onchocerciasis or tuberculoid leprosy).
    *   *Mansonelliasis ozzardi:* Mostly asymptomatic; occasionally presents with fever, joint pain, pruritus, headache, hepatomegaly, pulmonary symptoms, or corneal lesions in Amazonia.
    *   *Oropouche Fever:* Acute febrile illness featuring sudden high fever, severe headache, myalgia, arthralgia, retro-orbital pain, photophobia, nausea, vomiting, maculopapular rash, and occasional aseptic [[meningitis]].
*   **Secondary Complications:** Excoriation from persistent scratching causes secondary bacterial pyoderma, impetigo, and [[cellulitis]]. Swarms of midges in coastal resort areas cause economic disruption to tourism and outdoor labor.
*   **Laboratory & Dermoscopic Findings:**
    *   *Entomological Identification:* Examination under low-power stereomicroscopy. Confirm minute size (1.0–2.5 mm), short broad non-hairy wings with dark/pale spot patterns, 5-segmented drooping maxillary palps, non-plumose female antennae, and a pair of dorsal **humeral pits** on the anterior thorax (diagnostic for *Culicoides*).
    *   *Filarial Parasitology:* Demonstration of unsheathed microfilariae in peripheral blood (Knott's concentration or 3 µm membrane filtration) for *M. perstans* (200 µm, blunt tail with nuclei extending to tip) and *M. ozzardi* (200 µm, pointed tail with terminal space free of nuclei). Demonstration of unsheathed microfilariae in skin snips for *M. streptocerca* (180–240 µm, characteristic **shepherd's crook** curved tail with nuclei to tip).
    *   *Viral Diagnostics:* RT-[[PCR]] or serological [[ELISA and Immunoassays|ELISA]] for Oropouche virus during acute febrile illness.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Geographic Distribution:** Worldwide / cosmopolitan, ranging from sea level to 14,000 feet in Tibet (Everest base camp):
    *   *Culicoides perstans vectors:* Sub-Saharan Africa (West, Central, and East Africa south to Zimbabwe), Central/South America, Trinidad.
    *   *Culicoides streptocerca vectors:* Rainforest belt of West and Central Africa (Ivory Coast, Gabon, Congo, DRC, Uganda).
    *   *Culicoides ozzardi vectors:* Mexico, Panama, Colombia, Venezuela, Brazil, Bolivia, Argentina, and Caribbean islands (Trinidad, Haiti).
    *   *Oropouche virus vector (C. paraensis):* Brazil, Colombia, Panama, Peru, Trinidad and Tobago.
*   **Transmission Route:** Biological vector transmission via the bite of an infected female *Culicoides* or *Leptoconops* midge. Deposition of L3 infective filarial larvae onto skin during blood feeding, or salivary inoculation of Oropouche virus. No direct person-to-person transmission occurs.
*   **Institutional & Crowding Risks:** High risk in coastal resort areas near salt marshes, mangrove swamps, or wet mudflats; agricultural communities near banana and plantain plantations; forestry camps; and outdoor harvesting workers.
*   **Public Health & Outbreak Management:** Integrated control of biting midges is exceptionally difficult because larval habitats (vast marshes, muddy riverbanks, heathlands) are extensive and difficult to treat:
    1.  *Environmental Management:* Clearing or destroying localized breeding sites near dwellings (e.g., removing cut banana/plantain stumps, clearing decaying leaf piles, draining localized mud collections).
    2.  *Adult Control:* ULV insecticidal aerosol fogs (pyrethroids) applied to vegetation provide only short-lived, transient local suppression of adult resting midges.
    3.  *Personal Protection & Screening:* Applying chemical insect repellents (DEET 20%–50%, picaridin, or IR3535) to exposed skin; fitting dwellings with fine-mesh screening (standard window screening is too coarse and permits midge entry); sleeping under long-lasting insecticide-treated bed nets (LLINs / Olyset nets) impregnated with [[permethrin]] or deltamethrin.

#### Advanced Diagnostics
*   **Direct Examination & Skin Scrapings:** Stereomicroscopic identification of wild-caught flies, larvae, or pupae:
    *   *Adult Midges:* Inspect size (1.0–2.5 mm), drooping palps, wing scale patterns (patterned non-hairy wings), and verify presence of paired shiny black **humeral pits** on the anterior dorsum of the thorax.
    *   *Larvae:* Verify nematode-like cylindrical body (3–6 mm), dark conical head, 12 smooth segments without legs or prolegs, extruded four-lobed retractile anal papillae, and active serpentine swimming motion.
    *   *Pupae:* Verify 2-segmented respiratory trumpets, tuberculate abdominal segments with fine hairs, and paired horn-like processes on the terminal segment.
*   **Dermoscopy Features:** Dermoscopy (x10 to x20 magnification) of an acute midge bite demonstrates a central punctate hemorrhagic core surrounded by an edematous pale wheal and an outer erythematous inflammatory halo.
*   **Biopsy Findings:**
    *   *Bite Site Biopsy:* Demonstrates focal epidermal spongiosis, papillary dermal edema, dilated superficial blood vessels, and a dense perivascular lymphohistiocytic and eosinophilic infiltrate.
    *   *[[Mansonella streptocerca]] Skin Biopsy:* Histopathology demonstrates unsheathed microfilariae with shepherd's crook tails residing within the upper dermis, accompanied by dilated dermal lymphatics, perivascular lymphocytic infiltrates, and variable dermal edema.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Eradication
*   **Topical Scabicides & Pediculicides:** Topical scabicides or pediculicides applied to host skin are ineffective and not indicated for biting midge bites. Personal protection relies on topical chemical repellents applied to exposed skin: DEET (N,N-diethyl-m-toluamide, 20% to 50%), Picaridin, or IR3535. [[Permethrin]] 0.5% spray applied to clothing, uniforms, and fine-mesh bed nets acts as an effective contact insecticide/repellent.
*   **Oral Systemic Treatments:**
    *   *Symptomatic Bite Relief:* Topical calamine lotion, hydrocortisone 1% cream, or oral H1-antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine, diphenhydramine) to relieve pruritus and reduce allergic papular edema.
    *   *Systemic Antiparasitic Therapy for Transmitted Mansonelliasis:*
        *   **[[Mansonella perstans]]:** [[Doxycycline]] (200 mg PO daily for 4 to 6 weeks) targeting the obligate bacterial endosymbiont *Wolbachia*, which clears microfilaremia and sterilizes adult worms; alternatively, combination therapy with [[Mebendazole]] (100–200 mg PO BID for 30 days) plus [[Ivermectin]]. Standard [[Diethylcarbamazine]] ([[Diethylcarbamazine|DEC]]) or single-agent [[ivermectin]] are generally ineffective against *M. perstans*.
        *   **[[Mansonella streptocerca]]:** [[Diethylcarbamazine]] ([[Diethylcarbamazine|DEC]]; 6 mg/kg daily for 12 days) OR oral [[Ivermectin]] (single dose of 150 µg/kg). Both agents clear microfilariae rapidly and resolve cutaneous dermatitis.
        *   **[[Mansonella ozzardi]]:** Oral [[Ivermectin]] (single dose of 150 µg/kg to 200 µg/kg). [[Diethylcarbamazine|DEC]] is completely ineffective against *M. ozzardi*.
        *   **Oropouche Fever:** Supportive symptomatic therapy (antipyretics, analgesics, oral rehydration).
*   **Treatment Nuances & Re-application Schedules:** Topical insect repellents must be reapplied every 4 to 6 hours during outdoor exposure. Fine-mesh bed nets must be impregnated with [[permethrin]] or deltamethrin every 6 months (or LLINs used) because midges can physically crawl through standard coarse mosquito netting if un-treated.
*   **Environmental & Laundry Decontamination Protocols:** Wearing light-colored long-sleeved shirts and trousers. Treating clothing with [[permethrin]]. Closing fine-mesh window screens before dusk. Eliminating small, localized standing water and mud collections, decaying leaf piles, and cut banana stumps near residential areas.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "No-see-ums" / "Punkies" / "Flying teeth" (common names reflecting tiny size and sharp bite), "Humeral pits" (diagnostic paired thoracic depressions in *Culicoides*), "Banana stump breeding" (*C. milnei*, *C. austeni*, *C. grahamii*), "Serpentine / eel-like swimming motion" (live larval movement), "Four-lobed retractile anal papillae" (terminal larval respiratory lobes), "Pass through standard mosquito netting" (requires insecticidal impregnation or fine mesh), "Shepherd's crook tail" (*[[Mansonella streptocerca]]* microfilaria in skin snips), "Pool feeding / Telmophagy" (slashing skin to drink pooled capillary blood), "14,000 feet altitude record" (Everest expedition bites in Tibet).
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   Biting midges are among the smallest blood-sucking flies in the world (1.0–2.5 mm), yet their short mouthparts CANNOT bite through clothing; midges are forced to swarm and bite uncovered skin areas such as the face, forehead, scalp, and ankles.
    *   Standard window screening and untreated mosquito bed nets fail to exclude biting midges because of their tiny size; netting MUST be fine-meshed or impregnated with pyrethroid insecticides ([[permethrin]]/deltamethrin) to prevent midge entry.
    *   *[[Mansonella perstans]]* is resistant to standard single-dose [[ivermectin]] or [[Diethylcarbamazine|DEC]] treatment, but responds dramatically to oral [[doxycycline]] (targeting its obligate *Wolbachia* endosymbionts), whereas *[[Mansonella ozzardi]]* responds to [[ivermectin]] but is completely resistant to [[Diethylcarbamazine|DEC]].
    *   Larvae of *Culicoides* are best recognized by their small dark head, 12 legless segments, four-lobed retractile anal papillae, and distinct serpentine wriggling motion in water or mud.
    *   Unlike [[mosquitoes]] (where salivary glands transmit [[Plasmodium sp.|malaria]] and viruses), the salivary glands of *Culicoides* play no part in the transmission of *Mansonella* filarial worms; microfilariae develop in thoracic muscles and migrate to the labium/proboscis.
