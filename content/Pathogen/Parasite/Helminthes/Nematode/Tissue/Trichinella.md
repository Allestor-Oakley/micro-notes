### Trichinella

#### Helminth Anatomy & Ova Morphology
*   **Adult Worm Morphology & Attachment Structures:** Extremely small, slender, creamy-white roundworms just visible to the naked eye. Adult males measure 1.2 to 1.6 mm in length by 0.04 mm in diameter, featuring a caudal extremity with a cloaca situated posteriorly between two conical caudal papillae (lacking chitinous copulatory spicules). Adult females measure 2.2 to 4.0 mm (typically 3–4 mm) in length by 0.06 mm in diameter, with a vulva located in the anterior fifth of the body, a single ovary in the posterior half, and a coiled uterine tube in the anterior portion. Adults possess a homomyarian-type musculature and reside embedded within or attached to the epithelial brush border of the small intestinal mucosa (duodenum and jejunum) without specialized chitinous teeth or cutting plates.
*   **Ova / Egg Microscopic Features:** *Trichinella* species are strictly viviparous / ovoviviparous. Adult female worms DO NOT produce, lay, or shed eggs into the lumen or host environment. Fertilized female worms discharge live first-stage larvae (L1) directly into the intestinal mucosa and lamina propria. Free eggs are NEVER present, produced, or observed in human stool, urine, or tissue biopsy specimens (N/A).
*   **Larval Stage Morphologies:** First-stage larvae (L1) released in the gut measure approximately 80 to 120 µm in length. Migrating L1 larvae penetrate striated skeletal muscle fibers and invade host myocytes. In encapsulated species (*T. spiralis*, *T. nativa*, *T. britovi*, *T. murrelli*, *T. nelsoni*, *T. patagoniensis*, genotypes T6, T8, T9), the larva reprograms the muscle cell into a specialized "nurse cell" and induces the formation of an oval/elliptical collagenous cyst capsule around itself. Inside the nurse cell, the larva coils tightly into a classic "watch-spring" configuration (measuring ~0.8 to 1.0 mm when uncoiled). In unencapsulated species (*T. pseudospiralis*, *T. papuae*, *T. zimbabwensis*), larvae inhabit muscle fibers without inducing a surrounding collagen capsule.
*   **Sexual Dimorphism & Reproductive Characteristics:** Sexes are separate (dioecious). Pronounced size dimorphism: adult females (3–4 mm × 0.06 mm) are roughly twice as long as adult males (1.2–1.6 mm × 0.04 mm). Mating occurs in the small intestinal mucosa approximately 30 hours to 2 days post-infection. Male worms die shortly after copulation. Fertilized females begin releasing live L1 larvae around day 5 to 7 post-infection and continue larval production for several weeks (1 to 4 months) before being expelled by the host Th2 intestinal immune response.

#### Lifecycle & Transmission Pathways
*   **Definitive Host:** Carnivorous and omnivorous mammals, including domestic swine/pigs, wild boars, bears (polar, brown, grizzly), walruses, horses, seals, wolves, foxes, rodents, and humans. *Unique Lifecycle Feature:* A single individual host acts sequentially as both the definitive host (harboring adult worms in the gut) and the intermediate host (harboring encysted larvae in skeletal muscle). Humans serve as accidental, dead-end hosts.
*   **Intermediate Host(s):** Same as definitive hosts (swine, bears, walruses, horses, wild carnivores, rodents, humans).
*   **Transmission Mode & Ingestion/Penetration:** Acquired strictly via the oral route through ingestion of raw, improperly cooked, undercooked, rare, or inadequately cured/frozen meat (or meat products such as homemade sausages) containing viable, infective encysted (or unencapsulated) L1 larvae. Transmission in nature is maintained through animal cannibalism, carrion feeding, and feeding raw offal/garbage to domestic swine.
*   **Intra-Host Larval Migration Pathway:** Swallowed meat is digested in the stomach by acid and pepsin, liberating infective larvae into the intestinal lumen. Released larvae penetrate the mucosal epithelium of the duodenum and jejunum, undergoing four rapid molts over 30 hours to mature into adult male and female worms. After mating, gravid females deposit live L1 larvae into intestinal mucosal lymphatics and mesenteric venules starting on day 5–7. Larvae travel via systemic circulation through the heart and pulmonary capillaries to reach capillary beds across the body. Larvae selectively breach capillaries and invade highly oxygenated, active skeletal muscles (diaphragm, extraocular muscles, masseter, tongue, larynx, neck, intercostals, biceps, gastrocnemius, and muscle insertions into tendons). Within myocytes, larvae develop into infective L1 larvae and form nurse cell complexes (encysting in most species) by days 10–14. Larvae entering non-striated tissues (myocardium, brain parenchyma) cannot encyst and trigger severe focal necrotizing inflammation before degenerating. Muscle cysts undergo dystrophic calcification over 5 to 6 months, which may or may not kill the enclosed larvae (larvae can remain viable for years in muscle tissue).
*   **Diagnostic Stage & Shedding Site:** Diagnostic confirmation relies on finding encysted/unencapsulated larvae in histologic sections or squash preparations of striated muscle biopsy tissue, or detecting parasite-specific anti-*Trichinella* antibodies in serum. Stool O&P examinations are strictly NEGATIVE, as adult worms and larvae are not shed in human feces (except for rare pass-through of ingested larvae/adults within the first 24 hours of eating heavily infected meat).

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Tissue Invasion & Structural Damage:**
    *   *Enteric Phase:* Larval invasion and molting in duodenal/jejunal mucosa cause localized mechanical mucosal irritation, villous edema, surface erosion, and catarrhal enteritis.
    *   *Muscle Invasion Phase:* Larval penetration of skeletal muscle fibers causes focal myocyte destruction, loss of striations, basophilic cell transformation, nurse cell complex formation, and intense acute and chronic inflammatory responses (heavy infiltration with polymorphonuclear neutrophils, eosinophils, lymphocytes, and [[Macrophage|macrophages]]). Tissue injury releases muscle enzymes (creatine kinase, aldolase, LDH) into serum.
    *   *Vasculitis & Extra-Muscular Damage:* Circulating larvae and excretory-secretory (ES) antigens provoke widespread allergic leukocytoclastic vasculitis, manifest as periorbital/facial edema, subconjunctival hemorrhages, subungual "splinter" hemorrhages beneath fingernails/toenails, and macular cutaneous eruptions.
    *   *Cardiac & Neural Damage:* Larval transit through the myocardium causes acute focal myocardial necrosis, patchy interstitial infiltrates, and severe eosinophilic myocarditis (the primary cause of death in fatal cases). Larval invasion of the central nervous system causes focal micro-infarcts, perivascular cuffing, cerebral edema, and necrotizing meningoencephalitis.
*   **Mechanical Obstruction:** N/A
*   **Nutritional Depletion & Anemia:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** *Trichinella* larvae reprogram host skeletal muscle cells into specialized nurse cells that synthesize a protective collagen capsule (in encapsulated species), protecting the larva from direct leukocyte destruction and enabling multi-year survival in host tissue. Parasites also induce polyclonal lymphocyte activation and [[Macrophage]] modulation.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Host defense depends on Th2-mediated intestinal expulsion (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IgE, mucosal mast cells, and eosinophils) to clear adult worms from the gut. In severe infection, an unbridled host allergic and cellular hypersensitivity response to migrating larval antigens drives systemic morbidity (high fever, severe myositis, vasculitis, and cerebral/cardiac inflammation).

#### Clinical Syndromes & Complications
*   **Acute Migration Phase Presentation:**
    *   *Phase 1: Intestinal Phase (Days 2 to 7 post-ingestion):* Onset within 24–48 hours to 7 days. Presents with gastroenteritis-like symptoms: diarrhea (or constipation), nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, loss of appetite, mild fever, frontal headache, dizziness, and general malaise. Often misdiagnosed as bacterial food poisoning or viral intestinal flu.
    *   *Phase 2: Muscle Invasion Phase (Days 9 to 28 post-ingestion):* Characterized by systemic larval migration and muscle entry. Symptoms include high remittent fever (40°C–41°C), periorbital and facial edema (hallmark finding present in ~75–84% of symptomatic patients), intense muscle pain (myalgia), muscle tenderness, swelling, and weakness (most severe in diaphragm, extraocular, masseter, tongue, larynx, and limb muscles, causing pain on chewing, swallowing, breathing, and eye movement). Accompanied by subungual splinter hemorrhages under fingernails, subconjunctival/retinal hemorrhages, macular skin rash, urticaria, profuse sweating, and tachycardia.
*   **Chronic Intestinal or Tissue Presentation:**
    *   *Phase 3: Encystment & Convalescent Phase (Weeks 4 to 8+):* Fever and acute allergic manifestations subside as larvae complete nurse cell encystment. Muscle stiffness, pain, easy fatigability, and weakness may persist for months. Cysts calcify over 5–6 months.
    *   *Severe Complications:*
        *   *Myocarditis:* The most dangerous and life-threatening complication; presents with dyspnea, precordial pain, arrhythmias, electrocardiographic abnormalities, elevated cardiac troponin, and acute heart failure.
        *   *Neurotrichinosis:* Meningoencephalitis, intense headaches, nuchal pseudorigidity, confusion, delirium, psychosis, seizures, cranial nerve palsies, hemiplegia, and cerebrovascular accidents (CVA).
        *   *Pulmonary Complications:* Pneumonitis, pleurisy, and respiratory failure secondary to severe invasion of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** The classic clinical triad of persistent fever, periorbital/facial edema, and myositis/myalgia accompanied by subungual splinter hemorrhages beneath the fingernails and high peripheral blood eosinophilia; or direct microscopic visualization of coiled larvae within muscle biopsy tissue.
*   **Eosinophilia Profile & IgE Response:** Marked, profound peripheral blood eosinophilia (characteristically 20% to 90%, or >1,000 to >5,000 eosinophils/µL) accompanied by leukocytosis and significantly elevated total serum IgE levels. Eosinophilia appears during week 2, peaks during weeks 3–4 (muscle invasion), and persists for 3 to 6 months before declining. *Note:* Eosinophilia may be absent in fulminant, overwhelming, or fatal cases.

#### Epidemiology & Exposures
*   **Geographic Endemicity:** Cosmopolitan / Worldwide distribution. Species-specific distribution patterns:
    *   *Trichinella spiralis* (T1): Worldwide; most important species in domestic pigs, rats, horses, and humans.
    *   *Trichinella nativa* (T2) & Genotype T6: Arctic and subarctic regions of North America, Europe, and Asia (bears, walruses, seals, wolves).
    *   *Trichinella britovi* (T3): Temperate regions of Europe, Asia, Northern/Western Africa; second-most common human pathogen (wild boars, horses, foxes, bush pigs).
    *   *Trichinella murrelli* (T5): United States and southern Canada (wild carnivores, bears, horses; does not develop in swine).
    *   *Trichinella nelsoni* (T7): Eastern and Southern Africa (warthogs, bush pigs).
    *   *Trichinella pseudospiralis* (T4): Cosmopolitan (birds and mammals).
    *   *Trichinella papuae* (T10): Papua New Guinea (swine, wild reptiles/varans/caimans).
    *   *Trichinella zimbabwensis* (T11): Sub-Saharan Africa (wild and farmed reptiles).
    *   *Trichinella patagoniensis* (T12): Neotropics, Argentina (carnivores).
    *   *Genotypes T8 & T9:* South Africa/Namibia (T8) and Japan (T9).
*   **Soil & Environmental Requirements:** Sylvatic and synanthropic cycles maintained in nature via carnovorism, necrophagy (carrion eating), and cannibalism. Soil plays no direct developmental role. Cysts can remain viable for years in decaying muscle carcasses.
*   **Dietary Exposures & Intermediate Host Foods:** Ingestion of raw, undercooked, rare, cold-smoked, or inadequately cured meat products, including pork, bear meat (grizzly, brown, polar bear), walrus meat, horse meat, wild boar, warthog, bush pig, or homemade pork/wild game sausages. Major horse meat outbreaks have occurred in France and Italy; bear meat outbreaks in New York and Tennessee; wild boar outbreaks in France and Thailand; and *T. murrelli* outbreaks in Northern California.
*   **Public Health & Mass Drug Administration:** European Union and international regulations require mandatory slaughterhouse testing of pig diaphragms using direct microscopy (trichinoscopy) or artificial peptic digestion of pooled meat samples. Prevention relies on consumer education regarding meat preparation, prohibiting feeding raw garbage/offal to pigs, and strict thermal processing or freezing protocols.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Stool Ova & Parasite Examination:** Stool O&P examination is strictly UNHELPFUL / NEGATIVE. Adult worms and larvae inhabit tissues and are not passed in stool (except rare incidental passage of ingested larvae/adults in feces within the first 24 hours of eating heavily infected raw meat).
*   **Sedimentation & Flotation Concentration Techniques:** N/A for stool. Artificial peptic digestion procedure: small muscle biopsy specimens or meat samples are digested in a solution of 0.5% pepsin and 0.7% hydrochloric acid in water at 37°C for several hours, then poured into a Baermann funnel filled with warm water. Released motile larvae settle to the bottom of the funnel tube for collection and microscopic counting/identification.
*   **Serology & Tissue Biopsy Diagnostics:**
    *   *Muscle Biopsy (Diagnostic Gold Standard / Confirmation):* Biopsy of affected skeletal muscle (deltoid, biceps, or gastrocnemius) taken after day 17 post-infection (when encystment begins). Prepared as a fresh muscle squash/compression preparation pressed between two glass slides and examined under low power (10× objective) for coiled larvae; or processed for routine histopathology (H&E stain), demonstrating cross-sections or coiled longitudinal views of L1 larvae inside modified nurse cells surrounded by chronic granulomatous/eosinophilic myositis and fibrosis.
    *   *Serological Assays:* Enzyme Immunoassay (EIA / ELISA), Bentonite Flocculation (BF), Indirect Fluorescent Antibody Test (IFAT), and Western Blot assays detect anti-*Trichinella* antibodies in serum. Seroconversion or a 4-fold rise in antibody titer provides definitive diagnosis. Antibodies usually become detectable 2 to 3 weeks post-infection.
    *   *Biomarkers & Serum Chemistry:* Elevated serum muscle enzymes (creatine kinase / CK, aldolase, lactate dehydrogenase / LDH, aspartate aminotransferase / AST) reflect active myositis; elevated cardiac troponin levels confirm clinical or subclinical myocarditis.
*   **Imaging Findings:** Brain MRI in neurotrichinosis demonstrates multiple small, punctate or nodular hyperintense lesions on T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences in subcortical white matter, representing focal infarction or larval inflammatory nodules.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Anti-Helminthic Agents:**
    *   [[Albendazole]]: 400 mg orally twice daily (BID) for 8 to 14 days (children: 15 mg/kg/day divided in 2 doses, max 400 mg/dose).
    *   [[Mebendazole]]: 200 to 400 mg orally three times daily (TID) for 3 days, followed by 400 to 500 mg TID for 10 days (or 5 mg/kg/day).
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Agents:** [[Pyrantel pamoate]] (administered during the early intestinal phase to clear adult worms from the gut lumen).
*   **Treatment Nuances & Corticosteroid Protocols:**
    *   *Mandatory Corticosteroid Protocol:* Systemic corticosteroids (e.g., Prednisolone 40 to 60 mg/day orally, or Prednisone 20 mg TID) MUST be co-administered alongside anthelmintic therapy in patients with moderate-to-severe disease, high fever, severe allergic signs, persistent myositis, central nervous system involvement, or myocarditis. Steroids suppress severe hypersensitivity and inflammatory reactions triggered by dying larvae. Steroids are gradually tapered and discontinued over 2 to 3 weeks as acute allergic symptoms resolve.
    *   *Therapeutic Limitation:* Anthelmintics effectively eliminate adult worms from the gut and target circulating/migrating larvae, but have limited efficacy against long-encysted larvae in muscle tissue; management of late muscle phase relies primarily on anti-inflammatory and supportive care.
*   **Prophylaxis, Sanitation & Food Safety:**
    *   *Thermal Processing (Cooking):* Thoroughly cooking all pork, wild game, bear, walrus, horse, and wild boar meat to an internal temperature of at least 160°F (71°C) until juices run clear completely destroys larvae.
    *   *Freezing Protocols:* Deep-freezing commercial pork less than 6 inches thick at 5°F (-17°C) for 20 days, -10°F (-23°C) for 10 days, or -20°F (-29°C) for 6 days inactivates *T. spiralis* larvae.
    *   *CRITICAL FREEZING EXCEPTION:* Standard freezing DOES NOT kill freeze-resistant sylvatic/Arctic species (*T. nativa*, genotype T6, *T. britovi*) encysted in bear, walrus, horse, or wild carnivore meat. Wild game meat from Arctic/subarctic or mountain regions MUST be thoroughly cooked, not frozen.
    *   *Agricultural Sanitation:* Prohibiting the feeding of raw garbage or uncooked animal offal to domestic pigs, maintaining strict rodent control in swine facilities, and preventing pig access to wild animal carcasses.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Pork worm, *Trichinella spiralis*, *T. nativa*, nurse cell complex, watch-spring coiled larva, periorbital edema, subungual splinter hemorrhages, triad of fever + periorbital edema + myalgia with marked eosinophilia, muscle biopsy squash prep, Baermann funnel artificial peptic digestion, polar bear / walrus meat outbreak, freeze-resistant larvae, unencapsulated *T. pseudospiralis*, elevated troponin in myocarditis.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   *Single-Host Lifecycle:* Unlike most helminths that require distinct species for definitive and intermediate hosts, *Trichinella* completes its entire developmental cycle (adults and larvae) within a SINGLE individual host animal.
    *   *Viviparous Reproduction:* Female *Trichinella* worms do not lay eggs; they are viviparous, releasing live first-stage larvae directly into the host mucosal lymphatics.
    *   *Ineffectiveness of Freezing for Arctic Game:* While deep-freezing kills *T. spiralis* in domestic pork, Arctic/sylvatic species (*T. nativa*, T6) possess extreme cold tolerance and remain fully viable and infectious in frozen bear, walrus, or wolf muscle for months to years.
    *   *Unencapsulated Species:* *T. pseudospiralis*, *T. papuae*, and *T. zimbabwensis* do not induce collagen capsule formation in host muscle, living as free, unencapsulated larvae within myocytes.
    *   *Absence of Stool Eggs/Larvae:* Routine O&P stool examinations are completely useless and negative in human trichinosis despite the presence of adult worms in the gut mucosa.
