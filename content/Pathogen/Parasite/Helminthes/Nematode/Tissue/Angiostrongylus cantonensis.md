### Angiostrongylus cantonensis

#### Helminth Anatomy & Ova Morphology
*   **Adult Worm Morphology & Attachment Structures:** Mature adult worms naturally reside inside the pulmonary arteries of definitive rodent hosts. Adult females measure 20 to 25 mm in length by 0.26 to 0.36 mm in width. Young adult worms migrating within human or animal brain tissue measure approximately 2 mm in length. Taxonomic reclassification has placed *A. cantonensis* alongside *A. costaricensis*, *A. malaysiensis*, and *A. mackerrasae* into the genus *Parastrongylus*, though the generic name *Angiostrongylus* remains widely used in clinical literature. Adults and larvae lack chitinous biting teeth or cutting plates, existing as tissue-dwelling and intravascular roundworms.
*   **Ova / Egg Microscopic Features:** N/A in human clinical specimens. Mature female adults lay eggs inside the pulmonary arteries of definitive rat hosts, where eggs lodge in lung capillaries and hatch into first-stage larvae (L1). Because adult worms do not reach reproductive maturity or undergo oviposition in the non-permissive human host, eggs are never produced or shed in human feces, urine, or tissue.
*   **Larval Stage Morphologies:** First-stage larvae (L1) passed in rat feces are ingested by gastropod mollusks, developing within 2 weeks into infective third-stage larvae (L3). When ingested by mammalian hosts, L3 larvae penetrate the intestinal tract, enter the systemic circulation, and migrate to the central nervous system, developing into fourth-stage larvae (L4) and young adults (~2 mm) within 4 weeks.
*   **Sexual Dimorphism & Reproductive Characteristics:** Sexes are separate (dioecious). Mature adult males and females reside in copula within the pulmonary arterial tree of rats. In humans (accidental dead-end hosts), young adult male and female worms migrate into the brain tissue, subarachnoid space, or eye, but die in situ without successfully reproducing or shedding progeny.

#### Lifecycle & Transmission Pathways
*   **Definitive Host:** Rats / Rodents (e.g., *Rattus* spp., where adult worms mature in the pulmonary arteries). An established natural reservoir exists in feral rodent populations in New Orleans, Louisiana.
*   **Intermediate Host(s):** Gastropod mollusks, specifically terrestrial and freshwater snails (e.g., common garden snails, giant African land snails) and slugs.
*   **Paratenic / Transport Hosts:** Land planarians (flatworms), land crabs, coconut crabs, freshwater prawns, amphibians (frogs), reptiles (lizards, snakes), fish, and birds (chickens, domestic ducks).
*   **Transmission Mode & Ingestion/Penetration:**
    *   *Primary Route (Ingestion of Mollusks):* Ingestion of raw, undercooked, or live snails or slugs containing infective L3 larvae (often ingested accidentally, on a dare, or in traditional culinary dishes).
    *   *Ingestion of Paratenic Hosts:* Ingestion of raw or undercooked paratenic host animal flesh (land crabs, coconut crabs, freshwater prawns, frogs, fish, reptiles, or poultry).
    *   *Contaminated Produce & Water:* Consumption of raw unwashed vegetables, salad greens, or fruit contaminated with small hidden slugs, crushed snails, or larval secretions; drinking untreated groundwater or water containing free larvae.
    *   *Other Exposure Pathways:* Accidental hand-to-mouth transmission after handling live snails/slugs or exposure through skin contact/abrasions.
*   **Intra-Host Larval Migration Pathway:** Ingested L3 larvae excyst in the gastrointestinal tract, penetrate the intestinal wall, enter the mesenteric venules, and travel via the bloodstream through the liver and heart to reach the central nervous system (brain tissue, meninges, and subarachnoid space). In humans, larvae molt into L4 and young adults (~2 mm in length), but cannot return to the pulmonary arteries to complete their development; instead, they wander through brain parenchyma, subarachnoid spaces, or eye chambers and die in situ. In natural rat hosts, young adults leave the subarachnoid space, re-enter the venous system, and migrate to the pulmonary arteries to mature and lay eggs. Prepatent period in rats is roughly 4 weeks; incubation period in humans averages 20 days (range: days to about 1 month / 20–40 days).
*   **Diagnostic Stage & Shedding Site:** Recovery and microscopic identification of young adult worms (~2 mm) or larvae in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), surgical brain biopsy specimens, or the anterior chamber/vitreous of the eye. Stool examinations are strictly negative, as no parasite stages are shed in human feces.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Tissue Invasion & Structural Damage:** Larval and young adult migration through brain parenchyma and subarachnoid spaces causes direct mechanical tissue disruption and nerve tract compression. The death of larvae and young adults within the central nervous system releases potent metabolic and somatic antigens, provoking an intense local type I and type IV hypersensitivity response. This leads to heavy eosinophilic and lymphocytic meningeal infiltration, focal necrosis, granuloma formation, cerebral edema, hyperesthesias, and elevated intracranial pressure. Larval migration into ocular structures causes focal retinal penetration, choroiditis, retinal detachment, vitreous clouding, and scarring.
*   **Mechanical Obstruction:** High larval loads or inflammatory exudates in the subarachnoid space can impede normal cerebrospinal fluid circulation, contributing to increased intracranial pressure and hydrocephalus.
*   **Nutritional Depletion & Anemia:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** Live infective larvae migrate through host tissues initially provoking only mild localized host cell activation; however, immune evasion fails as worms reach the CNS and mature or die, triggering an unbridled host inflammatory reaction to dead parasite debris.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Pathogenesis in humans is driven primarily by the host's own vigorous cell-mediated and allergic immune response (marked eosinophilia, IL-5, IgE) directed against dying larvae in the immune-privileged central nervous system.

#### Clinical Syndromes & Complications
*   **Acute Migration Phase Presentation:** Incubation period ranges from days to approximately 1 month (typically ~20 days). The cardinal, predominant clinical symptom present in virtually all cases of meningitic angiostrongyliasis is severe, agonizing, incapacitating headache. Accompanying acute manifestations include:
    *   Fever (low-grade to moderate) and chills.
    *   Meningeal signs: stiff neck (nuchal rigidity) and photophobia.
    *   Gastrointestinal distress: nausea, vomiting, and anorexia.
    *   Neurological deficits: convulsions/seizures, limb weakness, muscle twitching, paresthesias, hyperesthesias (burning or painful skin sensations of the trunk and extremities), myalgias, and cranial nerve palsies (most frequently cranial nerve VIII / 8th nerve palsy causing auditory/vestibular dysfunction).
*   **Chronic Intestinal or Tissue Presentation:** Meningitic disease typically follows a self-limited, relatively benign course over a few weeks to months, with a generally good prognosis. Symptoms resolve gradually: meningeal signs and headaches resolve first, followed by visual abnormalities, while paresthesias and hyperesthesias are often the last to disappear (persisting for months). Severe meningoencephalitis, permanent neurological deficits, or fatal outcomes occur rarely.
*   **Ocular Angiostrongyliasis:** Larval migration into the eye causes visual impairment, ocular pain, anterior chamber masses, retinal hemorrhages, retinal detachment, vitreous opacities, and rare cases of permanent blindness.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Severe headache accompanied by prominent CSF eosinophilia (>10% up to 90% eosinophils), or direct visualization of a 2 mm young adult roundworm crawling in the anterior chamber of the eye or recovered from CSF in a patient with recent raw snail/slug exposure.
*   **Eosinophilia Profile & IgE Response:** Marked peripheral blood eosinophilia (frequently up to 90% white blood cells) accompanied by striking cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) eosinophilia (hallmark of eosinophilic meningitis; CSF eosinophil differential typically ranges from 10% to over 80%). Total serum and CSF IgE levels are significantly elevated.

#### Epidemiology & Exposures
*   **Geographic Endemicity:** Endemic across tropical and subtropical regions worldwide, particularly in Southeast Asia (Thailand, Vietnam, Malaysia), China, Japan, Taiwan, Hawaii, and the Western Pacific Islands (Fiji). Also endemic in Australia (eastern coast), Madagascar, Egypt, Africa (Nigeria), the Caribbean (Cuba, Jamaica, Puerto Rico), Central America, and North America (foci in Louisiana / New Orleans).
*   **Soil & Environmental Requirements:** Requires warm, humid, moist environments that support dense populations of gastropod intermediate hosts (snails, slugs) and rat definitive hosts.
*   **Dietary Exposures & Intermediate Host Foods:**
    *   Ingestion of raw or undercooked land snails (e.g., *Helix* spp., giant African snails) or slugs (consumed intentionally as local delicacies, undercooked, or swallowed raw on dares).
    *   Ingestion of raw or improperly cooked paratenic hosts: land crabs, coconut crabs, freshwater prawns, frogs, fish, or chicken/duck meat.
    *   Consumption of unwashed raw leafy vegetables, salads, or fruits contaminated with microscopic juvenile slugs, snails, or larval secretions.
    *   Drinking untreated groundwater or open surface water contaminated with larvae.
*   **Public Health & Mass Drug Administration:** Public health management centers on health education regarding the risks of eating raw mollusks or paratenic hosts, food hygiene (washing produce), rodent control to limit definitive rat host density, and snail population management.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Stool Ova & Parasite Examination:** Stool O&P examination is strictly NEGATIVE / UNHELPFUL. Human infections do not produce adult worms in the gut, and no eggs or larvae are passed in feces.
*   **Sedimentation & Flotation Concentration Techniques:** N/A (Fecal concentration techniques are unhelpful/negative).
*   **Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) Analysis:** Lumbar puncture is essential. Analysis demonstrates elevated CSF opening pressure, turbid or clear fluid, pleocytosis (typically 200–2,000 WBC/µL) with marked CSF eosinophilia (>10% to 90%), elevated protein levels, and normal or slightly low glucose levels. Microscopic examination of wet mounts or cytospin preparations of CSF sediment occasionally recovers motile young adult worms (~2 mm long).
*   **Serology & Tissue Biopsy Diagnostics:**
    *   *Serological Assays:* Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA/EIA) and Western blot / Immunoblot assays (available through reference laboratories such as Parasitic Disease Consultants / CDC) detect specific anti-*Angiostrongylus* antibodies in serum and CSF.
    *   *Molecular Diagnostics:* Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and real-time PCR assays targeting *A. cantonensis* DNA in CSF or tissue samples provide definitive molecular identification.
    *   *Histopathology:* Examination of brain biopsy, ocular tissue, or autopsy sections reveals longitudinal or cross-sections of nematodes (2 mm in length) within the meninges, subarachnoid space, or brain tissue surrounded by intense eosinophilic and lymphocytic granulomatous inflammation.
*   **Imaging Findings:** Brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) or Computed Tomography (CT) may demonstrate leptomeningeal enhancement, ventricular enlargement, subtle punctate or nodular hyperintense lesions in the subcortical and deep white matter, or linear tracks in brain parenchyma.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Anti-Helminthic Agents:**
    *   Mebendazole: 100 mg orally twice daily for 5 days.
    *   Note: Anthelmintic therapy remains controversial because rapid drug-induced killing of larvae within the brain can trigger massive release of parasitic antigens, exacerbating central nervous system inflammatory reactions and worsening acute neurological symptoms.
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Agents:** Albendazole (400 mg orally twice daily for 7 to 14 days) or Levamisole.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Corticosteroid Protocols:**
    *   *Supportive Pressure Relief:* Repeated therapeutic lumbar punctures to drain CSF are highly effective for immediately lowering elevated intracranial pressure and providing rapid relief from severe headache.
    *   *Corticosteroid Protocol:* Concomitant systemic corticosteroids (e.g., Prednisolone 30 to 60 mg/day or Dexamethasone) are strongly recommended whenever anthelmintics are administered—or used as primary monotherapy—to suppress CNS hypersensitivity reactions, diminish intracranial swelling, and shorten the clinical duration of pain and neurological symptoms. Analgesics should be co-administered for pain control.
*   **Prophylaxis, Sanitation & Food Safety:** Personal protection involves avoiding the consumption of raw or undercooked snails, slugs, land crabs, coconut crabs, freshwater prawns, or frogs; thoroughly cooking all seafood and meats (larvae are destroyed by heat); thoroughly washing raw leafy vegetables and salad greens before eating; immersing fresh vegetables in strong vinegar for at least 5 hours (note: soaking in lime juice or refrigeration at 4°C for 1 month DOES NOT kill larvae); drinking boiled or treated water; washing hands thoroughly after gardening or handling soil/mollusks; and controlling rat populations around human habitations.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Rat lungworm, *Parastrongylus cantonensis*, eosinophilic meningitis, severe agonizing headache, eating raw snail on a dare, garden snail / slug, freshwater prawns/crabs, marked CSF eosinophilia, repeated therapeutic lumbar puncture, corticosteroids to suppress dying worm inflammation, New Orleans feral rodent reservoir.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   *Non-Intestinal Nematode:* Unlike classic soil-transmitted intestinal nematodes (*Ascaris*, *Trichuris*, hookworms), *A. cantonensis* never matures in or inhabits the human GI tract, making stool O&P examinations completely negative.
    *   *Dead-End Host Neuropathology:* Human disease is caused entirely by accidental, dead-end migration of non-multiplying larvae into the central nervous system, where disease pathology stems from host inflammatory reactions against dying worms rather than active replication.
    *   *Inactivation Resistance:* L3 larvae inside mollusks or vegetation are remarkably resistant: soaking contaminated produce in lime juice or refrigerating at 4°C for up to 1 month DOES NOT kill infective larvae, whereas thorough cooking or immersion in strong vinegar for at least 5 hours successfully inactivates them.
