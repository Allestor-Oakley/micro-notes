### Angiostrongylus costaricensis

#### Helminth Anatomy & Ova Morphology
*   **Adult Worm Morphology & Attachment Structures:** Slender, cylindrical roundworms residing within the mesenteric arteries and their branches supplying the ileocecal region of definitive hosts. In histological tissue sections (e.g., H&E-stained sections of appendix or bowel wall), cross-sections of adult female worms display a characteristic multinucleate intestine (IN), paired uterine branches (UT) filled with eggs (E), and a smooth outer cuticle. Worms lack chitinous teeth, cutting plates, or suckers, living intravascularly or embedded within the bowel wall and submucosa. Taxonomic reclassification places this parasite in the genus *Parastrongylus* (*Parastrongylus costaricensis*) alongside *P. cantonensis*, *P. malaysiensis*, and *P. mackerrasae*, although *Angiostrongylus* remains widely used in clinical medicine.
*   **Ova / Egg Microscopic Features:** Eggs are deposited by adult females directly into the lumens of small mesenteric arterioles and adjacent intestinal/appendiceal wall tissues. In tissue sections, oval/elliptical eggs are observed embedded within the mucosal, submucosal, and muscular layers of the appendix or small intestine, frequently surrounded by intense eosinophilic granulomatous inflammation. Eggs are NOT reliably passed or detected in human stool O&P examinations.
*   **Larval Stage Morphologies:** Third-stage infective larvae (L3) develop inside slug intermediate hosts. Upon host ingestion, L3 larvae penetrate the intestinal tract and migrate to the mesenteric arterial circulation. Larvae mature into juvenile and adult worms within tissue.
*   **Sexual Dimorphism & Reproductive Characteristics:** Sexes are separate (dioecious). Fertilized adult females residing in branches of the mesenteric arteries produce eggs in situ within host tissue (e.g., in the wall of the appendix, intestine, or mesenteric vessels).

#### Lifecycle & Transmission Pathways
*   **Definitive Host:** Rodents (primary natural definitive hosts). Naturally endemic infections also occur in non-human primates, raccoons, and opossums (documented in Miami, Florida). Humans serve as accidental, non-permissive hosts.
*   **Intermediate Host(s):** Terrestrial slugs (molluscan intermediate hosts).
*   **Transmission Mode & Ingestion/Penetration:** Acquired via the oral route through accidental ingestion of infective third-stage larvae (L3) in raw or undercooked slugs, or by consuming raw salad greens, unwashed vegetables, or fruits contaminated with small hidden slugs or slug mucous secretions.
*   **Intra-Host Larval Migration Pathway:** Ingested L3 larvae penetrate the gut mucosa, enter mesenteric lymphatics or veins, and travel to the mesenteric arterial branches supplying the ileocecal region, small intestine, and appendix. Worms mature into adults within the mesenteric arterial system and bowel wall. Prepatent period in natural rodent hosts is a few weeks.
*   **Diagnostic Stage & Shedding Site:** Surgical recovery or histopathologic identification of adult worms, larvae, or embedded eggs in tissue biopsy specimens (e.g., appendectomy or surgical bowel resection specimens from the right lower quadrant). Stool O&P examinations for eggs or larvae are unhelpful and usually negative in humans.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Tissue Invasion & Structural Damage:** Adult worm maturation and oviposition within mesenteric arteries cause arterial occlusion, thrombosis, localized ischemic necrosis, mucosal ulceration, transmural inflammation, and heavy eosinophilic granulomatous enteritis or appendicitis.
*   **Mechanical Obstruction:** Dense inflammatory swelling, bowel wall thickening, and pseudotumor granuloma formation in the ileocecal region produce mechanical small bowel obstruction or luminal occlusion of the appendix.
*   **Nutritional Depletion & Anemia:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** N/A
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Pathogenesis is driven by intense host cell-mediated and allergic hypersensitivity reactions (marked tissue and peripheral blood eosinophilia) responding to adult worms, larvae, and eggs trapped within the mesenteric vasculature and intestinal wall.

#### Clinical Syndromes & Complications
*   **Acute Migration Phase Presentation:** Acute presentation typically mimics acute appendicitis or an acute abdomen: sudden onset of severe abdominal pain and tenderness localized to the right lower quadrant, accompanied by high fever, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea.
*   **Chronic Intestinal or Tissue Presentation:** Manifests as abdominal angiostrongyliasis characterized by a palpable, tender, tumor-like inflammatory mass in the right lower quadrant (ileocecal area), prolonged right lower quadrant abdominal pain, low-grade to moderate fever, recurrent diarrhea, vomiting, leukocytosis, and intestinal obstruction. Histopathologic examination reveals severe eosinophilic appendicitis or granulomatous mass lesions.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Palpable right lower quadrant abdominal mass combined with high peripheral blood eosinophilia (up to 60%) and histologic identification of adult female roundworms displaying a multinucleate intestine and uterine eggs inside appendiceal or intestinal wall tissue.
*   **Eosinophilia Profile & IgE Response:** Striking peripheral blood eosinophilia (characteristically up to 60%) accompanied by marked leukocytosis and dense tissue eosinophilic infiltration surrounding intestinal and appendiceal lesions.

#### Epidemiology & Exposures
*   **Geographic Endemicity:** Predominantly endemic in Central America (particularly Costa Rica) and South America. Also present in North America, with documented endemic reservoir infections in wild raccoons, opossums, and non-human primates in Miami, Florida, United States.
*   **Soil & Environmental Requirements:** Requires warm, humid, tropical or subtropical environments supporting dense populations of terrestrial slug intermediate hosts and rodent definitive hosts.
*   **Dietary Exposures & Intermediate Host Foods:** Ingestion of raw unwashed salad vegetables, produce, or fruits contaminated with infected slugs or slug mucus secretions; accidental ingestion of raw or undercooked slugs.
*   **Public Health & Mass Drug Administration:** Prevention relies on food hygiene, washing produce thoroughly before consumption, controlling slug populations in agricultural fields and gardens, and domestic/wild rodent control.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Stool Ova & Parasite Examination:** Stool O&P examination is UNHELPFUL / NEGATIVE in human cases, as eggs and larvae remain trapped within mesenteric vessels and bowel tissue and are rarely passed in feces.
*   **Sedimentation & Flotation Concentration Techniques:** N/A (Fecal concentration procedures are unhelpful/negative).
*   **Serology & Tissue Biopsy Diagnostics:**
    *   *Histopathology (Definitive):* Microscopic examination of appendectomy or intestinal resection specimens reveals cross-sections or longitudinal views of adult female roundworms (displaying a characteristic multinucleate intestine and intrauterine eggs) or larvae embedded in granulomatous lesions with prominent eosinophilic infiltrates.
    *   *Serological Assays:* Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) performed at reference centers aids in presumptive diagnosis.
*   **Imaging Findings:** Abdominal CT or ultrasonography demonstrates localized bowel wall thickening, an inflammatory pseudotumor mass in the right lower quadrant / ileocecal region, appendiceal enlargement, or luminal narrowing.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Anti-Helminthic Agents:** [[Mebendazole]]: 200 to 400 mg orally three times daily (TID) for 10 days (drug of choice).
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Agents:** [[Thiabendazole]]: 75 mg/kg/day in 3 divided doses for 3 days (maximum 3 g/day).
*   **Treatment Nuances & Corticosteroid Protocols:** Anthelmintic administration requires careful clinical monitoring. Concomitant systemic corticosteroids may be added to suppress intense tissue hypersensitivity reactions, diminish inflammatory swelling around dying worms/eggs, and shorten the clinical course of infection. Surgical intervention (appendectomy or partial bowel resection) is frequently performed when patients present with an acute abdomen or suspected tumor prior to definitive parasitic diagnosis.
*   **Prophylaxis, Sanitation & Food Safety:** Thoroughly washing and inspecting all salad greens, fresh vegetables, and fruits prior to consumption; avoiding contact with or ingestion of slugs; controlling slug and rodent populations around gardens and agricultural crops.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Abdominal angiostrongyliasis, *Parastrongylus costaricensis*, palpable right lower quadrant mass, tumor-like mass mimicking appendicitis or carcinoma, female worm in appendix with multinucleate intestine and intrauterine eggs, 60% eosinophilia, slug vector, Miami Florida animal reservoir.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   *Abdominal vs. Cerebral Tropism:* Unlike *[[Angiostrongylus cantonensis]]* (which migrates to the central nervous system to cause eosinophilic meningitis), *Angiostrongylus costaricensis* exhibits strict abdominal/gastrointestinal vascular tropism, migrating to the mesenteric arteries to cause severe ileocecal and appendiceal disease.
    *   *Stool Test Negativity:* Stool O&P tests are consistently negative in humans because eggs deposited in mesenteric vessels hatch into larvae that become trapped and die in intestinal tissue granulomas rather than entering the fecal stream.
    *   *Neoplasm & Appendicitis Mimicry:* Clinical presentation frequently misleads surgeons into suspecting acute bacterial appendicitis or an ileocecal malignancy, leading to exploratory laparotomy and surgical resection.
