### Strongyloides

#### Helminth Anatomy & Ova Morphology
*   **Adult Worm Morphology & Attachment Structures:** Parasitic adults living in the human intestinal lumen are exclusively female. They are small, slender, transparent, threadlike roundworms measuring approximately 2.0 to 2.5 mm in length by 0.034 to 0.040 mm (34–40 µm) in width. The body tapers anteriorly and ends in a short conical tail. The esophagus is elongated, occupying roughly one-quarter of the body length, and possesses a double-bulbed structure divided by a central constriction (or simple tubular structure in parasitic forms). Parasitic females burrow into mucosal crypts and tissue channels beneath the villi of the duodenum and upper jejunum; they lack chitinous biting teeth, cutting plates, or suckers. Free-living female adult worms residing in damp soil are smaller and thicker (1.0 mm × 0.05 mm) with a double-bulbed muscular esophagus; free-living soil adult males measure 0.7 mm in length by 0.035 mm in width with a curved tail.
*   **Ova / Egg Microscopic Features:** Gravid parasitic female uteri contain up to 50 thin-shelled, transparent, oval eggs measuring 50 to 58 µm in length by 30 to 34 µm in width (generally slightly smaller than hookworm eggs). Eggs hatch almost immediately within the intestinal mucosal wall or lumen, liberating first-stage larvae. Consequently, unhatched eggs are exceptionally rare in human stool O&P examinations (except in *Strongyloides fuelleborni* / *S. fuelleborni kellyi* infections, where thin-shelled, unhatched eggs measuring 50–58 × 30–34 µm or 70 × 40 µm are routinely passed in feces).
*   **Larval Stage Morphologies:**
    *   *Rhabditiform Larva (L1 - Noninfective Stage):* Measures 180 to 380 µm in length by 14 to 24 µm in width. Key morphologic features differentiating it from hookworm rhabditiform larvae include an extremely short buccal capsule/mouth opening (only a few micrometers long, compared to the long buccal cavity of hookworms) and a prominent, prominent packet of genital primordium cells located midway down the body length. Esophagus exhibits a rhabditiform structure with a distinct posterior bulb.
    *   *Filariform Larva (L3 - Infective Stage):* Long, slender, non-feeding larva measuring up to 600 to 630 µm in length by 16 µm in width. Features a simple, non-bulbed muscular esophagus extending through half the body length and a characteristic notched, split, or bifurcated tail tip (a crucial diagnostic feature distinguishing it from the sharp, pointed tail of hookworm filariform larvae).
*   **Sexual Dimorphism & Reproductive Characteristics:** Intestinal parasitic adults are strictly female and reproduce via parthenogenesis (virgin reproduction without male fertilization). Parasitic males disappear from the intestine shortly after initial infection. Free-living soil generations are dioecious (separate males and females) and reproduce sexually. *Strongyloides* possesses developmental switching between alternative pathways: the homogonic route (direct development of rhabditiform larvae into infective filariform larvae) and the heterogonic route (development into free-living adult males and females in soil to establish soil populations). Developmental direction is governed by anterior chemosensory amphidial neurons (ASF and ASI neurons, homologous to ADF/ASI in *Caenorhabditis elegans*) responding to environmental temperature and moisture cues.

#### Lifecycle & Transmission Pathways
*   **Definitive Host:** Humans (primary host), dogs, cats, chimpanzees, and African non-human primates.
*   **Intermediate Host(s):** N/A (Direct lifecycle with an optional free-living environmental cycle).
*   **Transmission Mode & Ingestion/Penetration:**
    *   *Percutaneous Route (Primary):* Active penetration of exposed human skin (e.g., bare feet walking on wet topsoil) by infective L3 filariform larvae.
    *   *Autoinfection (Internal & External):* First-stage rhabditiform larvae transform into infective L3 filariform larvae while passing through the host gut lumen (internal autoinfection) or on perianal/perineal skin (external autoinfection), penetrating the colonic mucosa or perianal skin to re-enter the venous circulation without ever leaving the host.
    *   *Sexual Transmission:* Transmission via oral-anal sexual contact.
    *   *Transmammary / Lactogenic Route:* Larval shedding in breast milk demonstrated in animal models and strongly implicated for *S. fuelleborni* / *S. fuelleborni kellyi* transmission to nursing human infants.
    *   *Fecal-Oral Route:* Ingestion of food or water contaminated with infective filariform larvae.
*   **Intra-Host Larval Migration Pathway:** Skin-penetrating filariform larvae enter cutaneous blood vessels and lymphatics, pass via the venous system to the right heart, and lodge in pulmonary capillaries. Larvae break out into alveolar spaces, ascend the bronchial tree to the trachea and pharynx, and are swallowed into the gastrointestinal tract. In the small intestine (duodenum and upper jejunum), larvae enter mucosal crypts, molt, and mature into parthenogenetic adult females within 2 weeks. *Alternative Non-Canonical Pathways:* Research demonstrates that autoinfective filariform larvae can migrate directly through abdominal viscera and peritoneal tissues to reach the duodenum without making an obligatory pulmonary circuit. Prepatent period (time from skin entry to passage of rhabditiform larvae in stool) is approximately 25 to 30 days (1 month).
*   **Diagnostic Stage & Shedding Site:** First-stage rhabditiform (L1) larvae passed in stool/feces represent the primary diagnostic stage. In hyperinfection or heavy infections, filariform larvae, adult females, and unhatched eggs appear in stool, sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid, tracheal aspirates, duodenal aspirates, CSF, urine, and peritoneal fluid. In *S. fuelleborni*, unhatched eggs in stool are the main diagnostic stage.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Tissue Invasion & Structural Damage:** Skin invasion causes petechial micro-hemorrhages, cutaneous congestion, edema, and intense local itching. Pulmonary breakout damages capillary walls and alveolar septa, causing intra-alveolar hemorrhage, leukocyte infiltration, bronchial epithelial invasion, and lobular consolidation. In the small intestine, female worms burrowing through tissue channels beneath mucosal villi cause villous flattening and atrophy, crypt proliferation, mucosal edema, focal ulcerations, and transmural eosinophilic granulomatous inflammation. In hyperinfection, massive larval migration through the colonic and small intestinal wall produces extensive mucosal sloughing, severe ulceration, necrotizing jejunitis, intestinal wall perforation, paralytic ileus, and generalized peritonitis.
*   **Mechanical Obstruction:** High worm burdens, transmural intestinal wall inflammation, severe mucosal edema, and paralytic ileus in hyperinfection syndrome cause mechanical small bowel obstruction and acute surgical abdomen.
*   **Nutritional Depletion & Anemia:** Severe mucosal disruption impairs nutrient absorption, producing severe malabsorption syndrome, chronic watery diarrhea, fluid and electrolyte depletion (severe hypokalemia), and protein-losing enteropathy resulting in profound hypoproteinemia/hypoalbuminemia, generalized peripheral edema, ascites, and extreme emaciation ("swollen belly syndrome" in infants).
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** Autoinfection allows *Strongyloides* to sustain low-level, asymptomatic, persistent infections within host mucosal crypts and tissues for decades (documented to persist up to 65 years) without requiring external soil reinfection. The parasite undergoes developmental switching via amphidial neuron environmental sensing and maintains dormant reservoirs of third-stage tissue larvae or postreproductive mucosal females that periodically reactivate during host immunosuppression.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Host defense depends on cell-mediated immunity, Th2-skewed helper [[T Cell|T-cell]] activation, IL-4, IL-5, IL-9, and IL-13 production, eosinophil recruitment, mucosal mast cell expansion, and parasite-specific serum IgE elevation. Suppression of cell-mediated immunity triggers unbridled autoinfection, resulting in life-threatening Strongyloides Hyperinfection Syndrome and Disseminated Strongyloidiasis. Key predisposing conditions include:
    1.  *Systemic Corticosteroids:* The single most important trigger; even short courses (<10 days) of high- or low-dose glucocorticoids can precipitate fatal hyperinfection.
    2.  *[[HTLV]]-1 Coinfection:* Human T-lymphotropic virus type 1 co-infection inhibits IgE production and Th2 responses, causing high rates of anthelmintic treatment failure and severe hyperinfection.
    3.  *Transplantation:* Solid organ (kidney, liver, heart, lung, pancreas) and [[bone marrow]] transplant recipients experience severe reactivation or donor-derived hyperinfection under immunosuppressants (cyclosporin A, tacrolimus, steroids).
    4.  *Malignancies:* Hematologic malignancies (lymphomas, leukemias, Adult T-cell leukemia-lymphoma (AZTLL)).
    5.  *Other Risk Factors:* Malnutrition, alcoholism, diabetes mellitus, chronic renal failure, hypogammaglobulinemia, and chemotherapy (vinca alkaloids).
    *   *[[HIV]]/AIDS Exception:* Current epidemiology indicates [[HIV]] infection is NOT an independent risk factor for hyperinfection or dissemination (CDC and WHO removed strongyloidiasis from the list of AIDS-defining opportunistic infections in 1987).
    *   *Bacterial Sepsis Link:* Filariform larvae penetrating the bowel wall carry enteric mucosal bacteria (e.g., *[[Escherichia coli]]*, *Klebsiella*, *Enterococcus*) attached to their cuticles or inside their GI tract into the bloodstream and organs, causing recurrent polymicrobial Gram-negative bacteremia, septic shock, and purulent bacterial meningitis.

#### Clinical Syndromes & Complications
*   **Acute Migration Phase Presentation:** Percutaneous entry causes localized pruritic, erythematous papular skin eruptions at penetration sites (lasting ~3 weeks). Pulmonary migration produces dry cough, sore throat, asthmatic wheezing, dyspnea, fever, and transient lung infiltrates (Löffler syndrome). Early intestinal entry presents with epigastric distress, right lower quadrant abdominal pain, nausea, and watery diarrhea alternating with constipation.
*   **Chronic Intestinal or Tissue Presentation:** Uncomplicated chronic infections are often asymptomatic or manifest with intermittent epigastric/right upper quadrant pain (mimicking peptic ulcer disease), nausea, vomiting, chronic diarrhea, constipation, loud bowel gurgling (borborygmi), anal pruritus, recurrent urticaria, recurrent asthma, and strongyloides-related glomerulonephritis / nephrotic syndrome (which remits following anthelmintic cure).
*   **Larva Currens ("Racing Larva"):** Pathognomonic cutaneous manifestation of autoinfection. Characterized by rapidly advancing, intensely pruritic, linear, erythematous, urticarial tracks starting near the perianal/buttock region and extending down the thighs or across the trunk. Tracks move at remarkable speeds (2 to 10 cm/hour), feature a red flare with non-indurated borders, last for a few hours (12 to 18 hours) before disappearing, and recur intermittently over decades (documented up to 65 years).
*   **Hyperinfection Syndrome & Disseminated Strongyloidiasis:** Massive acceleration of the autoinfective cycle leading to overwhelming larval dissemination:
    *   *Gastrointestinal:* Severe abdominal colic, abdominal distension, profuse watery diarrhea, persistent vomiting, hematemesis, mucosal ulceration, necrotizing jejunitis, paralytic ileus, bowel obstruction, acute abdomen, and peritonitis.
    *   *Pulmonary:* Dyspnea, cough, hemoptysis, choking sensation, pleuritic chest pain, hoarseness, focal or bilateral ground-glass pulmonary infiltrates, diffuse alveolar hemorrhage, ARDS, and respiratory failure.
    *   *Dermatologic:* Perianal purpuric linear streaks, petechial and purpuric rashes across the lower trunk, abdomen, thighs, and buttocks ("thumbprint purpura").
    *   *Disseminated Organ Invasion:* Larval invasion of the brain (meningitis, brain abscess, myelitis), liver (elevated enzymes, hepatomegaly), gallbladder, heart (myocarditis, atrial fibrillation), kidneys, pancreas, and skeletal muscle (necrotizing myositis).
    *   *Infectious Complications:* Refractory septic shock, lactic acidosis, and polymicrobial Gram-negative sepsis or meningitis.
*   **Swollen Belly Syndrome:** Caused by *Strongyloides fuelleborni kellyi* in infants (2 to 6 months of age) in Papua New Guinea; presents with severe abdominal distension, mild diarrhea, protein-losing enteropathy, profound hypoproteinemia/hypoalbuminemia without proteinuria, generalized edema, ascites, and high mortality.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Serpiginous urticarial linear cutaneous tracks moving up to 10 cm/hour originating from the perianal area (*larva currens*); visible winding tracks of bacterial colonies formed on agar plate cultures as larvae crawl across the agar surface ("agar plate tracks").
*   **Eosinophilia Profile & IgE Response:** Moderate to high peripheral blood eosinophilia (50% to 75%, or up to 10–12 × 10^9/L) accompanied by elevated total serum IgE is classic in chronic uncomplicated strongyloidiasis. *Critical Prognostic Exception:* In severe Hyperinfection Syndrome or Disseminated Strongyloidiasis under corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy, peripheral blood eosinophilia completely DISAPPEARS (eosinopenia), signaling profound cell-mediated immune collapse and serving as an ominous prognostic indicator.

#### Epidemiology & Exposures
*   **Geographic Endemicity:** Cosmopolitan distribution, with an estimated global burden of 386 to 614 million infected individuals. Highly endemic throughout tropical and subtropical regions of South America (Amazon Basin), Central America, Southeast Asia (Thailand, Laos, Vietnam), China, Sub-Saharan Africa, and Papua New Guinea. Endemic foci persist in temperate zones, including parts of North America (southeastern United States, Appalachian region, and as far north as New York City) and southern Europe. High prevalence historically documented among World War II Far East ex-prisoners of war and Vietnam War veterans.
*   **Soil & Environmental Requirements:** Requires warm, damp, shady soil contaminated with human or animal feces. Larvae cannot tolerate desiccation, direct sunlight, or temperatures below 8°C or above 40°C. Heterogonic free-living sexual development predominates in moist, warm tropical climates, whereas homogonic direct development predominates in cooler temperate zones.
*   **Dietary Exposures & Intermediate Host Foods:** N/A (Transmission occurs via percutaneous larval entry, fecal-oral contamination, or autoinfection; no intermediate host or dietary food vector required).
*   **Public Health & Mass Drug Administration:** High prevalence occurs in institutionalized populations (mental health facilities, day-care centers), overcrowded rural communities with poor sanitation, and organ transplant networks. Mandatory pre-immunosuppression screening (serology + agar plate culture/stool PCR) is required for all patients from endemic areas prior to initiating systemic corticosteroids, chemotherapy, or antirejection therapy for organ transplantation. Universal contact precautions (gloves, gowns) are mandatory for healthcare workers caring for hyperinfection patients due to infectious filariform larvae present in stool, saliva, vomitus, sputum, and body fluids.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Stool Ova & Parasite Examination:** Microscopic identification of motile rhabditiform larvae (180–380 × 14–24 µm) in concentrated stool specimens. A single routine stool O&P examination is notoriously insensitive (~30% sensitivity) due to low and irregular larval excretion in "showers." A minimum of 3 to 4 consecutive daily stool samples must be examined before ruling out infection.
*   **Sedimentation & Flotation Concentration Techniques:** Formalin-ethyl acetate sedimentation concentration (centrifugation at 500 × g for 10 minutes) is standard. The Baermann funnel gauze concentration method utilizes active larval migration into warm water and is highly effective for processing large stool volumes. Harada-Mori filter paper culture, charcoal culture (incubated at 26°C for 1 week), and petri dish filter paper/slant culture allow larval hatching and concentration.
*   **Agar Plate Culture:** The diagnostic method of choice / gold standard for fecal larval detection (significantly more sensitive than routine O&P). Approximately 2 g of fresh stool is placed in the center of an agar plate (agar dried for 4–5 days), sealed with tape to prevent laboratory infection, and held for 2 days at room temperature (26–33°C). As larvae crawl across the agar, they carry enteric bacteria, creating visible, random tracks of bacterial colonies. Plates are examined microscopically for tracks and larvae, rinsed with 10% formalin, centrifuged, and wet mounts examined under 10× and 40× objectives to confirm rhabditiform or filariform larval morphology.
*   **Duodenal Sampling:** Entero-Test (string capsule test) or endoscopic duodenal aspirates/biopsies recover larvae and occasionally adult female worms or thin-shelled eggs in duodenal fluid when stool examinations remain negative.
*   **Serology & Tissue Biopsy Diagnostics:**
    *   *Serological Assays:* Crude larval antigen ELISA (CrAg-ELISA, 94.6% sensitivity; used at CDC), commercial IgG ELISAs (InBios Strongyloides Detect IgG ELISA, SciMedx ELISA), Luciferase Immunoprecipitation System (LIPS targeting 31-kDa recombinant NIE and SsIR antigens), and Immunoblot (98% specificity, ~95% sensitivity). Serology is the optimal screening test for at-risk patients prior to immunosuppression. *Diagnostic Limitation:* Cross-reacts with filarial nematodes and hookworms, and cannot differentiate past cured infection from active ongoing infection.
    *   *Molecular Diagnostics:* Real-time PCR / NAATs targeting 18S rRNA, 28S rRNA, or cytochrome c oxidase subunit I gene in fecal and urine samples.
    *   *Sputum / Respiratory / Body Fluid Microscopy:* Direct wet mounts, Giemsa, Gram, or Papanicolaou stains of sputum, BAL fluid, tracheal aspirates, or thoracic fluid reveal coiled filariform or rhabditiform larvae in pulmonary hyperinfection.
    *   *Histopathology:* Biopsy of duodenal, colonic, pulmonary, or skin tissue demonstrates rhabditiform/filariform larvae or adult females in mucosal crypts or lamina propria, accompanied by villous atrophy or transmural eosinophilic granulomas.
*   **Imaging Findings:** Chest radiography and high-resolution CT (HRCT) reveal focal or bilateral ground-glass opacities, interstitial/alveolar infiltrates, interlobular septal thickening, early honeycombing, or diffuse bilateral infiltrates from alveolar hemorrhage. Abdominal CT/plain films show small bowel loop dilatation, intestinal wall thickening, mucosal fold coarsening (mimicking Crohn's disease or peptic ulcer disease), paralytic ileus, or pancolitis.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Anti-Helminthic Agents:** [[Ivermectin]] (200 µg/kg single oral daily dose for 1 to 2 days for uncomplicated intestinal strongyloidiasis; or 200 µg/kg daily continued for up to 14 days / until stool and culture remain negative for 2 consecutive weeks in Hyperinfection Syndrome or Disseminated Strongyloidiasis).
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Agents:** [[Albendazole]] (400 mg orally twice daily for 3 to 7 days, or 400 mg once daily for 10 days), [[Mebendazole]] (less effective due to poor systemic absorption), or [[Thiabendazole]] (25 mg/kg twice daily for 2 days; highly effective but limited by high toxicity including severe nausea, foul-smelling urine, neuropsychiatric effects, dizziness, and malaise). Moxidectin and Tribendimidine are under clinical investigation.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Corticosteroid Protocols:**
    *   *Route & Special Formulations:* In severe hyperinfection with paralytic ileus, severe vomiting, or impaired oral absorption, rectal administration of [[ivermectin]] or parenteral formulations have been successfully utilized.
    *   *[[HTLV|HTLV-1]] Co-infection Nuance:* Patients co-infected with [[HTLV|HTLV-1]] frequently experience treatment failure or relapse with standard short-course anthelmintic regimens, requiring extended or repeated therapeutic courses.
    *   *Mandatory Pre-Steroid Screening Protocol:* MANDATORY screening (serology plus agar plate stool culture or stool PCR) must be performed on ALL patients with a history of residence in or travel to endemic areas BEFORE initiating systemic corticosteroids, chemotherapy, or immunosuppressive therapy for organ transplantation. If urgent immunosuppression is required before screening results are available, empiric [[ivermectin]] therapy must be administered. In hyperinfection triggered by steroids, corticosteroids should be rapidly tapered or discontinued if clinically possible, combined with broad-spectrum empiric IV antibiotics (e.g., [[Cefepime]], [[vancomycin]]) to cover enteric bacterial sepsis.
*   **Prophylaxis, Sanitation & Food Safety:** Sanitary disposal of human excreta, wearing shoes/sandals to prevent percutaneous skin penetration, avoiding skin contact with wet topsoil or surface water, strict hand hygiene, donor and recipient screening in organ transplantation, and enforcing contact precautions (gloves, gowns) in healthcare settings caring for hyperinfection patients.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Human [[Enterobius vermicularis|threadworm]], *larva currens* ("racing larva", moving 2–10 cm/h), internal and external autoinfection, Strongyloides hyperinfection syndrome, short buccal capsule, large genital primordium, split/notched tail tip of filariform larva, agar plate tracks, "swollen belly syndrome" (*S. fuelleborni kellyi*), thumbprint purpura, eosinopenia in severe hyperinfection, 65-year persistent infection.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   *Parthenogenesis & Autoinfection:* *Strongyloides stercoralis* is the ONLY major human intestinal nematode capable of reproducing via parthenogenesis (virgin female reproduction without males) inside the human host AND undergoing continuous internal autoinfection without leaving the body, allowing individual infections to persist for over 65 years.
    *   *Larval Diagnostic Stage:* Unlike most intestinal nematodes (*Ascaris*, *Trichuris*, hookworms) where eggs are shed in feces, *S. stercoralis* eggs hatch inside the intestinal mucosa; thus, *larvae* (not eggs) are the primary diagnostic stage found in stool O&P examinations.
    *   *Eosinophilia Inversion:* While chronic strongyloidiasis presents with marked peripheral eosinophilia (up to 75%), severe life-threatening hyperinfection syndrome causes total disappearance of eosinophils (eosinopenia) due to corticosteroid- or sepsis-induced immune suppression, turning a classic diagnostic marker into an absent finding.
    *   *Transmammary Transmission:* *Strongyloides fuelleborni kellyi* in Papua New Guinea is transmitted to infants via breast milk or contaminated string carrier bags, causing fatal "swollen belly syndrome" with heavy egg shedding in stool (unlike *S. stercoralis*).
