### Hepatitis B Virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:**
    - Small, circular, partially double-stranded DNA genome approximately 3.2 kb (3200 base pairs) in length (ranging between 3182 and 3200 bp).
    - Composed of a full-length negative-sense DNA strand (L or long strand, 3.2 kb, complementary to all viral mRNAs) and a shorter, variable-length positive-sense DNA strand (S or short strand, 50% to 80% of full unit length).
    - Contains two 11-bp direct repeat sequences (DR1 and DR2) at the 5' extremities of the minus and plus strands, essential for viral replication and genome integration.
    - Organized into four overlapping, in-frame open reading frames (ORFs) encoding seven distinct polypeptides:
        - **S gene (pre-S1, pre-S2, S):** Contains three in-frame initiation codons, encoding the envelope glycoproteins: Large HBsAg (pre-S1 + pre-S2 + S), Middle HBsAg (pre-S2 + S), and Small HBsAg (S).
        - **C gene (pre-C, core):** Contains two in-frame initiation codons, encoding the core structural antigen (HBcAg) and the precore polypeptide (processed and secreted as soluble HBeAg).
        - **P gene:** Encodes the large viral polymerase protein (832 amino acids), which possesses four functional domains: terminal protein domain (functioning as a protein primer), spacer region, reverse transcriptase / DNA polymerase domain, and Ribonuclease H (RNase H) domain.
        - **X gene:** Encodes the X protein (HBx; 154 amino acids, 17 kDa), a non-structural transcriptional transactivator and protein kinase that regulates cccDNA transcription, promotes viral replication, and drives host hepatocarcinogenesis.
    - Classified within the family Hepadnaviridae, genus Orthohepadnavirus.
    - Divided into 10 distinct genotypes (clades A through J) exhibiting sequence variation exceeding 8% across the genome, with distinct geographical distributions, clinical courses, and treatment responses:
        - **Genotype A:** Prevalent in North America, Northern Europe, India, and Africa; exhibits higher rates of sustained response to interferon therapy and HBsAg clearance.
        - **Genotypes B and C:** Prevalent in East and Southeast Asia. Genotype B is associated with earlier spontaneous HBeAg seroconversion and lower rates of cirrhosis/HCC. Genotype C is associated with higher HBeAg prevalence, delayed seroconversion, more active chronic hepatitis, and higher risk of hepatocellular carcinoma.
        - **Genotype D:** Prevalent in Southern Europe, the Middle East, India, and the Mediterranean; associated with higher rates of pre-core mutations (G1896A) and acute fulminant hepatitis.
        - **Genotype E:** Prevalent in West Africa.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:**
    - Icosahedral nucleocapsid core measuring 27 to 28 nm in diameter (18-nm nucleocapsid core structure).
    - Formed by the self-assembly of the hepatitis B core antigen (HBcAg; 183 amino acids, ~21 kDa).
    - The core shell encapsulates the circular partially double-stranded viral DNA genome, the viral P protein (polymerase/reverse transcriptase), and an intrinsic protein kinase.
    - Soluble hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) is a distinct non-structural protein product of the pre-C/C ORF; unlike HBcAg, it contains an N-terminal precore sequence signal peptide, is targeted to the rough endoplasmic reticulum, undergoes cleavage, and is secreted into serum as a 15-18 kDa dimer that does not assemble into capsid structures.
*   **Envelope Properties:**
    - Enveloped virus consisting of a host-derived lipid bilayer membrane embedded with viral-encoded envelope glycoprotein complexes (HBsAg).
    - Contains three distinct envelope glycoprotein forms translated from overlapping transcripts in the S gene:
        - **Small HBsAg (S / major glycoprotein; p23/gp27, 226 aa):** The most abundant envelope component; self-assembles into 22-nm non-infectious subviral spherical particles.
        - **Middle HBsAg (M; gp33/gp37, 281 aa):** Contains pre-S2 and S domains.
        - **Large HBsAg (L; gp39/gp42, 389-400 aa):** Contains pre-S1, pre-S2, and S domains. The N-terminal pre-S1 domain mediates binding to the host cell receptor (NTCP) and is present on intact, infectious virions (Dane particles) and filamentous forms.
    - [[Antigens, Immunogens, and Epitopes|Antigenic Determinants]]: All HBsAg particles contain a common, group-specific conformational antigen determinant designated **a** (located in the outer M/S loop), plus two pairs of mutually exclusive subdeterminants (**d** or **y**, and **w** or **r**). This yields four primary antigenic phenotypes: *adw* (predominant in the United States), *ayw*, *adr*, and *ayr*. Antibody against the group-specific **a** determinant confers protection across all subtypes.
    - Physical Stability: Unusually stable for an enveloped virus due to extensive disulfide cross-linking within surface glycoproteins. Resists ether, non-ionic detergents, low pH (stable at pH 2.4 for up to 6 hours, though infectivity is lost), freezing, and moderate heat (stable at 37°C for 60 minutes and remains viable after drying at 25°C for at least 1 week). Inactivated by sodium hypochlorite (0.5% / 1:10 dilution of chlorine bleach within 3 minutes), high heat (100°C for 1 minute or 60°C for 10 hours), and autoclaving. HBsAg is resistant to ultraviolet irradiation.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:**
    - Displays three distinct morphological forms visible on negative-stain electron microscopy in infected serum:
        - **Dane Particle (Complete virion):** Spherical, 42-nm enveloped infectious virion composed of a 27-28 nm icosahedral core shell encapsulating the viral genome and polymerase, surrounded by an HBsAg-bearing lipid envelope.
        - **Spherical Subviral Particles:** Non-infectious 20 to 22-nm pleomorphic spherical particles composed exclusively of HBsAg (primarily S protein) and host lipids without a nucleocapsid core. Overproduced up to 10,000 to 100,000-fold relative to Dane particles.
        - **Filamentous / Tubular Subviral Particles:** Non-infectious filaments measuring 20 to 22 nm in diameter and 100 to 700 nm in length, composed of HBsAg (S, M, and L forms) and host lipids lacking nucleocapsids.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:**
    - **Structural Proteins:**
        - **Small HBsAg (S; p23/gp27):** Major surface glycoprotein; forms 22-nm non-infectious subviral particles and envelope spikes.
        - **Middle HBsAg (M; gp33/gp37):** Surface glycoprotein containing pre-S2 and S domains.
        - **Large HBsAg (L; gp39/gp42):** Surface glycoprotein containing pre-S1, pre-S2, and S domains; essential for binding to the host hepatocyte receptor (NTCP) and viral assembly.
        - **HBcAg (Core protein; p21, 183 aa):** Major internal structural capsid protein forming the icosahedral nucleocapsid core.
    - **Non-Structural & Enzymatic Proteins:**
        - **Pol / P Protein (Large Polymerase; 832 aa):** Multi-functional enzyme containing terminal protein, spacer, reverse transcriptase / DNA-dependent DNA polymerase, and RNase H domains.
        - **HBeAg (Pre-core/Core product; p15-18):** Soluble secreted protein; serves as a serum biomarker for active viral replication, high infectivity, and high viral load.
        - **HBx (X protein; p17, 154 aa):** Non-structural transcriptional transactivator and protein kinase; transactivates viral and host gene promoters, promotes cccDNA transcription, regulates cell proliferation, and plays a major role in hepatocarcinogenesis.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:**
    - **Attachment:** Initial low-affinity attachment occurs via interaction between HBsAg and cell-surface heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) on hepatocytes in the Space of Disse. High-affinity, specific receptor binding is mediated by the N-terminal pre-S1 domain of the Large HBsAg glycoprotein binding to **sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP)**, a sodium/bile acid cotransporter expressed on the basolateral membrane of hepatocytes.
    - **Tropism:** Strict species and tissue tropism for human and chimpanzee hepatocytes in the liver. Minor extrahepatic replication occurs in kidneys and pancreas.
*   **Entry Mechanism:**
    - Following specific binding to NTCP, the virus enters hepatocytes via receptor-mediated endocytosis (clathrin-dependent or caveolin-mediated pathway), followed by fusion of the viral envelope with endosomal/cellular membranes to release the nucleocapsid core into the cytoplasm.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:**
    - The icosahedral nucleocapsid core is transported across the cytosol along the microtubule network to the nuclear pore complex.
    - Uncoating occurs at the nuclear pore, releasing the relaxed circular partially double-stranded DNA (rcDNA) genome into the nucleus.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:**
    - **cccDNA Formation:** In the nucleus, host cellular DNA repair machinery repairs the single-stranded gap in the (+) DNA strand, removes the covalently attached P protein primer from the (-) strand and RNA primer from the (+) strand, and ligates both strands to convert rcDNA into **covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA)**. The cccDNA persists in the nucleus as a stable episomal viral minichromosome complexed with host histones.
    - **Transcription:** Host cell RNA polymerase II transcribes the nuclear cccDNA template into six overlapping viral messenger RNAs (mRNAs), all sharing a common 3' polyadenylation site:
        - **3.5 kb Pregenomic RNA (pgRNA) & Pre-C mRNA:** Larger-than-genome transcripts. Pre-C mRNA translates the precore p25 precursor (secreted as HBeAg). pgRNA serves as the template for reverse transcription into genomic DNA and translates HBcAg (core) and P protein (polymerase).
        - **2.4 kb mRNA:** Translates Large HBsAg (pre-S1 + pre-S2 + S).
        - **2.1 kb mRNA:** Translates Middle HBsAg (pre-S2 + S) and Small HBsAg (S).
        - **0.7-0.9 kb mRNA:** Translates the HBx protein.
    - **Translation:** Processed viral mRNAs are exported to the cytoplasm and translated on host ribosomes.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:**
    - **Encapsidation:** In the cytoplasm, newly synthesized P protein binds to a specific stem-loop structure (epsilon, ε) located at the 5' end of the 3.5 kb pgRNA template, triggering packaging into a nascent core capsid assembled from HBcAg dimers.
    - **Reverse Transcription:** Within the assembling core particle, the reverse transcriptase activity of P protein utilizes its terminal protein domain as a primer to synthesize a negative-sense (-) DNA strand using pgRNA as a template. Concurrently, the RNase H activity of P protein degrades the pgRNA template, leaving a short 5' RNA fragment to serve as primer for positive-sense (+) DNA synthesis. Plus-strand DNA synthesis is initiated but terminates prematurely (reaching 50% to 80% length) upon envelopment.
    - **Nuclear Recycling vs. Egress:** Immature cores containing rcDNA follow two pathways:
        - *Nuclear Recycling:* Re-enter the nucleus to amplify and maintain the intranuclear cccDNA minichromosome pool.
        - *Egress:* Bud into multivesicular bodies and pre-Golgi membranes enriched with HBsAg glycoproteins (Large, Middle, Small), acquiring a lipid envelope.
    - **Egress:** Mature enveloped Dane particles and excess non-infectious 22-nm subviral particles (spheres and filaments) are released into the bloodstream via exocytosis without lysing the host hepatocyte.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:**
    - HBV is a non-cytopathic virus. High levels of active viral replication and antigen expression occur within hepatocytes without inducing direct viral cell lysis, cell death, or tissue necrosis.
    - Liver inflammation, hepatocellular necrosis, and clinical hepatitis are caused almost exclusively by host cell-mediated immune responses (specifically CD8+ cytotoxic [[T-Cell|T lymphocytes]] and CD4+ Th1 cells) targeting viral antigens (HBcAg and HBsAg) presented on [[MHC-1|MHC class I]] molecules on infected hepatocytes.
    - **Ground-Glass Hepatocytes:** Pathognomonic histological lesion of chronic HBV infection, characterized by hepatocytes with swollen, fine, eosinophilic, granular cytoplasm caused by massive intracellular accumulation and retention of HBsAg filaments in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:**
    - Establishes lifelong nuclear latency/persistence via **cccDNA** episomes in host hepatocyte nuclei and integrated viral DNA in host chromosomes.
    - **Reactivation Triggers:** Immunosuppressive therapy (such as systemic corticosteroids, anti-CD20 monoclonal [[Antibodies, Immunoglobulins|antibodies]] like rituximab, cancer chemotherapy, organ transplantation) or coinfection with [[HIV]]/[[HCV]] can trigger massive viral reactivation. Reactivation presents with sudden surges in serum HBV DNA, re-emergence of HBeAg, marked ALT elevations, and potential fulminant hepatic failure.
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:**
    - A primary etiologic agent of **Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC / Primary Hepatocellular Carcinoma / PHC)** worldwide (accounting for 250,000 to over 800,000 deaths annually).
    - Operates via both indirect and direct oncogenic pathways:
        - **Indirect Mechanism:** Chronic necroinflammation, continual [[T-cell]]-mediated hepatocyte destruction, and ongoing compensatory liver cell regeneration/hyperplasia drive accumulation of genetic mutations, tissue scarring, fibrosis, and cirrhosis.
        - **Direct Mechanism (HBx Protein):** The HBx protein functions as a potent transcriptional transactivator and protein kinase; it transactivates host growth-promoting genes, binds and inactivates the tumor suppressor p53, disrupts cell cycle checkpoints, inhibits apoptosis, and stimulates error-prone DNA repair.
        - **Direct Mechanism (Genomic Integration):** Linearized HBV DNA integrates randomly into host hepatocyte chromosomal DNA (frequently near DR1/DR2 sites). While integrated DNA cannot support viral replication, it induces host chromosomal rearrangements, deletions, microdeletions, places viral promoters (LTR-like) upstream of host proto-oncogenes, and drives persistent expression of HBsAg and HBx.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:**
    - **Immunological Decoy (HBsAg Overproduction):** Massive excess of non-infectious 22-nm spherical and subviral HBsAg particles secreted into blood (outnumbering Dane particles up to 100,000:1) binds and absorbs host neutralizing anti-HBs [[Antibodies, Immunoglobulins|antibodies]], preventing antibody-mediated clearance of circulating virions.
    - **Inhibition of Type I Interferon:** HBx and core proteins inhibit host interferon-alpha ([[Type I IFNs|IFN-α]]) and interferon-beta ([[Type I IFNs|IFN-β]]) transcription and block downstream cellular antiviral signaling pathways.
    - **HBeAg Immunotolerance:** Soluble HBeAg crosses the placenta in utero and induces systemic T-cell tolerance / clonal deletion of core/precore-reactive T helper cells in the fetus/neonate, establishing chronic infection in >90% of perinatally infected infants.
    - **[[T-Cell]] Exhaustion:** Persistent high viral antigen loads during chronic infection cause functional exhaustion of [[CD8+ Cytotoxic T Cell|CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes]]  (upregulation of [[T-Cell Co-stimulation and Inhibition|PD-1]] and [[T-Cell Co-stimulation and Inhibition|CTLA-4]]), impairing viral clearance.
    - **Pre-Core Stop Codon Mutants:** Emergence of pre-core stop codon mutations (G1896A) prevents HBeAg synthesis while maintaining active viral replication, escaping HBeAg-directed immune surveillance.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:**
    - **Host Defense:** Polyclonally active [[CD8+ Cytotoxic T Cell|CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs)]]  and CD4+ T helper cell responses directed against HBcAg and HBsAg are required for resolution of acute infection. Protective immunity against acquisition is mediated by anti-HBs [[Antibodies, Immunoglobulins|antibodies]] (targeting the group-specific **a** determinant).
    - **Age-Dependent Risk of Chronicity:** Inversely related to host age and immune maturity:
        - Perinatal / Neonatal Transmission (Mothers HBeAg+): **90–95%** progress to chronic infection.
        - Early Childhood Infection (1–5 years): **20–30%** progress to chronic infection.
        - Adult Acquisition (>18 years): **5–10%** progress to chronic infection (90–95% resolve acute infection completely).
    - **Immunodeficiency Links:** Immunocompromised hosts (such as patients with [[HIV]]/AIDS, organ transplant recipients, or patients undergoing hemodialysis) exhibit blunted cell-mediated immune responses resulting in mild or absent acute icteric symptoms but drastically higher rates of chronic carrier state (up to 50% in renal dialysis patients). [[HIV]] coinfection accelerates progression to cirrhosis, end-stage liver disease, and HCC.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:**
    - **Acute Hepatitis B:** Incubation period ranges from 45 to 160 days (mean 60–90 days / 1–4 months). Approximately 65–80% of adult cases are subclinical or anicteric. Symptomatic acute hepatitis (25–35% of adults) presents with an insidious onset over four sequential clinical periods:
        - **Incubation Period:** Asymptomatic; active viral replication establishes high viremia.
        - **Preicteric / Prodromal Phase:** Characterized by constitutional symptoms: low-grade fever, malaise, fatigue, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, right upper quadrant abdominal discomfort/pain, chills, myalgias, and a [[Type III Hypersensitivity|serum sickness]]-like syndrome (fever, urticarial skin rash, and polyarthritis/arthralgias in ~15%).
        - **Icteric Phase:** Characterized by dark yellow/tea-colored urine (bilirubinuria), pale clay-colored stools, overt jaundice (scleral icterus, yellowing of skin), hepatomegaly with right upper quadrant tenderness, pruritus/itching (~10%), and markedly elevated serum aminotransferases (ALT and AST often >1,000 to >2,000 IU/L).
        - **Convalescent Phase:** Gradual resolution of jaundice, fever, and anorexia, with restoration of normal liver function over weeks to months.
    - **Chronic Hepatitis B:** Defined as persistence of HBsAg in serum for **6 months or longer** (with or without HBeAg). Affects 257 million people globally.
        - Comprises four distinct clinical/serological phases:
            1. *Immune Tolerant Phase:* HBeAg positive, extremely high serum HBV DNA, normal ALT, minimal liver inflammation/fibrosis (typical in perinatally infected individuals, lasting 10–30 years).
            2. *Immune Active / Clearance Phase (HBeAg+ Chronic Hepatitis):* HBeAg positive, high serum HBV DNA, elevated ALT, active chronic necroinflammation, and progressive liver fibrosis; marked by recurrent hepatitis flares.
            3. *Inactive Carrier Phase (Low Replicative):* HBeAg negative, anti-HBe positive, low/undetectable serum HBV DNA (<2,000 IU/mL), normal ALT, minimal inflammation.
            4. *HBeAg-Negative Chronic Hepatitis (Pre-Core / Core Promoter Mutant Phase):* HBeAg negative, anti-HBe positive, fluctuating/high serum HBV DNA, elevated ALT, active chronic hepatitis, and frequent progression to cirrhosis.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:**
    - **Fulminant Hepatitis (Massive Hepatocellular Necrosis):** Severe, life-threatening acute liver failure occurring in 1–2% of icteric acute cases (risk increases 10-fold in coinfection or superinfection with [[HDV]]). Manifests as rapid liver shrinkage, severe jaundice, coagulopathy (INR >1.5, prolonged prothrombin time), ascites, gastrointestinal bleeding, and hepatic encephalopathy (altered mental status, confusion, coma). Mortality exceeds 80% without emergency liver transplantation.
    - **Extrahepatic Manifestations ([[Type III Hypersensitivity]] / [[Type III Hypersensitivity|Immune Complex Disease]]):** Occurs in 10–20% of chronic HBsAg carriers:
        - **Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN):** Severe necrotizing vasculitis of medium-sized arteries driven by circulating HBsAg-anti-HBs immune complexes; presents with fever, hypertension, abdominal pain, renal vasculitis, and peripheral neuropathy.
        - **Membranous Nephropathy / Membranoproliferative Glomerulonephritis:** Glomerular immune complex deposition causing nephrotic syndrome, heavy proteinuria, and renal impairment (especially in children).
        - **[[Type III Hypersensitivity|Serum Sickness]]-Like Prodrome:** Transient fever, maculopapular/urticarial skin rash, and symmetrical polyarthritis during acute prodrome.
        - **Aplastic Anemia:** Rare extrahepatic [[bone marrow]] failure complication.
*   **Incubation Period:**
    - Long and dose-dependent: ranges from 45 to 160 days (1 to 6 months), with an average of 60 to 90 days.
*   **Classic Prodrome:**
    - Insidious prodrome of anorexia, malaise, fatigue, nausea, vomiting, low-grade fever, right upper quadrant discomfort, dark urine, and [[Type III Hypersensitivity|serum sickness]]-like skin rash/arthralgias preceding jaundice by 1 to 2 weeks.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:**
    - N/A (There are no strictly pathognomonic clinical signs; however, the clinical finding of insidious jaundice, right upper quadrant pain, dark urine, [[Type III Hypersensitivity|serum sickness]]-like prodromal rash/arthralgias, and ground-glass hepatocytes on liver biopsy in a patient with parenteral or sexual risk factors is highly suggestive).
*   **Complications & Sequelae:**
    - **Cirrhosis:** Progressive hepatic scarring, lobular disruption, and portal hypertension resulting from chronic active necroinflammation.
    - **Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC):** Primary liver malignancy; 100-fold higher risk in chronic HBsAg carriers.
    - **End-Stage Liver Disease:** Ascites, esophageal variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, and hepato-renal syndrome.
    - **Fulminant Hepatic Failure & Death.**

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:**
    - **Primary Reservoir:** Humans (chronically infected HBsAg carriers, estimated at 257 million globally). Non-human primates (chimpanzees) can be experimentally infected but do not serve as natural reservoirs.
*   **Transmission Route:**
    - **Parenteral / Percutaneous Route:** Inoculation of infected blood, blood products, or body fluids. High-risk exposures include sharing contaminated needles/syringes among people who inject drugs (PWID), occupational needlestick injuries (risk ~30% from HBeAg+ source), unsterile medical equipment, blood transfusions (prior to universal donor screening), tattooing, body piercing, and sharing razors/toothbrushes.
    - **Sexual Contact:** Unprotected heterosexual or male-homosexual intercourse with infected partners. High concentration of virus in blood, semen, vaginal secretions, and saliva.
    - **Perinatal / Vertical Transmission:** Transmission from infected mother to infant during labor and delivery (exposure to maternal blood and cervicovaginal fluids) or in utero (rare) or via breast milk. Rate of infant transmission is 90% from HBeAg-positive mothers vs 10-20% from HBeAg-negative mothers.
    - **Horizontal Transmission:** Close personal contact in households or institutional settings via micro-abrasions, open skin lesions, or mucosal contact with blood/saliva.
    - **Non-Transmissible Routes:** Fecal-oral transmission does NOT occur; casual non-intimate contact does not transmit the virus.
*   **Vectors:**
    - None.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:**
    - **Global Prevalence:** High endemicity (≥8% HBsAg prevalence) in Sub-Saharan Africa, East Asia (China), Southeast Asia, and Pacific Islands; intermediate endemicity (2-7%) in Southern/Eastern Europe, Middle East, and South America; low endemicity (<2%) in North America and Western Europe.
    - **Public Health Elimination Strategy:** WHO global elimination goal aims to reduce chronic HBV prevalence in children under 5 years to <0.1% via universal birth-dose vaccination, routine maternal HBsAg screening, post-exposure prophylaxis for exposed newborns, and blood donor screening.
    - **Isolation / Precautions:** Universal Blood and Body Fluid Precautions in healthcare settings. Wearing gloves, eye protection, and protective gowns when handling blood or body fluids; careful disposal of needles/sharps without recapping. Decontamination of surfaces using 10% household bleach (0.5% sodium hypochlorite). Standard patient isolation is not required.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:**
    - **Ground-Glass Hepatocytes:** Pathognomonic histological feature of chronic HBV infection on liver biopsy, characterized by swollen hepatocytes with diffuse, fine, eosinophilic, granular cytoplasm caused by massive accumulation of HBsAg filaments in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum.
    - **Histological Patterns:** Acute hepatitis shows lobular disarray, hepatocyte swelling (ballooning degeneration), councilman bodies (apoptotic acidophilic bodies), and pericentral mononuclear inflammatory infiltrates. Chronic active hepatitis shows portal/periportal inflammation, interface hepatitis (piecemeal necrosis), bridging fibrosis, and cirrhosis.
    - **Inclusion Bodies:** Nuclear inclusion bodies containing HBcAg can be identified by immunohistochemical staining.
*   **Molecular Testing:**
    - **Quantitative Real-Time [[PCR]] (HBV DNA):** Gold standard for measuring viral load (expressed in IU/mL; 1 IU ~ 5.6 copies). Essential for assessing replicative activity, determining eligibility for antiviral therapy, and monitoring treatment response/suppression. Lower limit of detection is typically 10-15 IU/mL.
    - **Genotypic Testing:** DNA sequencing of P gene to identify HBV genotypes (A-J) and detect drug-resistance mutations (e.g., M204V/I for [[lamivudine]], N236T for [[adefovir]], L180M, etc.).
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:**
    - **Serological Markers:**
        - **HBsAg (Hepatitis B Surface Antigen):** First marker to appear (2-6 weeks post-exposure); indicates current active infection (acute or chronic). Persistence >6 months defines chronic infection.
        - **Anti-HBs (Antibody to HBsAg):** Indicates recovery and immunity (following resolved natural infection or successful vaccination). Protective level is ≥10 mIU/mL.
        - **HBcAg (Hepatitis B Core Antigen):** Intracellular core protein; NOT detected free in serum.
        - **Anti-HBc Total (IgM + IgG):** Indicates past or present infection with HBV. Appears shortly after HBsAg and persists for life.
        - **IgM anti-HBc:** Marker of acute or recent infection (positive for 4-6 months). Primary diagnostic marker during the "window period". Low titers can re-appear during severe chronic flares.
        - **HBeAg (Hepatitis B e Antigen):** Soluble nucleocapsid protein marker of active viral replication, high infectivity, and high viral load.
        - **Anti-HBe (Antibody to HBeAg):** Signals reduction of viral replication, low infectivity, and onset of resolution/inactive state.
    - **The Window Period:** Diagnostic gap during acute resolving hepatitis B when HBsAg has cleared from serum but anti-HBs has not yet risen to detectable levels. **IgM anti-HBc is the sole positive marker** during this period.
    - **Serological Diagnostic Profiles:**
        - *Acute Infection:* HBsAg (+), Total anti-HBc (+), IgM anti-HBc (+), Anti-HBs (-), HBeAg (+).
        - *Resolved / Immune (Natural Infection):* HBsAg (-), Total anti-HBc (+), IgM anti-HBc (-), Anti-HBs (+), HBeAg (-).
        - *Immune (Vaccination):* HBsAg (-), Total anti-HBc (-), Anti-HBs (+), HBeAg (-).
        - *Chronic Infection:* HBsAg (+) for >6 months, Total anti-HBc (+), IgM anti-HBc (-), Anti-HBs (-).
        - *Isolated Anti-HBc Positive:* Four interpretations: remote resolved infection, low-level chronic/occult carrier, window period, or false-positive.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:**
    - Viral culture is extremely difficult, non-routine, and restricted to specialized research settings using primary human hepatocytes or transfected hepatoma cell lines.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:**
    - **First-Line Antiviral Agents (Oral Nucleos(t)ide Analogs & PegIFN):**
        - **[[Tenofovir]] Disoproxil Fumarate (TDF, 300 mg/day) / [[Tenofovir]] Alafenamide (TAF, 25 mg/day):** Nucleotide analog inhibitors of HBV reverse transcriptase / DNA polymerase. Potent viral suppression, very high genetic barrier to resistance. TAF offers improved renal and bone safety profile compared to TDF.
        - **[[Entecavir]] (ETV, 0.5 mg/day naive; 1 mg/day [[lamivudine]]-refractory):** Guanosine nucleoside analog inhibitor of HBV DNA polymerase. High potency and high genetic barrier to resistance in naive patients.
        - **Pegylated Interferon Alfa-2a (PegIFN-α, 180 µg/week SC for 48 weeks):** Immunomodulatory and antiviral cytokine. Finite duration of therapy; higher rates of HBeAg/HBsAg loss in select patients (genotype A), but limited by systemic toxicity.
    - **Second / Third-Line Agents (Older NRTI Agents):**
        - **[[Lamivudine]] (3TC):** Cytosine analog; low genetic barrier (high resistance rate ~70% at 5 years via M204V/I mutation).
        - **[[Adefovir]] Dipivoxil:** Adenine nucleotide analog; second/third-line; risk of nephrotoxicity.
        - **[[Telbivudine]]:** Thymidine nucleoside analog.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:**
    - **[[Lamivudine]] Resistance:** Driven by **M204V/I** (YMDD motif mutation) in the reverse transcriptase domain of the P gene, often accompanied by compensatory L180M mutation.
    - **[[Adefovir]] Resistance:** Driven by **N236T** or A181V mutations.
    - **[[Tenofovir]] & [[Entecavir]]:** Extremely high genetic barrier to resistance; de novo resistance is rare in treatment-naive patients.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:**
    - **Goal of Treatment:** Suppression of HBV DNA to undetectable levels (<10-15 IU/mL), ALT normalization, histological improvement, and prevention of cirrhosis/HCC. Complete cure (loss of cccDNA) is rarely achieved; treatment is usually lifelong.
    - **Cryptococcal / TB / [[HIV]] Coinfection:** In [[HIV]]/HBV coinfection, ARV regimens must incorporate [[tenofovir]] (TDF or TAF) + [[emtricitabine]] (FTC) or [[lamivudine]] (3TC) to treat both pathogens simultaneously and prevent HBV flares.
    - **Pregnancy:** High-risk mothers with HBV DNA >200,000 IU/mL should receive oral TDF starting at 28-32 weeks gestation through 1-3 months postpartum to prevent perinatal transmission.
    - **Acute Hepatitis B:** Antiviral treatment is generally NOT indicated for uncomplicated acute HBV (>95% resolve spontaneously). Reserved for severe/fulminant acute hepatitis B (INR >1.5, severe jaundice, prolonged course) using TDF, TAF, or [[entecavir]].
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:**
    - **Active Immunization (Hepatitis B Vaccines):**
        - **Recombinant Subunit Vaccines (e.g., Recombivax HB, Engerix-B):** Contain purified, yeast-expressed (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) 22-nm HBsAg virus-like particles formulated with alum adjuvant. Non-infectious subunit vaccine.
        - **CpG-Adjuvanted Vaccine (Heplisav-B):** Recombinant HBsAg combined with [[Toll-like receptor]] 9 (TLR9) agonist CpG-ODN adjuvant; 2-dose series given 1 month apart in adults ≥18 years; superior immunogenicity.
        - **Standard Pediatric Schedule:** 3-dose oral/IM series given at birth (within 24 hours), 1-2 months, and 6-18 months.
    - **Passive Immunization (Hepatitis B Immune Globulin / HBIG):** Concentrated human IgG with high-titer anti-HBs. Provides immediate, temporary passive immunity.
    - **Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP):**
        - *Perinatal Exposure (Infants born to HBsAg+ mothers):* Administer single dose of **HBIG (0.5 mL IM)** AND first dose of **HBV vaccine** at separate injection sites strictly within **12 hours of birth**, followed by completion of 3-dose vaccine series. Reduces transmission by 85-95%.
        - *Occupational / Needlestick / Sexual Exposure (Unvaccinated or non-responder):* Administer HBIG (0.06 mL/kg IM) as soon as possible (within 24 hours for needlestick; within 14 days for sexual exposure) plus initiate 3-dose HBV vaccine series.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "Dane particle" (42-nm complete infectious virion).
    - "Australia antigen" (original historical term for HBsAg, discovered by Baruch Blumberg in 1965).
    - "Ground-glass hepatocytes" (pathognomonic liver biopsy finding of smooth ER filled with HBsAg).
    - "cccDNA" (covalently closed circular DNA, stable nuclear episomal minichromosome template).
    - "YMDD motif mutation" (M204V/I reverse transcriptase mutation causing [[lamivudine]] resistance).
    - "Window period" (diagnostic gap where IgM anti-HBc is the sole positive marker).
    - "Pre-core mutant" (G1896A stop codon mutation preventing HBeAg production).
    - "NTCP receptor" (sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide, host hepatocyte receptor).
    - "Immunological decoy" (22-nm spherical/filamentous subviral particles lacking DNA).
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - **The DNA/Reverse Transcriptase Exception:** HBV is a DNA virus (family Hepadnaviridae), yet it replicates via reverse transcription of an RNA intermediate (pgRNA) using an encoded reverse transcriptase, a strategy shared with retroviruses.
    - **Non-Lytic Enveloped Virus:** Most enveloped viruses kill host cells during egress or budding. HBV is completely non-cytopathic; infected hepatocytes continuously secrete virions and excess HBsAg particles via exocytosis without undergoing cell lysis or direct viral cytolysis.
    - **Non-Infectious Subviral Decoys:** HBV produces and secretes vast quantities (up to 100,000-fold excess over Dane particles) of empty, non-infectious 22-nm spherical and filamentous HBsAg subviral particles lacking core capsids or DNA, which function as immunological decoys to absorb host neutralizing [[Antibodies, Immunoglobulins|antibodies]].
    - **Enveloped Virus Environmental Stability:** Despite being an enveloped virus, HBV virions exhibit remarkable resistance to environmental desiccation, low pH, and ambient temperatures, remaining viable on dry surfaces for at least 7 days and resisting standard non-bleach detergents.
    - **The Age-Inversion Rule for Chronicity:** Unlike most viral infections where adults experience more persistent outcomes, HBV infection progression to chronicity is inversely proportional to age at acquisition (90-95% in neonates vs 5-10% in adults).
