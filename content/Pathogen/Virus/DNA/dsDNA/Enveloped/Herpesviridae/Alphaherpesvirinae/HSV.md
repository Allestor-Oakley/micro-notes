### Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1 & HSV-2)

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:**
    - Linear, double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) genome, approximately 150 kbp (125–150 kbp) in overall size.
    - Base composition: high G + C content (67% for HSV-1, 69% for HSV-2).
    - Features a complex sequence arrangement containing unique long (UL) and unique short (US) regions, each bracketed/flanked by direct or inverted terminal and internal repeat sequences.
    - Genome recombination between inverted repeats allows the UL and US regions to invert independently relative to one another, yielding four distinct genomic configurations or isomeric forms.
    - HSV-1 and HSV-2 share substantial sequence organization and exhibit approximately 50% nucleic acid sequence homology across their genomes.
    - Restriction endonuclease cleavage yields characteristically distinct restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) patterns ("fingerprinting"), allowing precise epidemiologic tracing and differentiation of individual viral strains and types.
    - Encodes at least 70 to 80 polypeptides, with roughly half required for viral replication in vitro and the remainder facilitating host cell interaction, tissue tropism, virulence, and immune evasion.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:**
    - Icosahedral (icosadeltahedral) protein coat / capsid measuring 100 nm in diameter (or 125 nm "naked" virion).
    - Constructed from 162 capsomeres (hexagons and pentagons).
    - Surrounds a central toroidal DNA core (DNA genome wrapped tightly around a proteinaceous spool or toroid).
    - The space between the capsid and envelope is filled with an amorphous, proteinaceous matrix called the **tegument**, which contains at least 15 to 20 viral proteins, enzymes, and transcription factors (including VP16 / alpha-TIF and the virion host shutoff protein vhs / UL41).
*   **Envelope Properties:**
    - Spherical, enveloped virion measuring 150 to 200 nm in total diameter.
    - Acquired by budding through the altered host cell inner nuclear membrane and/or trans-Golgi network membranes.
    - Embedded with at least 10 to 12 distinct viral glycoprotein spikes (approximately 8 nm long), including gB, gC, gD, gE, gG, gH, gI, gL, gM, and gN.
    - Contains viral-encoded Fc receptors (gE/gI complex binds the Fc portion of IgG) and complement-binding proteins (gC binds C3b) to facilitate immune evasion.
    - As an enveloped virus, HSV is environmentally fragile and labile: highly sensitive to acid, lipid solvents (ether, chloroform), non-ionic detergents, heat, drying, and standard disinfectants (bleach, 70% ethanol, soap).
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:**
    - Spherical, pleomorphic enveloped particle measuring 150 to 200 nm in total diameter.
    - Exhibits a characteristic four-layered architectural structure: central DNA toroid core, icosahedral capsid (100–125 nm), surrounding amorphous tegument layer, and lipid envelope with outer glycoprotein spikes.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:**
    - **Glycoproteins (Envelope & Surface Spikes):**
        - **gB & gH/gL:** Essential viral fusion machinery; gB acts as the primary fusion protein and gH/gL acts as the fusion trigger. gB also participates in attachment.
        - **gC:** Viral attachment protein; binds cell-surface heparan sulfate proteoglycans. Functions as a complement C3b receptor to inhibit complement activation.
        - **gD:** Primary viral attachment protein and potent inducer of neutralizing antibodies; binds to coreceptors nectin-1, nectin-2, or HveA (HVEM) to trigger membrane fusion.
        - **gE/gI:** Heterodimer complex functioning as an Fc receptor that binds the Fc region of host IgG, shielding infected cells from antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC).
        - **gG:** Type-specific envelope glycoprotein (gG-1 for HSV-1, gG-2 for HSV-2); allows antigenic and serological discrimination between HSV-1 and HSV-2.
    - **Tegument Proteins:**
        - **VP16 (alpha-TIF / UL48):** Transactivator protein that complexes with host cellular transcription factors (Oct-1, HCF-1) to selectively induce transcription of viral immediate-early (alpha) genes.
        - **vhs (UL41):** Virion host shutoff protein; degrades host cellular mRNAs to shut off host protein synthesis and suppress innate immune signaling.
    - **Enzymes & Non-Structural Proteins:**
        - **DNA-Dependent DNA Polymerase (UL30):** Replicates viral genomic DNA; primary target of [[acyclovir]], [[ganciclovir]], [[foscarnet]], and [[cidofovir]].
        - **Thymidine Kinase (TK / UL23):** Scavenging enzyme that monophosphorylates deoxynucleosides (and nucleoside analog drugs like [[acyclovir]]) to facilitate replication in non-dividing cells (neurons).
        - **Ribonucleotide Reductase:** Converts host ribonucleotides to deoxyribonucleotides to support viral DNA synthesis in non-dividing cells.
        - **ICP34.5 (Neurovirulence Factor):** Reverses host translation shutoff induced by protein kinase R (PKR) and type I interferons, antagonizes autophagy, and promotes viral neuroinvasiveness.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:**
    - **Attachment:** Initial rapid adherence occurs via binding of envelope glycoproteins gC and gB to cell-surface glycosaminoglycans, primarily heparan sulfate proteoglycans.
    - **Coreceptor Binding:** High-affinity binding occurs when glycoprotein gD interacts with specific host cell entry receptors:
        - **Nectin-1 and Nectin-2 (HveC / HveB):** Members of the immunoglobulin superfamily (intercellular adhesion molecules) expressed on epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and neurons.
        - **HveA (HVEM / Herpesvirus Entry Mediator):** Member of the tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) family expressed on activated [[T-Cell|T lymphocytes]], neurons, and mucosal epithelial cells.
    - **Tropism:** Broad cellular host range in vitro. In vivo, exhibits dual tropism: lytic/cytolytic tropism for mucoepithelial cells and keratinocytes, and neurotropism for sensory neurons of dorsal root/cranial ganglia.
*   **Entry Mechanism:**
    - Binding of gD to coreceptors triggers a conformational change in gH/gL, activating gB to mediate direct fusion of the viral envelope with the host cell plasma membrane at neutral pH, or entry via endocytosis followed by endosomal membrane fusion.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:**
    - Following membrane fusion, the nucleocapsid and tegument proteins are released directly into the host cell cytoplasm.
    - Tegument protein vhs remains in cytoplasm to degrade host mRNAs, while VP16 and the nucleocapsid interact with host microtubule motors (dynein) for retrograde axonal transport to the nuclear pore complex.
    - The capsid docks at the nuclear pore and injects the linear dsDNA genome into the nucleus.
    - Upon entry into the nucleus, the linear genome immediately circularizes without requiring protein synthesis.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:**
    - Circularized viral DNA is transcribed in the nucleus by host cellular RNA polymerase II in a tightly regulated, sequential cascade of three gene classes:
        - **Immediate-Early (alpha) Genes:** Transcribed first (0–2 hours), stimulated by virion tegument protein VP16. Encodes alpha-proteins (e.g., ICP0, ICP4, ICP27, ICP47) which take over host cellular machinery, suppress host innate responses, and transactivate expression of early genes.
        - **Early (beta) Genes:** Transcribed next (2–8 hours), activated by alpha-proteins. Encodes beta-proteins required for viral DNA replication, including viral DNA polymerase, thymidine kinase, ribonucleotide reductase, and single-stranded DNA-binding proteins.
        - **Late (gamma) Genes:** Transcribed after viral DNA replication begins (>8 hours), divided into gamma-1 (early-late) and gamma-2 (true late) genes. Encodes gamma-proteins comprising structural components (capsid, tegument, envelope glycoproteins).
    - **Replication:** Viral DNA replicates via a **rolling circle mechanism** in the nucleus, generating long, head-to-tail concatemeric dsDNA intermediates.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:**
    - Capsid structural proteins assemble in the nucleus into empty procapsids.
    - Concatemeric viral DNA is site-specifically cleaved into unit-length genomes and packaged ("sucked") into preformed procapsids.
    - Nucleocapsids bud through the inner nuclear membrane into the perinuclear space (acquiring a primary envelope), fuse with the outer nuclear membrane to enter the cytoplasm, acquire tegument proteins in the cytosol, and bud into trans-Golgi network (TGN) vesicles enriched with viral glycoproteins to acquire their final envelope.
    - Mature virions are transported inside exocytic vesicles to the plasma membrane and released by exocytosis or cell lysis.
    - Virions can also spread directly to neighboring uninfected cells via intercellular junctions or cell-to-cell fusion (syncytia), escaping extracellular neutralizing [[Antibodies, Immunoglobulins|antibodies]].

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:**
    - Productive lytic infection of epithelial cells causes inhibition of host macromolecular synthesis (DNA, RNA, and protein synthesis shutoff by vhs), host DNA fragmentation, cytoskeletal disruption, ballooning degeneration of epithelial cells, nuclear chromatin margination, and cell lysis.
    - **Cowdry Type A Intranuclear Inclusions:** Pathognomonic histopathological eosinophilic intranuclear inclusion bodies surrounded by a clear halo and marginated nuclear chromatin.
    - **Syncytia Formation:** Envelope glycoproteins (gB, gH/gL) expressed on the surface of productively infected cells induce fusion with adjacent uninfected cell membranes, forming multinucleated giant cells.
    - Lytic cell destruction leads to intraepithelial edema, ballooning degeneration, loss of cell cohesion, and formation of thin-roofed, clear fluid-filled intraepidermal vesicles on an erythematous base ("dewdrop on a rose petal").
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:**
    - **Latency:** Following primary mucosal/cutaneous infection, nucleocapsids enter sensory nerve endings and travel by retrograde axonal transport to neuronal cell bodies in sensory ganglia (**Trigeminal ganglia** for oral/facial infections; **Sacral ganglia** [S2–S5] for genital/perianal infections).
    - In latently infected neurons, viral lytic gene expression is completely repressed, and no infectious virions are produced. The viral genome persists indefinitely as an episomal circular minichromosome in the neuronal nucleus.
    - **Latency-Associated Transcripts (LATs):** The only region of the viral genome actively transcribed during latency. Non-coding LAT RNAs and micro-RNAs inhibit lytic gene transcription, promote neuronal survival by blocking apoptosis, and tether the viral genome to nuclear structures.
    - **Reactivation Triggers:** Latent virus reactivates periodically in response to provocative stimuli: physical or emotional stress, fever ("fever blisters"), exposure to ultraviolet B (UVB) light (sunlight, skiing, tanning), local tissue/axonal trauma, menstruation, immunosuppression (systemic corticosteroids, [[HIV]] infection, organ transplantation), or fatigue.
    - Upon reactivation, viral lytic replication resumes in individual neurons, and new nucleocapsids travel by anterograde axonal transport down the peripheral nerve to the innervated dermatome, shedding from skin/mucosa to cause recurrent vesicular lesions or asymptomatic shedding.
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:**
    - N/A.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:**
    - **Direct Cell-to-Cell Spread & Syncytia:** Spreading directly between adjacent cells via intercellular bridges or syncytia formation shields the virus from circulating extracellular neutralizing [[Antibodies, Immunoglobulins|antibodies]] and complement.
    - **Latency in Neurons:** Residing as a transcriptionally silent episome inside non-dividing sensory neurons—which express extremely low levels of [[MHC-1|MHC class I]] molecules—allows the virus to remain completely hidden from host cytotoxic T lymphocytes.
    - **Fc Receptor Activity (gE/gI Complex):** Envelope gE/gI heterodimer binds the Fc portion of host IgG antibodies, bipolarizing host antibodies and blocking Fc-mediated effector functions (complement activation and [[Antibody Effector Functions|ADCC]]).
    - **Complement Inhibition (gC):** Glycoprotein gC binds host complement component C3b, accelerating its decay and preventing complement-mediated viral [[Antibody Effector Functions|neutralization]] and cell lysis.
    - **[[MHC-1|MHC Class I]] Downregulation (ICP47):** Immediate-early protein ICP47 binds to transporter associated with antigen processing (TAP), blocking cytosolic peptide transport into the endoplasmic reticulum and preventing antigen presentation on [[MHC-1|MHC class I]] molecules to CD8+ T cells.
    - **Innate Interferon & Autophagy Antagonism:** ICP34.5 dephosphorylates eIF2alpha to reverse PKR-mediated protein synthesis shutoff, antagonizes type I interferon (IFN-alpha/beta) signaling, and blocks autophagic degradation of viral capsids. vhs degrades host innate mRNAs.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:**
    - **Host Defense:** Innate immunity (type I interferons, NK cells) restricts early viral replication. Resolution of acute lesions and containment of latency require robust cell-mediated immunity, specifically **CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes** and **Th1 CD4+ T cells** (secreting IFN-gamma). Neutralizing [[Antibodies, Immunoglobulins|antibodies]] against surface glycoproteins (gD, gB) limit extracellular viral dissemination and reduce severity of heterotypic reinfection, but cannot clear latent virus or prevent recurrences.
    - **Immunodeficiency Links:** Patients with impaired cell-mediated immunity (HIV/AIDS, organ transplant recipients, congenital [[T-cell]] immunodeficiencies, chemotherapy) suffer frequent, severe, persistent, progressive, non-healing, extensive, or disseminated vesiculoulcerative lesions (e.g., chronic mucocutaneous herpes, verrucous lesions, herpetic [[esophagitis]], tracheobronchitis, or visceral dissemination to liver, lungs, and brain). Neonates lack mature cell-mediated immunity and are at extreme risk for life-threatening disseminated disease or severe [[encephalitis]].

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:**
    - **Primary Oropharyngeal Disease:** Primary HSV-1 infection occurs mostly in young children (1–5 years). Manifests as **acute herpetic gingivostomatitis**: abrupt onset of high fever, irritability, sore throat, cervical lymphadenopathy, and painful vesicular/ulcerative lesions involving the buccal mucosa, gums, tongue, palate, and lips. Gums are swollen, erythematous, and bleed easily. Duration is 2–3 weeks. In young adults, primary HSV-1 presents as severe [[pharyngitis]] and tonsillitis with ulcerative lesions and exudates.
    - **Primary Genital Herpes:** Caused predominantly by HSV-2 (and increasingly HSV-1). Incubation period is 2–7 days (mean 3–5 days). Characterized by severe, painful, bilateral vesiculoulcerative lesions on the external genitalia (glans, shaft of penis; vulva, vagina, cervix, perineum, perianal area). Accompanied by prominent systemic symptoms (fever, headache, myalgia, malaise in ~70%), severe local pain, dysuria, vaginal/urethral discharge, and painful tender inguinal lymphadenopathy. Lesions persist for 2–3 weeks before crusting and healing. Complications include aseptic [[meningitis]] (~10%) and extragenital lesions (~20%).
    - **Herpetic Keratoconjunctivitis (Ocular Herpes):** Primary or recurrent HSV-1 infection of the eye. Presents with eye pain, photophobia, tearing, conjunctival injection, eyelid vesicles, and a characteristic branching **dendritic corneal ulcer** visible on slit-lamp examination with fluorescein staining. Recurrent episodes can cause stromal keratitis, neovascularization, corneal opacification, and permanent blindness (second leading cause of corneal blindness in the US).
    - **Herpes Simplex [[Encephalitis]] (HSE):** Acute, necrotizing focal [[encephalitis]] caused predominantly by **HSV-1** (most common cause of sporadic, fatal viral [[encephalitis]] in Western countries). Characterized by sudden onset of high fever, severe headache, confusion, behavioral/personality changes, memory impairment, speech disturbances (aphasia), focal neurological deficits, and focal temporal lobe seizures. MRI/CT reveals characteristic localized destruction and edema in the **temporal lobe(s)**. CSF demonstrates lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, and presence of erythrocytes (hemorrhagic necrosis). Mortality exceeds 70% if untreated; high rate of permanent neurological sequelae in survivors.
    - **Neonatal Herpes Simplex Infection:** Acquired predominantly during delivery through an infected maternal birth canal shedding HSV-2 (or HSV-1) (~85%), or in utero, or postnatally. Manifests in three distinct clinical patterns appearing in the 1st–3rd week of life:
        1. *Localized Skin, Eye, and Mouth (SEM) Disease:* Vesicular lesions on skin, scalp (fetal monitor sites), eyes, or mouth.
        2. *Central Nervous System (CNS) Disease:* Necrotizing [[encephalitis]] with seizures, lethargy, irritability, bulging fontanelle, and high mortality/morbidity.
        3. *Disseminated Disease:* Severe [[sepsis]]-like presentation with multiorgan failure, DIC, shock, hepatitis, pneumonia, and high mortality (>60%).
    - **Herpetic Whitlow:** Cutaneous infection of the fingers or hand presenting as painful, erythematous, grouped pustular/vesicular lesions. Seen in infants (autoinoculation from oral lesions), healthcare workers/dentists (occupational exposure without gloves), or sexually active individuals.
    - **Herpes Gladiatorum ("Scrum Pox"):** Cutaneous HSV-1 infection on the head, neck, or torso acquired through direct skin-to-skin contact during contact sports (wrestling).
    - **Eczema Herpeticum (Kaposi's Varicelliform Eruption):** Severe, widespread, potential life-threatening superinfection of eczematous or burned skin by HSV-1 in patients with underlying chronic eczema/atopic dermatitis; presents with extensive crops of umbilicated vesicles across affected skin, high fever, and systemic toxicity.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:**
    - **Recurrent Herpes Labialis (Cold Sores / Fever Blisters):** Reactivation of HSV-1 from trigeminal ganglia. Prodrome of localized tingling, burning, or itching at the crimson border of the lip for 6–24 hours, followed by a cluster of small, painful clear vesicles that rupture, form a wet ulcer, crust over, and heal without scarring in 7–10 days.
    - **Recurrent Genital Herpes:** Reactivation of HSV-2 (or HSV-1) from sacral ganglia. Shorter duration and less severe than primary infection (healing in 7–10 days). Preceded by a prodrome of burning, tingling, or shooting sacral neuralgic pains. Unilateral, localized group of vesicles/ulcers; systemic symptoms are rare. Asymptomatic genital shedding (<24 hours duration) is extremely common and drives transmission.
    - **Herpes Simplex [[Meningitis]]:** Aseptic [[meningitis]] caused predominantly by **HSV-2** (associated with primary or recurrent genital herpes). Presents with fever, headache, photophobia, stiff neck, and lymphocytic CSF pleocytosis. Self-limiting. Responsible for most cases of recurrent benign lymphocytic [[meningitis]] (**Mollaret [[meningitis]]**), characterized by large friable [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]] ("Mollaret cells") in CSF.
    - **Herpetic Proctitis:** Severe anorectal pain, tenesmus, constipation, and perianal ulcerative lesions, seen primarily in individuals engaging in receptive anal intercourse.
    - **Herpetic Sacral Radiculomyelopathy:** Neurological complication of genital HSV-2 featuring urinary retention, severe constipation, paresthesias, and leg weakness.
    - **Chronic Mucocutaneous Ulcerative Herpes:** Non-healing, deep, extensive, painful, persistent, or verrucous herpetic ulcers lasting >1 month in advanced [[HIV]]/AIDS or severely immunocompromised patients.
*   **Incubation Period:**
    - Typically 2 to 12 days (average 3 to 7 days for primary infections).
*   **Classic Prodrome:**
    - Localized paresthesia, burning, tingling, or hyperesthesia at the innervated dermatomal site lasting 6 to 24 hours prior to the eruption of recurrent cutaneous/mucosal lesions.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:**
    - N/A. (No single sign is strictly pathognomonic; however, the clinical finding of clustered, clear fluid-filled vesicles on an erythematous base ("dewdrop on a rose petal"), multinucleated giant cells with Cowdry A inclusions on Tzanck smear, or a branching dendritic corneal ulcer with fluorescein staining is highly characteristic).
*   **Complications & Sequelae:**
    - **Corneal Scarring & Blindness:** From recurrent stromal keratitis.
    - **Permanent Neurological Deficits:** Memory loss, motor deficits, epilepsy, and cognitive impairment following HSV [[encephalitis]].
    - **Secondary Bacterial Superinfection:** Staphylococcal or streptococcal impetiginization of cutaneous ulcers.
    - **Erythema Multiforme:** Hypersensitivity skin reaction (target lesions) triggered 1–2 weeks following an HSV recurrence.
    - **Enhanced [[HIV]] Acquisition & Transmission:** Ulcerative genital herpes lesions disrupt mucosal barriers and recruit activated CD4+ target cells, increasing [[HIV]] acquisition and transmission risk 2- to 3-fold.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:**
    - Strictly humans (chronically infected individuals carrying latent virus in sensory ganglia). No animal reservoirs or insect vectors.
*   **Transmission Route:**
    - Direct contact with virus-containing infectious secretions, mucosal surfaces, or active lesion fluid ("mixing and matching of mucous membranes").
    - **HSV-1:** Spread primarily by oral secretions, saliva, kissing, sharing contaminated drinking glasses/utensils/toothbrushes, or autoinoculation.
    - **HSV-2:** Spread predominantly via sexual contact (vaginal, anal, oral-genital) with genital secretions or active lesions.
    - **Asymptomatic Viral Shedding:** Transmission frequently occurs in the complete absence of apparent clinical lesions or symptoms (responsible for >50% of genital herpes transmissions).
    - **Vertical / Perinatal Transmission:** Transmission from mother to newborn during passage through an infected birth canal shedding HSV (risk is 30–50% during primary maternal infection at term; <1% during recurrent maternal shedding).
*   **Vectors:**
    - None.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:**
    - **Ubiquitous Global Distribution:** HSV-1 infects >65% of the world population (seroprevalence 70–90% by adulthood). HSV-2 infects >13% of the global population aged 15–49 years (higher in women and sub-Saharan Africa).
    - **Infection Control & Precautions:** Standard precautions in healthcare settings. Wearing gloves prevents occupational acquisition on fingers (herpetic whitlow).
    - **Prevention of Sexual & Neonatal Transmission:** Individuals with active genital lesions or prodromal symptoms must refrain from sexual intercourse until lesions are completely re-epithelialized. Condom use and daily suppressive antiviral therapy ([[valacyclovir]]) significantly reduce transmission risk. Elective cesarean section is indicated at term for women with active genital herpetic lesions or prodromal symptoms to prevent neonatal herpes.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:**
    - **Tzanck Smear / Cytopathology:** Rapid cytologic test performed by scraping the base of an active vesicular lesion, fixing on a glass slide, and staining with Giemsa, Wright's, or Pap stain. Demonstrates characteristic cytopathic effects (CPE): **multinucleated giant cells (syncytia)**, ballooning degeneration of cells, and **Cowdry Type A intranuclear inclusion bodies** (eosinophilic nuclear inclusions surrounded by a clear halo and marginated chromatin). Cannot differentiate HSV-1, HSV-2, and [[VZV]].
    - **Biopsy / Histopathology:** Liver or brain biopsy demonstrates focal tissue necrosis, hemorrhagic inflammation, syncytia, and Cowdry A inclusions.
*   **Molecular Testing:**
    - **Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests (NAAT / [[PCR]]):** The diagnostic method of choice for all HSV infections due to superior sensitivity and specificity. Essential for detecting HSV DNA in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for diagnosis of HSV [[encephalitis]] and [[meningitis]], as well as testing mucosal swabs, blood, and neonatal samples. Enables rapid molecular differentiation of HSV-1 vs. HSV-2.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:**
    - **Type-Specific Serology:** Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays ([[ELISA and Immunoassays|ELISA]]) utilizing type-specific glycoprotein G (**gG-1 for HSV-1** and **gG-2 for HSV-2**) detect circulating IgG [[Antibodies, Immunoglobulins|antibodies]] to distinguish HSV-1 from HSV-2.
    - Primary infection is confirmed by seroconversion (appearance of IgG [[Antibodies, Immunoglobulins|antibodies]] 2–4 weeks post-infection). Serology is not useful for diagnosing acute recurrent episodes because IgG [[Antibodies, Immunoglobulins|antibodies]] persist for life and titers do not change significantly during recurrences.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:**
    - **Viral Culture:** Inoculation of lesion fluid into susceptible cell lines (HeLa, human embryonic fibroblasts). HSV grows rapidly, producing characteristic rounded, swollen cell CPE and syncytia within 24 to 72 hours. Confirmation and typing are performed using fluorescein-conjugated monoclonal [[Antibodies, Immunoglobulins|antibodies]] (DFA) or shell vial culture. Culture is less sensitive for crusted/healing lesions or CSF samples.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:**
    - **First-Line Nucleoside Analogs:**
        - **[[Acyclovir]] (ACV):** Guanosine nucleoside analog. Selectively monophosphorylated by viral **thymidine kinase (TK)** within infected cells, then converted by host cellular kinases to [[acyclovir]] triphosphate. [[Acyclovir]]-triphosphate competitively inhibits viral **DNA polymerase** and causes DNA chain termination upon incorporation into viral DNA. Administered IV for severe disease (HSE, neonatal HSV, disseminated HSV) and orally for mucocutaneous disease.
        - **[[Valacyclovir]]:** L-valyl ester prodrug of [[acyclovir]] with significantly superior oral bioavailability (3- to 5-fold higher blood levels). Converted to [[acyclovir]] in the liver and intestine. Administered orally.
        - **[[Famciclovir]]:** Oral prodrug of **penciclovir**. Phosphorylated by viral TK and inhibits viral DNA polymerase.
        - **Topical Agents:** Topical 1% trifluridine, 3% [[acyclovir]] ointment, or 0.15% [[ganciclovir]] gel for herpetic keratitis; 1% penciclovir cream or 10% [[docosanol]] cream for herpes labialis.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:**
    - **Thymidine Kinase Mutations (Most Common):** Mutations in the viral TK gene (UL23) resulting in TK deficiency (TK-negative mutants) or altered substrate specificity prevent initial monophosphorylation of [[acyclovir]], [[valacyclovir]], [[famciclovir]], and [[ganciclovir]], conferring cross-resistance across all TK-dependent drugs. Occurs predominantly in severely immunocompromised ([[HIV]]+) patients.
    - **DNA Polymerase Mutations:** Mutations in the viral DNA polymerase gene (UL30) alter drug binding; confers resistance to [[acyclovir]], [[foscarnet]], and [[cidofovir]].
    - **Alternative Agents for [[Acyclovir]]-Resistant HSV:** **[[Foscarnet]]** (pyrophosphate analog that directly inhibits DNA polymerase without requiring phosphorylation) or **[[Cidofovir]]** (nucleotide analog that circumvents TK phosphorylation).
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:**
    - **Early Initiation:** Antiviral therapy is most effective when initiated as early as possible during the prodrome or within 24–72 hours of lesion onset.
    - **[[Encephalitis]] Management:** Intravenous [[acyclovir]] (10 mg/kg IV q8h for 14–21 days) must be initiated immediately upon clinical suspicion of HSV [[encephalitis]], prior to [[PCR]] confirmation.
    - **Renal Toxicity:** High-dose IV [[acyclovir]] can cause crystalline nephropathy/acute renal failure due to drug precipitation in renal tubules; requires adequate hydration and dose adjustment for renal impairment.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:**
    - **Suppressive Therapy:** Daily oral [[valacyclovir]], [[acyclovir]], or [[famciclovir]] reduces recurrence frequency by 70–80% and reduces sexual transmission of HSV-2 to uninfected partners by 50%.
    - **Post-Exposure & Perinatal Prophylaxis:** Oral [[acyclovir]] or [[valacyclovir]] administered to pregnant women with recurrent genital herpes starting at 36 weeks gestation reduces risk of viral recurrence and need for cesarean delivery.
    - **Vaccines:** There are currently no approved vaccines available for HSV-1 or HSV-2. Recombinant glycoprotein subunits, mRNA, and live-attenuated vaccine candidates remain under clinical investigation.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "Dewdrop on a rose petal" (clear fluid vesicle on an erythematous base).
    - "Cowdry Type A inclusion bodies" (eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions with marginated chromatin).
    - "Syncytia" / "Multinucleated giant cells" (fusion of infected cells).
    - "Tzanck smear" (cytologic preparation showing multinucleated giant cells).
    - "Dendritic corneal ulcer" (branching lesion in herpetic keratitis).
    - "Temporal lobe hyperintensity / hemorrhagic necrosis" (pathognomonic MRI/CT finding in HSV-1 [[encephalitis]]).
    - "Mollaret cells" (large friable [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]] in CSF during recurrent HSV-2 [[meningitis]]).
    - "Herpetic whitlow" (lesion on fingers in dentists/nurses).
    - "Herpes gladiatorum" (cutaneous infection in wrestlers).
    - "Kaposi's varicelliform eruption" (eczema herpeticum).
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - **The Neurotropic Alphaherpesvirus Paradox:** HSV causes lytic, highly destructive cytopathic infection in epithelial cells, but establishes a completely non-destructive, transcriptionally silent, lifelong latent infection in non-dividing sensory neurons.
    - **Thymidine Kinase Selectivity:** [[Acyclovir]] is a non-toxic prodrug in normal uninfected host cells because host cellular kinases cannot efficiently phosphorylate it; it is exclusively activated inside HSV-infected cells by the virus-encoded thymidine kinase.
    - **Cross-Anatomical Tropism Shift:** Classically, HSV-1 is "above the waist" (oropharyngeal) and HSV-2 is "below the waist" (genital); however, due to changing sexual practices, HSV-1 has become a leading cause of primary genital herpes in developed nations, and HSV-2 can cause oropharyngeal lesions.
