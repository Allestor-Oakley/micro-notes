### Epstein-Barr virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** Contains a large, double-stranded DNA genome. During latent infection in [[B Cell|B cells]], the genome becomes circularized to form an episome (viral minichromosome).
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** Features a large, icosadeltahedral capsid.
*   **Envelope Properties:** The virion is enveloped and contains viral envelope glycoproteins, including the membrane antigen (MA).
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** Large, enveloped virion with complex morphology typical of the *Herpesviridae* family.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** EBV encodes more than 70 proteins, many of which are unnecessary for basic replication but are utilized to manipulate the host cell and immune response. Structural proteins include the viral capsid antigen (VCA) and membrane antigen (MA). Nonstructural proteins produced during latency and immortalization include Epstein-Barr nuclear antigens (EBNAs 1, 2, 3A, 3B, and 3C), latent proteins (LPs), latent membrane proteins (LMPs 1, 2A, and 2B), two small Epstein-Barr-encoded RNAs (EBER-1 and EBER-2), early antigens (EA-R (restricted to cytoplasm) and EA-D (diffuse in cytoplasm and nucleus)), and the ZEBRA peptide. EBNA-1, EBNA-2, and LPs are DNA-binding proteins; EBNA-1 is essential for establishing and maintaining infection by tethering the genome to host chromosomes during cell division, while EBNA-2 is essential for [[B Cell|B-cell]] immortalization. LMPs exhibit oncoprotein-like activity. The virus also encodes its own DNA polymerase.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** EBV is highly lymphotropic with a very restricted host range and tissue tropism. The virus specifically binds to the C3d receptor ([[CD89|CR2]]) which is primarily expressed on human [[B Cell|B cells]]. It also targets epithelial cells in the oropharynx.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** Binds to the CR2 (C3d) receptor on [[B Cell|B cells]].
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** N/A
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** EBV exhibits complex transcriptional regulation; different groups of viral proteins and antigens are expressed depending on whether the virus is undergoing a lytic, latent, or immortalizing infection cycle.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** DNA replication and capsid assembly occur exclusively in the host nucleus. The mature virus is released via exocytosis, cell lysis, and through cell-to-cell bridges.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** Can cause lytic infections in permissive [[B Cell|B cells]] and epithelial cells (such as those in the pharynx and salivary glands), producing abundant virus.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** EBV establishes lifelong latency. Virus in saliva initially infects epithelial cells and naive resting [[B Cell|B cells]] in the tonsils. These infected [[B Cell|B cells]] migrate to lymphoid follicles and form germinal centers where they differentiate into memory [[B Cell|B cells]]. Viral protein synthesis ceases, and the virus establishes long-term latency in these memory [[B Cell|B cells]] (occurring in approximately 1 in 10^5 to 10^6 circulating [[B Cell|B cells]]). Reactivation leads to recurrent, often asymptomatic, viral shedding in saliva.
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** EBV is the ultimate B-lymphocyte parasite and is directly oncogenic. It stimulates the growth of and immortalizes [[B Cell|B cells]], leading to severe malignancies (e.g., Burkitt lymphoma, primary CNS lymphoma, Hodgkin/non-Hodgkin lymphomas, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and gastric carcinoma). Immortalization requires EBNA-2 and is driven by the oncoprotein-like activity of LMPs.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** EBV encodes an IL-10 analog (BCRF-1) that actively blocks host interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) production, suppressing the antiviral immune response.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Cell-mediated immunity (specifically CD8+ [[T Cell|T cells]]) is absolutely required to limit the outgrowth of EBV-infected [[B Cell|B cells]] and maintain latent infection. [[T Cell|T cells]] are stimulated to proliferate massively, resulting in "mononucleosis." Immunocompromised patients (e.g., organ transplant recipients, [[HIV]]/AIDS patients) are at extreme risk for life-threatening complications, particularly post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease (PTLD) and primary CNS lymphoma. Patients with Duncan disease (an X-linked recessive immunodeficiency caused by an SH2D1A mutation that prevents proper B and [[T cell]] communication) experience fatal, uncontrolled [[B Cell|B-cell]] proliferation and fatal infectious mononucleosis upon EBV exposure.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Infectious mononucleosis ("kissing disease"). In children, primary infection is usually asymptomatic or causes mild symptoms. In teenagers and adults, it typically causes symptomatic mononucleosis.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Development of a distinctive maculopapular rash if the patient is erroneously treated with [[Ampicillin]]. It is also directly associated with immunological disorders such as multiple sclerosis. Can cause viral meningitis and encephalitis.
*   **Incubation Period:** Long incubation period ranging from 30 to 50 days (up to 2 months).
*   **Classic Prodrome:** Mild headache, fatigue, malaise, and fever.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** The classic clinical triad of lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and exudative pharyngitis. Hematologic hallmarks include hyperplasia, absolute lymphocytosis (mononuclear cells constituting 60% to 70% of the WBC count), and the presence of 30% atypical/reactive lymphocytes (Downey cells, which are activated CD8 [[T Cell|T cells]]). Transient production of heterophile antibodies.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** X-linked lymphoproliferative disease (Duncan disease) progression to agammaglobulinemia, aplastic anemia, or fatal lymphoma. Severe neurological complications (encephalitis). Oncogenic sequelae include African Burkitt lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and other lymphomas/carcinomas.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Humans (Memory [[B Cell|B cells]]).
*   **Transmission Route:** Direct contact with infected bodily fluids, primarily transmitted via saliva, close oral contact ("kissing disease"), or sharing of contaminated items such as toothbrushes and cups.
*   **Vectors:** None.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Ubiquitous globally. Lifelong infection ensures that recurrent disease/asymptomatic shedding by carriers serves as the primary ongoing source of contagion.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** Blood smears reveal significant lymphocytosis with abundant atypical/reactive T lymphocytes (Downey cells).
*   **Molecular Testing:** Nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT/PCR) is utilized to detect viral DNA in the blood or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Quantitative PCR (viral load) is especially crucial for monitoring post-transplant patients for the development of post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorders and diagnosing EBV-driven CNS lymphomas in AIDS patients.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:**
    *   **Heterophile Antibody (Monospot test):** Present transiently during the early, acute phase of infectious mononucleosis.
    *   **EBV-Specific Antibodies:**
        *   *VCA-IgM*: Appears early in acute primary infection and disappears.
        *   *VCA-IgG*: Appears during acute infection and persists for life.
        *   *EA (Early Antigen)*: Appears during acute disease and typically wanes.
        *   *EBNA (Nuclear Antigen)*: Crucially, anti-EBNA antibodies do *not* appear during acute disease; they develop only during convalescence (past infection) and persist for life.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** Virus isolation in routine cell culture is not practical. Culturing immortalized [[B Cell|B-cell]] lines from patient peripheral blood can indicate aberrant [[T Cell|T-cell]] control in immunodeficiency workups (e.g., diagnosing Duncan disease).

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** N/A (The sources do not specify highly effective primary antiviral therapies for EBV).
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** Empirical administration of [[Ampicillin]] for a suspected streptococcal sore throat in an EBV-infected patient frequently triggers a characteristic [[Ampicillin]]-induced maculopapular rash.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** No vaccine is currently available. Prevention relies on avoiding the sharing of saliva and personal items.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Kissing disease", heterophile antibody (Monospot), atypical lymphocytes (Downey cells), [[Ampicillin]]-induced rash, C3d receptor (CR2) tropism, African Burkitt lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Downey cells.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** While most viral infections either cause cytolytic destruction or are cleared, EBV drives unchecked cell proliferation (immortalization) in [[B Cell|B cells]], acting directly as a viral oncogene to cause multiple types of human cancer. Additionally, unlike typical viral serology where nuclear and capsid antigens rise together, antibodies to the EBV nuclear antigen (EBNA) are uniquely absent during acute infection and only appear after the disease has resolved.
