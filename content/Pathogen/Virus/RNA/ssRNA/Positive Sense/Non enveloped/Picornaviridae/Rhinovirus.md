### Rhinovirus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** The viral genome is a linear, single-stranded, positive-sense RNA of approximately 7.2 to 8.4 kilobases in size. The genome has a molecular weight of 2.5 million, is infectious on its own, and contains a genome-linked protein (VPg) attached to its 5' end. More than 150 types are known, divided into three major species (HRV-A, HRV-B, and HRV-C). HRV-C was recognized in 2006 and has distinct structural and biological features. Nucleotide sequence identity over the entire genome is >50% among all rhinoviruses.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** The virus possesses a naked, small (25 to 30 nm in diameter) icosahedral capsid.
*   **Envelope Properties:** The virion lacks a lipid envelope. Unlike other picornaviruses, rhinoviruses are distinctly acid labile; virions are unstable below a pH of 5.0 to 6.0, and complete inactivation occurs at a pH of 3.0. They are, however, relatively thermostable and can survive for hours on environmental surfaces. They can be disinfected by mild acids (like citric acid), 2% glutaraldehyde, ≈10% sodium hypochlorite (bleach), or toilet bowl cleaners combining 23% hydrochloric acid and quaternary ammonium compounds.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** Virions are small and spherical, measuring 25 to 30 nm in diameter.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** The viral RNA is translated into a single polyprotein that is subsequently cleaved into structural and enzymatic proteins. There are four major structural polypeptides: surface capsid proteins VP1 and VP3 (which serve as major antibody-binding sites) and the internal protein VP4. Non-structural proteins include 2A (a protease that cleaves the host cell's eIF4g to inhibit host protein synthesis), 3B (encodes the VPg genome-binding protein), 3Cpro (viral protease), and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** Human rhinoviruses are divided into major and minor receptor groups. Viruses of the major group use intercellular adhesion molecule-1 ([[CAM#ICAM-1 (CD54)|ICAM-1]]) as their cellular receptor. Those of the minor group bind to members of the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) family. The virus displays a strict tropism for the upper respiratory tract and replicates best at 33°C, similar to the temperature of the human nasopharynx.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** The virus enters the host cell via receptor-mediated endocytosis. The naked RNA genome alone is sufficient to initiate infection upon entering the cytoplasm.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** N/A
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** Viral replication occurs entirely within the host cell cytoplasm. The positive-sense RNA genome acts directly as mRNA and is translated into a single polyprotein. This polyprotein is cleaved into functional enzymatic and structural proteins by viral-encoded proteases. The RNA-dependent RNA polymerase synthesizes a negative-sense RNA intermediate, which acts as a template for new positive-sense genomic/mRNA transcripts.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** Mature virions assemble in the cytoplasm. Because it is a naked capsid virus, rhinovirus is typically released from the cell through cell lysis.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** Most rhinoviruses are cytolytic, destroying the respiratory epithelial cells they infect.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** N/A
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The vast number of distinct rhinovirus types (over 150) precludes the host from developing comprehensive, long-term immune protection, allowing for repeated infections with heterologous types throughout life.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Control of the infection involves interferon, local, and nonimmune defenses, along with mucosal IgA and serum IgG antibodies. A strong homologous immunity develops in the short term, but it does not protect against the myriad of other circulating serotypes.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** The common cold (acute upper respiratory tract infection).
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Can present as acute respiratory disease, asthma exacerbation, acute bronchiolitis, pneumonia, and febrile seizures.
*   **Incubation Period:** Typically 1 to 3 days.
*   **Classic Prodrome:** Characterized by a runny nose, mild cough, malaise, and a low-grade fever.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** N/A
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** The sheer frequency of rhinovirus infections results in a significant economic and social burden due to lost days at work or school, reduced cognitive performance, and medical attendance. Secondary bacterial infections (e.g., sinusitis, otitis media) may occur following viral damage to the respiratory epithelium.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Humans, chimpanzees, and gibbons.
*   **Transmission Route:** Transmitted primarily via respiratory aerosols, inhalation of infectious droplets, and direct contact with infected hands or fomites (e.g., tissues, toys, environmental surfaces).
*   **Vectors:** N/A
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Rhinoviruses are the most frequent cause of the common cold worldwide. Infections occur year-round. Transmission is heavily promoted by poor hygiene, crowded living conditions, schools, and day-care centers. Viral titers in nasal secretions peak as early as 2 to 4 days after exposure (correlating directly with maximal illness severity) but can remain detectable for up to 3 weeks. In households in lower-income settings, rhinovirus invasions can occur with up to two different types per month.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** N/A
*   **Molecular Testing:** Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and multiplex nucleic acid amplification tests (NAT) of nasal washings, nasal swabs, throat swabs, or sputum are the preferred, most sensitive, and most specific diagnostic methods. Due to the difficulty of culturing species C (HRV-C), genotyping by sequence analysis is heavily utilized.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is available for antigen detection but is less commonly used than molecular methods.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** The virus can be grown in human cell lines such as WI-38 and MRC-5. Organ cultures of ferret and human tracheal epithelium may be required for more fastidious strains. Cultivation must be performed at 33°C (cooler than the standard 37°C) to successfully isolate the virus.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** There is no specific, approved antiviral therapy for rhinovirus infections. Pleconaril (an antiviral drug that targets picornavirus penetration and uncoating) has been studied, but clinical management relies strictly on supportive and symptomatic care.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** N/A
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** There is no available vaccine due to the existence of more than 150 distinct serotypes. Prevention relies on proper hygiene, frequent handwashing, and disinfection of contaminated fomites and environmental surfaces.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Common cold, Picornaviridae, acid labile, 33°C optimum growth temperature, ICAM-1 receptor, LDLR receptor, >150 serotypes, naked icosahedral capsid.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Although rhinoviruses are members of the Picornaviridae family alongside enteroviruses (such as [[poliovirus]] and [[coxsackievirus]], which are highly stable at acidic pH to survive the gastrointestinal tract), rhinoviruses are major exceptions because they are strictly acid labile and completely inactivated at pH 3.0. Additionally, unlike most human viruses that thrive at core body temperature (37°C), rhinoviruses replicate optimally at 33°C, effectively restricting them to the cooler environment of the upper respiratory tract (nasopharynx).
