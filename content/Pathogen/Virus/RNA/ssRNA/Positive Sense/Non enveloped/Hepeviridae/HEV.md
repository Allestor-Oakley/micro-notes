### Hepatitis E Virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** The viral genome consists of a positive-sense, single-stranded RNA measuring approximately 7.2 kilobases in length. The genome contains three open reading frames (ORFs). ORF1 (the largest, at 1693 codons) encodes non-structural proteins. ORF2 encodes the viral capsid proteins. ORF3 encodes a protein essential for virion release. The virus is classified as the sole member of the genus *Hepevirus* within the family *Hepeviridae*. There are four major genotypes and up to 24 subtypes; Genotypes 1 and 2 account for most new cases globally.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** The virus possesses an icosahedral nucleocapsid measuring 27 to 32 nm in diameter.
*   **Envelope Properties:** HEV is classically described as a naked, non-enveloped capsid virus that is heat stable. However, the virus exhibits a unique, quasi-enveloped life cycle: matured virions associate with lipid and the ORF3-encoded protein intracellularly, but upon secretion into the biliary system, the virions lose this lipid coat and are subsequently excreted in the stool as naked, non-enveloped particles.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** Virions are small and spherical/icosahedral, measuring 27 to 32 nm in diameter. The physical structure broadly resembles that of caliciviruses.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** ORF1 encodes non-structural proteins, including the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. ORF2 encodes the structural capsid proteins, which are commonly utilized as vaccine candidates. The ORF3 protein associates with matured virions and is essential for their release from the host cell.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** The virus exhibits a strict tissue tropism for the liver (hepatocytes).
*   **Entry Mechanism:** N/A
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** Upon entering the hepatocyte, the uncoated viral RNA is released into the cytoplasm.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** Replication and translation occur in the host cell cytoplasm, generally resembling the replication cycle of the [[HAV|Hepatitis A virus]] (HAV). The positive-sense genomic RNA is directly translated into non-structural proteins (including the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase) encoded by ORF1. The viral polymerase then generates a negative-sense RNA intermediate, which serves as a template for the transcription of the structural capsid proteins encoded by ORF2.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** Matured virions associate with lipid and the ORF3 protein, which is essential for virion release. The virus is secreted into the biliary system, where it loses its lipid coat prior to excretion in the feces.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** Infection leads to spotty parenchymal cell degeneration, hepatocyte necrosis, a diffuse lobular inflammatory reaction, and disruption of liver cell cords. This is accompanied by reticuloendothelial (Kupffer) cell hyperplasia and periportal infiltration by mononuclear cells. Later in the disease course, [[Macrophage|macrophages]] accumulate near degenerating hepatocytes. The reticulum framework is generally preserved, allowing hepatocyte regeneration and restoration of hepatic architecture within 8 to 12 weeks.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** N/A
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** The virus is not oncogenic and is not associated with an increased risk of primary hepatocellular carcinoma.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** N/A
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** While HEV does not typically establish chronic infections in immunocompetent hosts, chronic HEV disease is increasingly recognized in immunosuppressed individuals, particularly in low-prevalence settings.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Acute viral hepatitis. The disease is clinically indistinguishable from other causes of acute viral hepatitis and is usually mild and self-limiting in normal patients.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Fulminant hepatitis, severe acute liver failure, and maternal death can occur, particularly in pregnant women. Chronic hepatitis E infection may occur in immunocompromised patients.
*   **Incubation Period:** Typically 15 to 50 days (ranging from 2 to 6 weeks).
*   **Classic Prodrome:** Abrupt onset of disease featuring fever, chills, headache, fatigue, anorexia, nausea, and right upper quadrant abdominal pain. These symptoms are followed within days by jaundice, dark-colored urine, and pale stools.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** N/A
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** The disease carries a high mortality rate (up to 20%) when fulminant hepatitis develops in pregnant women, particularly during the third trimester. In the general population, the mortality rate is approximately 1% to 2%.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Genotypes 1 and 2 are primarily confined to humans. However, HEV is also a zoonosis; animal strains are common globally, with reservoirs including rodents, pigs, sheep, cattle, deer, and other game animals.
*   **Transmission Route:** Transmission is predominantly via the fecal-oral route, most frequently through the ingestion of fecally contaminated drinking water or food supplies. Infection can also be acquired zoonotically by consuming undercooked pork or game meat. While person-to-person transmission is uncommon, transmission can occur via blood transfusion and vertically (mother-to-child) during the third trimester of pregnancy in endemic areas.
*   **Vectors:** N/A
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** HEV is responsible for an estimated 20 million acute infections and 44,000 deaths annually worldwide. It is highly endemic in developing countries with weak infrastructure. Massive waterborne epidemics have occurred following sewage contamination of city drinking water supplies (e.g., a 1955 New Delhi outbreak with 29,000 cases; a 1978 Kashmir outbreak with 1,700 deaths; and a massive outbreak in China affecting over 100,000 individuals).

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** Liver biopsy reveals focal areas of hepatocyte necrosis, mononuclear inflammatory infiltrates, Kupffer cell hyperplasia, and [[Macrophage]] accumulation.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) can be used to detect the HEV RNA genome in blood and stool samples during the acute phase.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Diagnosis is typically confirmed via an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) detecting IgM anti-HEV antibodies, which indicates a recent infection. This IgM response generally remains positive for 4 to 6 months following infection.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** N/A

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** [[Ribavirin]] is utilized as a key therapeutic agent for HEV infections, particularly in severe or chronic cases.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** For the vast majority of cases, the disease is self-limiting and clinical management relies strictly on supportive care.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** An effective vaccine utilizing the ORF2-encoded capsid proteins has been developed and approved, but it is currently only available and adopted in China. Prevention globally relies on improvements in sanitation, provision of clean drinking water, and ensuring that pork and game meat are thoroughly cooked.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Fecal-oral hepatitis, contaminated drinking water, extreme mortality in pregnant women (20%), *Hepeviridae*, zoonosis (pigs/game meat), fulminant hepatitis in third trimester.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Although HEV is classically considered an acute, self-limiting virus that does not cause chronic liver disease (similar to [[HAV]]), it is a major exception in immunosuppressed individuals, in whom it has increasingly been recognized as a cause of chronic hepatitis. Furthermore, HEV exhibits a highly unusual structural dichotomy: it exists as an enveloped virion inside the host and in the bloodstream, but completely sheds this lipid envelope as it is secreted into the biliary tract, emerging in the feces as a naked capsid virus.
