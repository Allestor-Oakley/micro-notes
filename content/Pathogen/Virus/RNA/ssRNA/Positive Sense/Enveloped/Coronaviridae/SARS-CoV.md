### SARS-CoV

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** The viral genome is a linear, single-stranded, positive-sense RNA molecule of approximately 27 to 32 kb in length, making it the largest genome among RNA viruses. The genome is capped at the 5' end and polyadenylated at the 3' end. The gene order for encoded proteins is Pol-S-E-M-N-3'. The SARS virus contains a comparatively large number of interspersed genes for nonstructural proteins at the 3' end of the genome. It is classified in the family Coronaviridae, genus Betacoronavirus, and subgenus Sarbecovirus.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** The virus possesses a flexible, helical nucleocapsid measuring 9 to 11 nm in diameter. It is formed by the association of the positive-strand genomic RNA with numerous molecules of the phosphorylated nucleocapsid (N) protein (50-60 kDa).
*   **Envelope Properties:** The nucleocapsid is enclosed within a lipid bilayer envelope. Uniquely for enveloped viruses, the protective outer "corona" of glycoproteins renders the virion relatively resistant to inactivation by drying, detergents, and the acidic/bile conditions of the gastrointestinal tract.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** The virion is spherical, pleomorphic, and medium-sized, measuring 120 to 160 nm in diameter. It features 20-nm-long, club- or petal-shaped glycoprotein projections widely spaced on the outer surface, giving it a characteristic "solar corona" appearance under an electron microscope.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** The virus contains four main structural proteins: the Spike (S) glycoprotein (180–220 kDa), which forms the trimeric peplomers responsible for attachment and fusion; the Membrane (M) glycoprotein (20–35 kDa), which serves as a matrix protein embedded in the envelope interacting with the nucleocapsid; the Envelope (E) protein, which facilitates envelopment and release; and the Nucleocapsid (N) protein. The genome also encodes multiple non-structural proteins, including an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase and various proteases.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** The Spike (S) glycoprotein binds primarily to the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE-2) receptor. The virus displays a strong tropism for epithelial cells of the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** Following attachment, the S protein is cleaved by host cell surface or endosomal proteases (such as transmembrane serine protease 2 (TMPRSS2)) into S1 (attachment) and S2 (fusion) subunits. The particle is internalized via absorptive endocytosis, and the S2 subunit facilitates direct fusion of the viral envelope with the plasma membrane or endosomal membranes.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** Upon fusion, the viral RNA genome is uncoated and released directly into the host cell cytoplasm, where the entire replication cycle occurs.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** The positive-sense RNA genome acts directly as mRNA. The early phase of translation produces large polyproteins (from ORF1a and ORF1b) which are cleaved by viral proteases to yield the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase and other enzymes. These nonstructural proteins induce the formation of membrane vesicles from the endoplasmic reticulum to sequester transcription and replication. The viral polymerase synthesizes a full-length negative-sense RNA template, which is subsequently used to generate new full-length positive-sense genomes and a nested set of smaller subgenomic mRNAs that encode late structural and accessory proteins.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** Newly synthesized genomic RNA interacts with N proteins in the cytoplasm to assemble into helical nucleocapsids. The S, E, and M glycoproteins are synthesized and inserted into the rough endoplasmic reticulum. The nucleocapsids bind to M proteins at the endoplasmic reticulum-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) membranes, where virions assemble and bud into the lumen. Mature virions are transported in vesicles to the cell periphery and released via exocytosis-like fusion or upon cell lysis.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** SARS-CoV is a cytolytic virus that can replicate efficiently at core body temperature (37°C). It directly infects and kills epithelial cells in the respiratory tract and lungs, causing extensive tissue damage. It is also capable of inducing cell-to-cell fusion mediated by the S glycoprotein (at pH > 6.5).
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** N/A
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The virus sequesters its transcription and replication processes within virus-induced membrane vesicles, limiting the exposure of replicative RNA intermediates to host cell innate immune sensors (e.g., delaying interferon induction). It exhibits a high frequency of mutation and recombination during replication, facilitating the evolution of viral strains and immune evasion.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Progression of SARS involves a "cytokine storm" characterized by the sustained elevation of multiple proinflammatory chemokines and cytokines in the peripheral circulation for approximately 2 weeks. This dysregulated, hyperinflammatory response drives the development of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS), presenting as a classical atypical pneumonia characterized by high fever, chills, rigors, headache, dizziness, malaise, myalgia, cough, and shortness of breath.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Gastrointestinal symptoms, including diarrhea, may occur. The virus can also be detected in the kidneys, liver, and small intestine. Many patients present with abnormal chest radiographs.
*   **Incubation Period:** Averages about 6 days.
*   **Classic Prodrome:** Common early symptoms include fever, malaise, chills, headache, dizziness, cough, and sore throat, followed a few days later by shortness of breath.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** N/A
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Rapid progression to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) requiring ventilatory support, progressive respiratory failure, and death. The mortality rate of symptomatic patients is approximately 9.6% to 10%, with the highest death rates occurring among the elderly. Mortality is significantly lower in children.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Chinese horseshoe bats (*Rhinolophus* species) are the natural evolutionary reservoir. Intermediate amplifying hosts include masked-palm civets, raccoon dogs, and Chinese ferret badgers.
*   **Transmission Route:** The virus jumped to humans in live animal (wet) markets in Guangdong, China. Person-to-person transmission occurs primarily through close contact with respiratory droplets. The virus is also present in sweat, urine, and feces, implicating oral-fecal and fomite transmission routes. Transmission is most efficient in the later stages of infection. "Super-spreaders" (individuals infecting more than 10 contacts) played a significant role in widespread dissemination.
*   **Vectors:** N/A
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** The outbreak erupted in southern China in late 2002 and spread rapidly globally via international air travel, resulting in over 8,000 cases and 744-800+ deaths across 29 countries before waning in mid-2003. Healthcare workers accounted for up to 21% of cases. Control was achieved through extraordinary public health efforts including strict patient isolation, quarantine of exposed individuals, travel restrictions, and the rigorous use of personal protective equipment (gloves, gowns, goggles, and N95 respirators).

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** N/A
*   **Molecular Testing:** Real-time reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) of upper or lower respiratory secretions, nasal swabs, saliva, and stool samples is the preferred diagnostic method. Viremia is also detectable in the plasma by PCR.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Over 95% of patients develop an antibody response detectable by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), indirect immunofluorescent antibody assays, or hemagglutination tests. Serodiagnosis using acute and convalescent sera is useful for retrospective epidemiologic confirmation.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** The virus is generally difficult to isolate but can be recovered from oropharyngeal specimens using Vero monkey kidney cell lines. Due to high infectivity, culture must be conducted in specialized Biosafety Level 3 (BSL-3) containment facilities.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** There is no proven specific antiviral treatment. Lopinavir (an [[HIV]] protease inhibitor) demonstrated in vitro activity against the SARS coronavirus. Clinical management relies on aggressive supportive care, including ventilatory support.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** N/A
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** There is no approved vaccine for SARS-CoV-1. Prevention relies entirely on infection control measures and contact tracing.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Guangdong China 2002, palm civets, wet markets, super-spreaders, Betacoronavirus, Sarbecovirus, ACE-2 receptor, solar corona morphology, atypical pneumonia, ARDS.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike most enveloped viruses (which are highly susceptible to detergents and gastrointestinal acids), the glycoprotein "corona" allows SARS-CoV to withstand drying and the gastrointestinal environment. Furthermore, despite its exceptionally large 30-kb RNA genome (which would normally accumulate lethal mutations), the virus utilizes an exoribonuclease with proofreading capacity to maintain its genetic stability. Unlike its successor [[SARS-CoV-2]], SARS-CoV-1 is most infectious during the later stages of clinical disease, which facilitated more effective public health containment.
