### Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** HIV belongs to the family Retroviridae and the genus Lentivirus. The genome consists of two identical copies (diploid) of positive-sense, single-stranded, linear RNA, approximately 7 to 11 kilobases (kb) in length. HIV-1 is divided into four groups: M (main), N, O, and P. Group M is divided into 11 subtypes or clades (A–K) based on sequence differences, particularly in the env gene. HIV-2 has eight subtypes (A–H) and exhibits greater than 50% sequence divergence from HIV-1. The integrated form of the viral genome is a provirus with duplicated sequences called long terminal repeats (LTRs) at both ends. The genome includes standard retroviral genes (gag, pol, env) as well as accessory genes (tat, rev, nef, vif, vpu, vpr in HIV-1, and vpx in HIV-2).
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** The virus possesses a centrally located, D-type cylindrical or conical nucleocapsid core. The major capsid protein is p24 (CA), and the nucleocapsid proteins binding the RNA genome are p7 and p9 (NC).
*   **Envelope Properties:** The viral nucleocapsid is surrounded by a host-derived lipid bilayer envelope. The envelope is studded with trimeric viral glycoprotein spikes and contains the viral matrix protein p17 (MA) lining its inner surface. The virus is sensitive to drying and can be inactivated by standard disinfectants, including 10% bleach, 70% ethanol/isopropanol, glutaraldehyde, and detergents.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** The virion is spherical and measures approximately 80 to 110 nm in diameter.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** The virion carries several packaged enzymes, including reverse transcriptase (p66/p51, which possesses RNA-dependent DNA polymerase, DNA-dependent DNA polymerase, and RNase H activities), integrase (p32), and protease (p10). The envelope glycoprotein precursor (gp160) is cleaved by cellular proteases into the surface glycoprotein SU (gp120), which is the viral attachment protein, and the transmembrane glycoprotein TM (gp41), which serves as the fusion protein. The Gag polyprotein (p55) is cleaved into the matrix (p17), capsid (p24), and nucleocapsid (p7/p9) proteins. Accessory proteins include Tat (transactivator of viral and cellular genes), Rev (regulates RNA splicing and promotes RNA export to the cytoplasm), Nef (decreases cell-surface CD4, facilitates [[T Cell|T-cell]] activation, and is required for progression to AIDS), Vif (promotes virus infectivity and assembly, blocks cellular antiviral protein APOBEC3G), Vpu (facilitates virion assembly/release by inhibiting host tetherin and induces degradation of CD4), and Vpr (transports complementary DNA to the nucleus, arrests cell growth, and facilitates replication in [[Macrophage|macrophages]]).

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** Infection begins when the viral SU (gp120) protein binds to the primary host cell receptor, the CD4 molecule. Successful attachment also requires binding to a chemokine coreceptor, either CCR5 or CXCR4. Initially, the virus is primarily [[macrophage]]-tropic (M-tropic), binding to CCR5 on [[Macrophage|macrophages]], [[Dendritic Cell|dendritic cells]], and memory CD4 [[T Cell|T cells]]. During chronic infection, the virus frequently mutates to become [[T Cell|T-cell]] tropic (X4 virus), preferentially binding to CXCR4 on T lymphocytes.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** Following coreceptor binding, the TM (gp41) glycoprotein undergoes a conformational change to mediate direct fusion of the viral envelope with the host cell's plasma membrane.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** Upon fusion, the viral core is released into the cytoplasm. The Vpr protein facilitates the transport of the newly synthesized double-stranded complementary DNA (cDNA) complex through the nuclear pores into the nucleus.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** In the cytoplasm, the virion-associated reverse transcriptase synthesizes a double-stranded DNA copy from the positive-sense RNA genome. This cDNA is transported to the nucleus and stably integrated into the host cell chromosome as a provirus by the viral integrase enzyme. The integrated provirus acts as a cellular gene template. Host RNA polymerase II transcribes full-length genomic RNA and multiple spliced mRNAs, a process heavily regulated by the viral Tat and Rev proteins. The viral mRNAs are translated by host ribosomes into Gag, Gag-Pol, and Env polyproteins.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** The viral Env polyprotein (gp160) is synthesized and cleaved in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus before being transported to the plasma membrane. The Gag and Gag-Pol polyproteins associate with the viral RNA genomes at the plasma membrane. Virions assemble and bud from the cell surface. Unlike many viruses, HIV can infect terminally differentiated, non-dividing cells (like [[Macrophage|macrophages]]), but activation of the cell is required for efficient virus production. The host protein tetherin attempts to block virion release, but the viral Vpu protein counteracts this. Final maturation occurs after budding when the viral protease cleaves the Gag and Gag-Pol polyproteins into the functional structural proteins and enzymes, creating the mature infectious nucleocapsid.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** HIV is cytolytic to permissive CD4 T lymphocytes. The virus can also spread from cell to cell by inducing the formation of multinucleated giant cells (syncytia), which are fragile and ultimately undergo lysis. Infection of CD4 [[T Cell|T cells]] leads to their direct destruction.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** The virus establishes a latent reservoir by integrating its proviral DNA into the genome of resting memory CD4 T lymphocytes and cells of the [[macrophage]] lineage. The virus remains permanently associated with these cells and can persist for the lifetime of the host. Reactivation is triggered by cellular activation, and viral rebound rapidly occurs if antiretroviral therapy (ART) is interrupted.
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** HIV is not directly oncogenic; it lacks classical viral oncogenes and does not typically cause insertional mutagenesis leading to tumors. Instead, HIV-induced immunodeficiency severely impairs immune surveillance, allowing opportunistic oncogenic viruses to reactivate and drive cancer development. These include Kaposi sarcoma (caused by [[HHV-8|Human Herpesvirus 8]]), primary lymphoma of the brain and non-Hodgkin lymphomas (caused by [[EBV|Epstein-Barr virus]]), and anogenital or cervical carcinomas (caused by [[HPV|Human Papillomavirus]]).
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The error-prone nature of the viral reverse transcriptase leads to exceptionally high mutation rates, generating a heterogeneous swarm of viral genomes known as "quasispecies" within a single host. This allows continuous antigenic variation, particularly in the envelope glycoproteins (gp120 V regions), enabling the virus to evade neutralizing antibodies. The virus also evades intrinsic cellular defenses: Vif suppresses the host cytidine deaminase APOBEC3G, and Vpu counteracts the viral release-inhibiting protein tetherin. Furthermore, Nef downregulates CD4 expression, while HIV’s ability to remain latent in resting memory [[T Cell|T cells]] hides it from immune detection entirely.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Cellular restriction factors like APOBEC3G and TRIM5α (which targets the viral capsid to proteasomes) provide intrinsic host defense but are subverted by viral accessory proteins. The defining pathological hallmark of HIV is the profound, progressive depletion of CD4 [[T Cell|T cells]]. This depletion disrupts both cell-mediated and humoral immune regulation, leading to Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS). The severe immunodeficiency renders the host profoundly susceptible to opportunistic infections (e.g., *[[Pneumocystis jirovecii]]*, *[[Mycobacterium tuberculosis]]*, [[CMV|Cytomegalovirus]], *[[Toxoplasma gondii]]*, *[[Cryptococcus neoformans]]*) and malignancies.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** HIV infection progresses from an acute infection phase to a prolonged clinical latency phase, culminating in AIDS. Acute HIV infection presents as a mononucleosis-like illness. AIDS is defined by a CD4 [[T Cell|T-cell]] count dropping below 200 cells/µL or the development of specific AIDS-defining opportunistic infections or cancers.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** HIV infection can lead to HIV encephalopathy (AIDS dementia complex), characterized by memory loss, behavioral changes, and paralysis, caused by viral infection of microglial cells and [[Macrophage|macrophages]] in the brain. Other systemic presentations include HIV wasting syndrome, persistent generalized lymphadenopathy, lymphoid interstitial pneumonia, and various hematologic disorders (unexplained anemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia). Specific renal syndromes, including classic HIV-associated nephropathy (HIVAN), immune-complex glomerulonephritis, and thrombotic microangiopathy, can also develop.
*   **Incubation Period:** Acute HIV infection typically manifests 2 to 4 weeks (14 to 28 days) after exposure. The clinical latency period before the onset of AIDS is highly variable, typically lasting 1 to 10 years or more without treatment.
*   **Classic Prodrome:** Acute HIV infection is characterized by non-specific, flu-like or mononucleosis-like symptoms, including fever, malaise, fatigue, headache, myalgia, exudative pharyngitis, generalized lymphadenopathy, a maculopapular rash, and oral or genital ulcers.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Severe, unexplained depletion of CD4 T lymphocytes (specifically a CD4 [[T Cell|T-cell]] count <200 cells/µL or a substantially inverted CD4:CD8 ratio) in the presence of HIV antibodies or detectable viral RNA is characteristic of progression to AIDS.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** If untreated, HIV inevitably leads to fatal opportunistic infections and neoplasms. *[[Mycobacterium tuberculosis]]* is the most frequent cause of death in HIV-positive people worldwide. Other severe complications include *[[Pneumocystis jirovecii]]* pneumonia, [[CMV|Cytomegalovirus]] retinitis and colitis, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy ([[JC virus]]), severe mucocutaneous and esophageal candidiasis, cerebral toxoplasmosis, disseminated *[[Mycobacterium avium]]* complex, and cryptococcal meningitis. Patients receiving antiretroviral therapy may develop Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS), a paradoxical worsening of pre-existing infectious processes as the immune system recovers.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Humans are the only reservoir for HIV. (The virus evolved from closely related simian immunodeficiency viruses, SIVcpz and SIVsm, found in non-human primates).
*   **Transmission Route:** HIV is transmitted primarily through direct contact with infected blood, semen, vaginal secretions, and breast milk. Major modes of transmission include unprotected sexual contact, sharing of contaminated injection drug needles/equipment, maternal-neonatal transmission (transplacental, intrapartum, or via breastfeeding), and historically through transfusions of contaminated blood or blood products.
*   **Vectors:** N/A
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** HIV is a global pandemic, causing approximately 32 million deaths since its discovery and resulting in roughly 4,200 new infections daily worldwide. The highest burden of disease is in sub-Saharan Africa. Universal precautions must be rigorously maintained when handling blood and body fluids to prevent nosocomial transmission (e.g., needlestick injuries). Prevention strategies rely on widespread testing, safe sex practices (condom use), needle exchange programs, male circumcision, screening of the blood supply, and the use of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) with antiretroviral drugs.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** Tissue biopsies may reveal multinucleated giant cells (syncytia). In AIDS dementia, brain autopsies show demyelination, astrocytosis, and clusters of nonenveloped viral particles within neurons.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Real-time quantitative reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) is used to measure the HIV RNA viral load in plasma. Viral load testing is essential for monitoring disease progression, establishing the "virologic set point," predicting clinical outcome, and assessing the efficacy of antiretroviral therapy. Qualitative HIV-1 RNA PCR is also used to resolve indeterminate serological results or to diagnose acute infection before seroconversion. Genotypic sequencing of the viral RNA is routinely performed to detect resistance mutations to antiretroviral drugs.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Routine diagnosis generally involves screening with combination enzyme immunoassays (EIAs) that detect both HIV antibodies and the viral p24 antigen. The presence of p24 antigen or high viral load indicates acute infection before the development of an antibody response (the window period). Reactive screening tests are typically confirmed with an HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation assay or a Western blot (which detects antibodies against specific viral proteins such as gp160, gp120, p68, p55, gp41, p40, p34, p24, and p17).
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** HIV can be isolated by cocultivating a patient's peripheral blood mononuclear cells with uninfected, mitogen-stimulated donor lymphocytes. Viral growth is confirmed after 7 to 14 days by detecting viral reverse transcriptase activity or p24 antigen in the culture supernatant. Due to the slow growth of primary isolates and the requirement for specialized containment facilities, viral culture is rarely used for routine clinical diagnosis.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy (HAART) relies on combinations of drugs targeting multiple stages of the viral life cycle. Major drug classes include:
    *   **Nucleoside/Nucleotide Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs):** [[Zidovudine]] (AZT), [[Didanosine]], [[Stavudine]], [[Lamivudine]], [[Emtricitabine]], [[Tenofovir]].
    *   **Nonnucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs):** [[Nevirapine]], [[Delavirdine]].
    *   **Protease Inhibitors (PIs):** [[Saquinavir]], [[Ritonavir]], [[Darunavir]], [[Fosamprenavir]], [[Atazanavir]].
    *   **Integrase Inhibitors (INSTIs):** [[Dolutegravir]] (currently recommended by the WHO as part of the preferred first-line regimen due to its high barrier to resistance), [[Cabotegravir]].
    *   **Entry/Fusion Inhibitors:** [[Enfuvirtide]].
    *   **CCR5 Coreceptor Antagonists:** [[Maraviroc]].
    *   **Capsid Binders:** Lencapavir.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** The viral reverse transcriptase is highly error-prone, rapidly generating drug-resistant mutations under selective pressure. Single-drug therapy inevitably fails; therefore, strict adherence to a multi-drug HAART regimen is mandatory to suppress viral replication and prevent the emergence of resistant quasispecies. Baseline and follow-up genotypic resistance testing guides regimen selection.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** International guidelines strongly recommend initiating ART in all patients at the time of diagnosis, regardless of their CD4 [[T Cell|T-cell]] count, to preserve immune function and eliminate the risk of onward transmission. Co-management of HIV and opportunistic infections (like Tuberculosis) can be complicated by severe drug-drug interactions, overlapping toxicities, and the risk of developing Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS) when ART is initiated.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** There is currently no approved vaccine for HIV due to the immense genetic diversity and high mutation rate of the viral envelope glycoproteins. Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP) using daily oral antiretrovirals (e.g., [[Tenofovir]]/[[Emtricitabine]]) is highly effective at preventing acquisition in high-risk populations. To prevent opportunistic infections, patients with advanced HIV disease require prophylactic antibiotics, notably Co-trimoxazole ([[Cotrimoxazole|trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole]]) to prevent *[[Pneumocystis jirovecii]]* pneumonia and cerebral toxoplasmosis, and [[Isoniazid]] preventive therapy for Tuberculosis.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** AIDS, CD4 [[T Cell|T cells]], reverse transcriptase, integrase, protease, gp120/gp41, p24 antigen, CCR5/CXCR4 coreceptors, syncytia formation, opportunistic infections (*Pneumocystis*, Kaposi sarcoma), HAART, virologic set point, quasispecies, lentivirus.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike many typical RNA viruses that replicate exclusively in the cytoplasm and do not alter the host genome, HIV reverse-transcribes its RNA into DNA and permanently integrates it into the host cell's nuclear chromatin as a provirus. Additionally, unlike most typical retroviruses that rely on active cell division to access the host genome, lentiviruses like HIV have the unique capacity to actively transport their cDNA through the nuclear pores, allowing them to infect non-dividing, terminally differentiated cells such as [[Macrophage|macrophages]] and microglial cells.
