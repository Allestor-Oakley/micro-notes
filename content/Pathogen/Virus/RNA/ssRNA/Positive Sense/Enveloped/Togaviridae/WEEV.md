### Western equine encephalitis virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** The viral genome consists of a single-stranded, positive-sense, linear RNA molecule measuring approximately 11 to 12 kilobases in length. WEEV is classified in the family Togaviridae and the genus Alphavirus. Sequence data indicates that WEEV is actually a genetic recombinant of two different alphaviruses: [[EEEV|Eastern equine encephalitis virus]] (EEEV) and Sindbis virus. The beginning of the WEEV genome is almost identical to [[EEEV]] (containing similar glycoproteins and virulence genes), whereas the end of the genome resembles Sindbis.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** The virus possesses an icosahedral nucleocapsid consisting of 42 capsomeres.
*   **Envelope Properties:** The nucleocapsid is tightly surrounded by a lipid-containing envelope. The carboxy (COOH) terminus of the envelope glycoproteins is anchored in the capsid, forcing the envelope to wrap tightly (a "shrink-wrap" effect) and adopt the exact shape and icosahedral symmetry of the underlying capsid. The virus possesses hemagglutinating ability and is inactivated by acid pH, heat, lipid solvents, detergents, bleach, phenol, 70% alcohol, and formaldehyde.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** Virions are spherical in shape and measure approximately 40 to 70 nm in diameter.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** The viral genome encodes both early and late proteins. Structural polypeptides include the capsid (C) protein and the envelope spike glycoproteins (E1, E2, and E3). Two or three glycoproteins associate to form a single spike on the virion surface. The genome also encodes early non-structural proteins (NSP1 to NSP4), which include a transcriptase and replicase (RNA-dependent RNA polymerase).

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** The virus binds to specific cell surface receptors and displays a strong neurotropic affinity, spreading via the bloodstream to reach target tissues including the neurons and the brain.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** The virion is internalized into the host cell via receptor-mediated endocytosis into a coated vesicle. Upon acidification of the endosome, the viral envelope fuses directly with the endosomal membrane.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** Following envelope fusion, the viral nucleocapsid is uncoated and released directly into the host cell cytoplasm.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** Viral replication occurs entirely in the host cell cytoplasm. Host ribosomes bind to the positive-sense RNA genome to translate early, full-length polyproteins (p230 or p270). These are proteolytically cleaved into the non-structural replication enzymes (NSP1 to NSP4). These enzymes assemble onto cellular membrane scaffolds within vesicles, where the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase generates a negative-sense RNA intermediate template. This template is then utilized to synthesize new full-length 42S positive-sense genomic mRNAs and a smaller 26S late subgenomic transcript that specifically encodes the viral structural proteins.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** The structural capsid (C) protein is translated first, cleaved, and assembled on the 42S genomic RNA. The envelope (E) glycoproteins are synthesized and glycosylated in the endoplasmic reticulum, then transported through the Golgi apparatus to the plasma membrane. The nucleocapsid associates with these glycoprotein-modified membrane regions and mature progeny virions exit by budding through the host cell plasma membrane. The virus is cytolytic to human cells but not to insect cells.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** The virus enters the bloodstream (viremia) and spreads to the central nervous system, where it directly infects and damages neurons and brain tissue, leading to encephalitis.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** Alphaviruses can often establish persistent infections in certain hosts, though specific reactivation triggers for WEEV are not fully detailed.
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The evolutionary recombination of [[EEEV]] and Sindbis virus genomes to form WEEV demonstrates a high degree of genetic adaptability.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** The initial host response involves robust production of interferon and pro-inflammatory cytokines, which directly cause the classic prodrome of flulike symptoms. The development of specific neutralizing antibodies can successfully block viremia, preventing progression to clinical encephalitis and systemic disease. Immunity is antibody-mediated and protects against second attacks.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Western equine encephalitis, which can range from a mild systemic febrile illness to overt viral encephalitis.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** The vast majority of infections are inapparent or asymptomatic.
*   **Incubation Period:** Ranges from 5 to 10 days.
*   **Classic Prodrome:** In older children and adults, the onset is gradual, featuring mild fever, malaise, headache, photophobia, nausea, vomiting, and sore throat, sometimes accompanied by meningism and drowsiness.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** N/A
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Progression to encephalitis occurs in a minority of cases (about 1 in 1000 infected adults, but 1 in 50 infected children). In these cases, fever and headache increase, accompanied by a deterioration of the conscious state and possibly flaccid or spastic paralysis. Infants experience a much more rapid clinical course involving fever, convulsions, and coma. The case fatality rate is 3% to 7% (highest in the elderly). Severe permanent sequelae are largely confined to infants.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Wild birds are the primary reservoir hosts involved in the maintenance and amplification cycle. Humans and equines are "dead-end" or accidental hosts because they do not generate sufficient viremia to transmit the virus back to mosquitoes.
*   **Transmission Route:** The virus is an arbovirus transmitted zoonotically via the bite of infected mosquitoes in a bird-mosquito-bird cycle. Transplacental transmission from mother to fetus can also occur in humans.
*   **Vectors:** Mosquitoes, predominantly Culex tarsalis (which feeds readily on birds and transmits the infection in the western USA) and Culiseta melanura (which transmits the virus in areas where Culex tarsalis does not occur, such as the eastern USA). Aedes mosquitoes may also act as vectors. Notably, Culex quinquefasciatus is resistant to infection by WEEV.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** WEEV is distributed across North America (rural Pacific, Mountain, and Southwest regions, as well as Saskatchewan) and South America (Argentina, Brazil, Guyana) and Mexico. While equine epizootics occur in South America, human infections there have not been described. In the USA, human infections average about 15 confirmed cases annually, though wide-scale outbreaks affecting the western USA and Canada have occurred historically (most recently in 1987). The infection-to-clinical-case ratio is heavily age-dependent: 50:1 for children under 5 years, and 1000:1 for individuals over 15 years.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** N/A
*   **Molecular Testing:** Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and nucleic acid amplification testing (NAT) on serum, blood, or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) are the preferred methods for identifying viral RNA.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Serological diagnosis utilizes hemagglutination-inhibition (HI), immunofluorescence assays (IFA), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Because all alphaviruses are antigenically related and share antigenic sites, precise identification of WEEV requires specific neutralization tests.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** The virus can potentially be recovered from blood during the early febrile period or from postmortem tissue by intracerebral inoculation of suckling mice or the use of susceptible mammalian or mosquito cell lines.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** There is no specific antiviral treatment available for WEEV. Clinical management relies entirely on supportive therapy.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** N/A
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** There is no commercially available vaccine for general human use. An experimental inactivated human vaccine is available on an investigational basis to protect high-risk laboratory workers. Effective killed-virus vaccines have been developed and are widely used to protect horses. Public health prevention focuses heavily on arthropod vector control (eliminating mosquito breeding sites, spraying insecticides) and personal protective measures (using insect repellents, wearing protective clothing, and ensuring adequate window screens).

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Togaviridae, Alphavirus, recombinant virus, Culex tarsalis, bird-mosquito-bird cycle, dead-end host, "shrink-wrap" envelope.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** WEEV represents a remarkable evolutionary exception among RNA viruses: genomic sequencing definitively proves that it was created by the genetic recombination of two completely distinct alphaviruses ([[EEEV|Eastern equine encephalitis virus]] and Sindbis virus) rather than evolving solely through standard mutation (antigenic drift). Additionally, while the Culex mosquito genus is generally a prolific arbovirus vector, the specific species Culex quinquefasciatus is uniquely resistant to infection by WEEV.
