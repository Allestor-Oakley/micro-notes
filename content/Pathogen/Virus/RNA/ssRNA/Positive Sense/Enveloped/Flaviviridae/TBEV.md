### Tick-borne encephalitis virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** The virus is a member of the genus *Flavivirus* within the family *Flaviviridae*. The viral genome consists of a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA molecule approximately 11 kilobases in size. The genomic RNA is infectious on its own. Three major subtypes of the virus exist: the European, Far-Eastern, and Siberian subtypes.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** The virus possesses an icosahedral nucleocapsid.
*   **Envelope Properties:** The virion is enveloped and contains two viral glycoproteins. The virus is inactivated by acid pH, heat, lipid solvents, detergents, bleach, phenol, 70% alcohol, and formaldehyde. The virus also possesses hemagglutinating ability.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** Virions are spherical in shape, measuring 40 to 60 nm in diameter.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** The positive-sense genome is translated into a single precursor polyprotein that is elaborated by posttranslational cleavage into structural and non-structural proteins (including the replicase and transcriptase). There are three structural polypeptides, two of which are glycosylated. All flaviviruses are antigenically related, leading to cross-reactive antibodies among different flavivirus infections.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** The virus binds to cell surface receptors and displays a pronounced neurotropism, gaining access to the central nervous system following initial amplification.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** The virion enters the host cell via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Upon acidification of the endosome, the viral envelope fuses with the endosomal membrane.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** Following membrane fusion, the viral RNA genome is uncoated and released directly into the host cell cytoplasm.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** Viral replication occurs entirely in the host cell cytoplasm. The positive-sense RNA genome functions as messenger RNA and is translated into a polyprotein. The viral replication complex generates a negative-sense RNA intermediate, which acts as a template for synthesizing new full-length positive-sense genomic RNA.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** Virion morphogenesis and assembly take place within intracellular vesicles, specifically within the endoplasmic reticulum. The virus undergoes glycoprotein maturation during intracellular transport and is released from the host cell via vesicle fusion at the plasma membrane (exocytosis).

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** After multiplying at the initial site of entry, the virus spreads through the reticuloendothelial system where it is further amplified. Upon invading the central nervous system, it causes direct neuronal destruction in the cerebral cortex, basal ganglia, cerebellar cortex, brainstem, and the anterior horns of the spinal cord.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** N/A
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The virus can be transmitted directly from an infected tick to an uninfected tick while they are "cofeeding" on the same vertebrate host. This highly specialized transmission mechanism does not require an active systemic viremic infection in the vertebrate host, allowing the virus to efficiently bypass host systemic immunity.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Infections are often inapparent or asymptomatic, controlled by the host's immune response (including interferon, cytokines, and neutralizing antibodies).

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Tick-borne encephalitis, a severe acute viral encephalitis.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** The infection may manifest as a biphasic illness, starting with a non-specific febrile phase, followed by remission, and returning with neurological involvement. It can present as aseptic meningitis.
*   **Incubation Period:** Flavivirus encephalitides generally have an incubation period of 4 to 21 days.
*   **Classic Prodrome:** When overt, the illness has a sudden onset featuring fever, headache, chills, myalgia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration, and malaise.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** N/A
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** The disease can progress to severe neurological involvement, including marked drowsiness, stupor, mental confusion, tremors, convulsions, and coma. It is notably associated with upper limb flaccid paralysis (particularly in cases similar to the Far-Eastern subtype/Russian spring-summer encephalitis). Permanent neurological sequelae can include paralysis, mental deterioration, personality changes, aphasia, and cerebellar signs. The Far-Eastern variant appears to be the most virulent, carrying a significant mortality rate.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Small mammals (especially wild rodents) are the primary natural reservoir. Ticks also serve as a reservoir due to their ability to maintain the virus transstadially (passing the virus to succeeding tick stages during molting) and transovarially (passing the virus to progeny eggs). Domestic animals such as goats, cows, and sheep can be infected and excrete the virus in their milk.
*   **Transmission Route:** The virus is an arbovirus primarily transmitted to humans (accidental hosts) via the bite of an infected tick. Uniquely for an arbovirus, humans can also acquire the infection via the gastrointestinal route by ingesting raw, unpasteurized milk from infected goats and cows grazing in tick-infested pastures. Rarely, transmission can occur through injured skin/mucosa or by inhalation (e.g., laboratory accidents). Person-to-person transmission has not been reported. Both male and female ticks are involved in transmission.
*   **Vectors:** Ixodid ticks, principally *Ixodes persulcatus* (in Russia, Siberia, and Asia) and *Ixodes ricinus* (in Europe).
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** About 10,000 to 12,000 cases of tick-borne encephalitis are reported annually, heavily concentrated in Russia, the Baltic states, and Slovenia. The disease exhibits a distinct seasonal incidence, peaking in early spring and summer (transmission lasts from April to October in endemic areas) when humans are most likely to be exposed during outdoor or forest activities.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** N/A
*   **Molecular Testing:** Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) can be used to detect the viral RNA genome in early serum samples, blood, or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Serological diagnosis is the standard approach, utilizing enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), complement fixation (CF), hemagglutination-inhibition (HI), or neutralization (N) tests. Because flavivirus antibodies (IgG) cross-react broadly, the specific identification of TBEV must often be confirmed by neutralization tests to differentiate it from other related viruses like Omsk hemorrhagic fever virus.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** The virus can be isolated from the blood during the initial febrile period, though routine viral culture is generally restricted to specialized biosafety laboratories.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** There is no specific antiviral treatment available for tick-borne encephalitis. Clinical management relies entirely on supportive care.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** Access to high-level critical care and life support is essential in managing severe encephalitic cases.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** Effective inactivated vaccines (produced in Austria, Germany, and Russia) are available. These vaccines are based on the European and Far-Eastern strains of the virus and are recommended for individuals traveling to endemic regions or engaging in high-risk outdoor activities. Personal protective measures, such as avoiding tick-infested areas, wearing protective clothing, and using insect repellents, are critical. Avoiding the consumption of unpasteurized dairy products in endemic areas also prevents the milk-borne transmission route.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** *[[Hard ticks|Ixodes]]* ticks, Russian spring-summer encephalitis, Far-Eastern subtype, raw goat/cow milk transmission, transstadial/transovarial tick transmission, upper limb flaccid paralysis, *Flaviviridae*, biphasic illness, cofeeding tick transmission.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** While arboviruses are defined by their reliance on arthropod vectors (insect bites) for transmission to vertebrate hosts, TBEV is a notable exception as it can successfully bypass the insect vector entirely and infect humans via the gastrointestinal tract following the ingestion of raw, unpasteurized milk from infected livestock. Additionally, TBEV exhibits "cofeeding" transmission, where the virus is passed directly from an infected tick to a susceptible tick feeding nearby on the same animal, circumventing the need for the animal to develop a high-titer systemic viremia.
