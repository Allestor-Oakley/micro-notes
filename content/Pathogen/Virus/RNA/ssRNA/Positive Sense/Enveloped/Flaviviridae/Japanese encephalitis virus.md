### Japanese encephalitis virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** Japanese encephalitis virus is a member of the genus *Flavivirus* within the family Flaviviridae. The viral genome consists of a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA molecule approximately 11 kilobases in size. The genomic RNA is infectious on its own.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** The virus possesses an icosahedral nucleocapsid.
*   **Envelope Properties:** The virion is enveloped and contains two viral glycoproteins. Like other flaviviruses, it is inactivated by acid pH, heat, lipid solvents, detergents, bleach, phenol, 70% alcohol, and formaldehyde. The virus also possesses hemagglutinating ability.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** Virions are spherical in shape, measuring 40 to 60 nm in diameter (specifically noted as 50 nm for JEV).
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** The genome translates into a single polyprotein that is subsequently cleaved into structural and non-structural proteins. There are three structural polypeptides, two of which are glycosylated. The virus shares significant antigens with [[St. Louis encephalitis virus]] (SLEV), [[West Nile virus]] (WNV), and Murray Valley encephalitis virus (MVEV).

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** The virus binds to cell surface receptors and displays a strong neuro-specific tropism, gaining access to the central nervous system following a systemic viremic phase.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** The virion enters the host cell via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Upon acidification of the endosome, the viral envelope fuses with the endosomal membrane.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** Following membrane fusion, the viral genome is uncoated and released directly into the host cell cytoplasm.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** Viral replication occurs entirely in the host cell cytoplasm. The positive-sense RNA genome functions as messenger RNA and is translated into a single precursor polyprotein, which undergoes posttranslational processing. The replication complex generates a negative-sense RNA intermediate, which acts as a template for synthesizing new full-length positive-sense genomic RNA.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** Virion morphogenesis and assembly take place within intracellular vesicles, specifically within the endoplasmic reticulum. The virus undergoes glycoprotein maturation during intracellular transport and is released via vesicle fusion at the plasma membrane (exocytosis).

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** After initial replication in lymphatic tissues and possibly other organs, the virus invades the central nervous system. In the brain, it causes areas of necrosis with small hemorrhages and perivascular cuffing in the grey matter of the cerebral cortex, thalamus, midbrain, cerebellum, brainstem, and anterior horns of the spinal cord.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** N/A
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** N/A
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Most human infections are asymptomatic, controlled by the host immune response; neuroinvasion only occasionally occurs. Neutralizing antibodies confer protection but cross-react broadly with other flaviviruses.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Japanese B encephalitis, a severe acute viral encephalitis.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Aseptic meningitis, flaccid paralysis, and Parkinsonian movement disorders. Infection during the first and second trimesters of pregnancy can result in intrauterine infection and fetal death.
*   **Incubation Period:** Ranges from 4 to 21 days.
*   **Classic Prodrome:** In overt infections, onset is sudden with severe headache, chills, fever, nausea, vomiting, generalized pains, and malaise. Within 24 to 48 hours, marked drowsiness develops, and the patient may become stuporous.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** N/A
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** The disease course can involve severe mental confusion, tremors, convulsions, and coma, with the fever lasting 4 to 10 days. The mortality rate is high, reaching 25% to 30%, and can exceed 80% in older age groups. Up to 30% to 50% of survivors are left with severe, permanent neurologic and psychiatric sequelae, including mental deterioration, personality changes, paralysis, aphasia, cerebellar signs, and involuntary movements (due to basal ganglia involvement).

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Wild birds (such as night herons and egrets) and pigs. Birds frequently carry the infection from rural to urban areas.
*   **Transmission Route:** The virus is an arbovirus transmitted zoonotically via the bite of infected mosquitoes. The virus is amplified by pigs and birds and then conveyed to humans, who act as accidental, dead-end hosts. Vertical transmission of JEV has also been demonstrated within *Culex* and *Aedes* mosquitoes.
*   **Vectors:** Mosquitoes, primarily of the *Culex* genus. *Culex tritaeniorhynchus* (a rice-field-breeding mosquito) is the main vector in northern Asia and Japan. Other vectors include *Culex annulirostris* (Guam and northern Australia), *Culex gelidus* and *Culex fusocephala* (India, Malaysia, Thailand), and *Culex vishnui* (India).
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** JEV is the leading cause of viral encephalitis in Asia, responsible for an estimated 50,000 cases and 10,000 deaths annually in China, Japan, Korea, and the Indian subcontinent. The estimated ratio of asymptomatic to symptomatic infections is 300 to 1. The main source of human infection is proximity to rice fields where the *Culex* vector breeds. The disease exhibits a seasonal summer incidence (June to September) in temperate northern regions, while in tropical areas, transmission is closely linked to local monsoonal weather patterns.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** N/A
*   **Molecular Testing:** Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) can identify viral RNA sequences. If available, it should be performed on early serum samples, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and brain tissue, as the viremia is short-lived.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Serology is the primary diagnostic method. Enzyme immunoassay (EIA) or immunofluorescence assay (IFA) is used to detect IgM antibodies, which are present in the serum in 80% of cases early in the illness and in virtually all cases by 10 days after onset. IgM may persist for weeks or months and is detectable in the CSF in most encephalitis cases. Because IgG and IgM antibodies cross-react broadly with other circulating flaviviruses, specificity must be confirmed by neutralization (N) tests or monoclonal antibody epitope-blocking enzyme immunoassays.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** The virus is occasionally cultured from human material (mainly CSF in severe cases) collected early in the infection. JEV can grow in mosquito cell lines, a wide range of mammalian cell lines, and suckling mouse brains. Antigen-detection methods can also be used on brain tissue.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** There is no specific antiviral treatment available. Management is entirely supportive, and access to high-level critical care is essential for surviving severe cases.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** Neither dexamethasone nor interferon-alpha has been shown to influence the clinical outcome of the disease.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** Several effective vaccines are available. An inactivated Vero cell culture-derived vaccine was licensed in the United States in 2009 for travelers. In Asian countries, both killed-virus and attenuated live-virus vaccines are utilized. Additional prevention strategies include personal protective measures such as wearing appropriate clothing, using insect repellents, and mosquito control efforts.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Rice-field-breeding mosquito, *Culex tritaeniorhynchus*, amplifying hosts (pigs and wading birds/egrets), Parkinsonian movement disorders, basal ganglia involvement, dead-end host.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** While most viral encephalitides present with generalized neurologic symptoms, Japanese B encephalitis frequently presents with distinctive Parkinsonian movement disorders and involuntary movements specifically due to viral targeting and destruction of the basal ganglia.
