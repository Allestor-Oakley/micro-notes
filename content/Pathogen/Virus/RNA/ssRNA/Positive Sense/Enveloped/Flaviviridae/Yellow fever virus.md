### Yellow fever virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** Yellow fever virus is the prototype member of the genus Flavivirus within the family Flaviviridae. The viral genome consists of a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA of approximately 11 kilobases in length. Based on sequence analysis, at least seven genotypes have been identified (five in Africa and two in South America), but there is only a single recognized serotype.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** The virion possesses an icosahedral nucleocapsid.
*   **Envelope Properties:** The nucleocapsid is tightly associated with and surrounded by a lipid envelope. The envelope is inactivated by acid pH, heat, lipid solvents, detergents, bleach, phenol, 70% alcohol, and formaldehyde. The virions can survive at 4°C for a month and can remain viable freeze-dried for many years. The virus possesses hemagglutinating ability.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** Virions are spherical and measure approximately 40 to 60 nm (with some sources noting a range of 25 to 65 nm) in diameter.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** The viral genome encodes three structural polypeptides (Capsid [C], pre-membrane [prM], and Envelope [E]) and seven non-structural proteins (NS1, NS2a, NS2b, NS3, NS4a, NS4b, and NS5). The envelope contains two glycoproteins, E1 and E2. The genome is translated into a single polyprotein representing the entire genome, with the genes encoding the structural proteins located at the 5' end.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** The virus binds to specific cell receptors and is internalized. It displays a tropism for [[Dendritic Cell|dendritic cells]], the [[Monocyte and Macrophage]] system, liver, kidneys, and heart.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** The virus enters the host cell within a coated vesicle via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Upon acidification of the endosome, the viral envelope fuses with the endosomal membrane.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** Fusion of the viral envelope with the endosomal membrane releases the nucleocapsid directly into the host cell cytoplasm.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** Replication occurs entirely in the cytoplasm. Host ribosomes bind to the positive-sense RNA genome to translate a full-length polyprotein. This polyprotein is cleaved to produce the nonstructural proteins, including an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. The replication enzymes assemble onto cellular membrane scaffolds within vesicles, and the polymerase transcribes the genome into a negative-sense RNA template. This template is then utilized to synthesize new full-length positive-sense mRNAs and genomes.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** Maturation and virion assembly occur within the endoplasmic reticulum (intracellular membranes). The structural glycoproteins are synthesized and glycosylated in the endoplasmic reticulum, transferred to the Golgi apparatus, and eventually to the plasma membrane. Virions mature by budding at intracellular membranes.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** Direct, cytopathic viral injury without inflammation is pathognomonic for YFV and is restricted mainly to the liver, kidneys, and heart. In the liver, YFV primarily infects hepatic zone 2, leading to vast midzone necrosis. The hepatic cytoplasm experiences microvesicular fatty changes (steatosis) caused by an abrogation of apoprotein synthesis, as well as the formation of Torres bodies. Hepatocyte necrosis is extensive, with up to 75% of the cell population becoming necrotic upon death. The virus also causes acute tubular necrosis in the kidneys and degenerative damage to myocardial cells.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** N/A
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** N/A
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** If the patient survives, the hepatic reticulin network is preserved, allowing for the complete recovery of the liver after infection without any residual fibrosis. Neutralizing antibodies can successfully block viremia. However, severe liver dysfunction leads to a reduction of coagulation factors which, along with consumptive coagulopathy, drives haemorrhagic diathesis.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Yellow fever is an acute, febrile, mosquito-borne illness. The majority of infections (up to 55%) are either asymptomatic or present as a mild systemic disease with flu-like symptoms.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Severe cases rapidly progress to life-threatening haemorrhagic fever characterized by massive liver and renal dysfunction, hemorrhage, and circulatory collapse.
*   **Incubation Period:** Ranges from 2 to 9 days (or 3 to 6 days).
*   **Classic Prodrome:** The clinical course presents in three stages: infection, remission, and intoxication. The initial "period of infection" is marked by fever and malaise. This is often followed by a "period of remission" (lasting up to 2 days) where symptoms temporarily subside.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Direct cytopathic viral injury without inflammation is highly characteristic. Specific clinical hallmarks include black vomit (coffee-ground hematemesis) and severe jaundice. Laboratory evaluation characteristically reveals aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels that exceed alanine transaminase (ALT) levels.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Progression to the "period of intoxication" brings severe jaundice, epigastric pain, bradycardia, oliguria, and profound haemorrhagic diathesis (presenting as coffee-ground hematemesis, melena, haematuria, metrorrhagia, and uncontrollable bleeding at needle-puncture sites). Shock and multi-organ system failure frequently ensue, carrying a case fatality rate between 20% and 50% in untreated, severe cases. While the liver and kidneys can fully recover, convalescence is marked by extended asthenia (weakness). Cardiac damage from the virus is irreversible, potentially leading to late-stage myocarditis, arrhythmia, and heart failure.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Non-human primates (e.g., monkeys, marmosets) serve as the natural evolutionary reservoirs in the sylvatic cycle. Humans can serve as amplifier hosts in the urban cycle.
*   **Transmission Route:** The virus is zoonotic and transmitted through the bite of an infected mosquito. There are three distinct transmission cycles. The sylvatic (jungle) cycle occurs between non-human primates and tree-hole breeding mosquitoes. The savannah (intermediate) cycle occurs on the edge of forested areas involving sylvatic mosquitoes and humans (common in Africa). The urban cycle involves human-to-human transmission mediated entirely by the *Aedes aegypti* mosquito.
*   **Vectors:** *Aedes aegypti* is the primary vector for the urban cycle globally. *Haemagogus* and *Sabethes* mosquito species are responsible for transmission in the jungle cycle in South America. Sylvan *Aedes* species (such as *Aedes africanus* and *Aedes simpsonii*) transmit the virus in the jungle and savannah cycles in Africa.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Yellow fever causes upwards of 170,000 clinical cases and 60,000 deaths annually. It is highly endemic in 13 countries in tropical South America and 33 countries in sub-Saharan Africa, as well as Trinidad. Epizootics, characterized by massive die-offs of Howler monkeys in South America, frequently serve as a precursor or sentinel event for a human outbreak. The spread of the primary urban vector, *Aedes aegypti*, into densely populated areas outside endemic regions places massive naive populations at high risk.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** Pathoanatomic liver histology reveals vast midzone necrosis (hepatic zone 2). Diagnostic features include the eosinophilic degradation of Kupffer cells and hepatocytes, and microvesicular fatty changes (steatosis) that do not resemble alcoholic steatosis. A major hallmark is the formation of characteristic Torres bodies in the hepatic cytoplasm, which occurs without cell nucleus displacement.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) analysis of blood or formalin-fixed tissues (liver, kidney, heart) is highly effective for genomic detection.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is used to detect IgM, which indicates recent infection. Because IgG antibodies cross-react broadly with other flaviviruses, specific diagnosis must be confirmed using neutralization (N) tests or monoclonal antibody epitope-blocking enzyme immunoassays. Wild-type YFV induces complement-fixing antibodies, whereas the 17D vaccine does not; therefore, the presence of complement-fixing antibodies can be utilized to differentiate a wild-type infection from prior vaccination.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** The virus can be propagated in embryonated chicken eggs, chick embryo cell cultures, and a variety of cell lines originating from monkeys, humans, hamsters, and mosquitoes.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** There are no specific antiviral treatments available against yellow fever virus.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** Treatment is strictly reliant on supportive care to address symptomology. Due to the severe haemorrhagic nature of the disease, diagnostic biopsies must be strictly avoided, as the resulting bleeding could be fatal to the patient.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** The disease is effectively controlled prophylactically by the administration of a live attenuated vaccine, the 17D strain (which was developed in 1937 from the original 'Asibi' strain). Mosquito control and the elimination of vector breeding sites are also essential public health measures.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Black vomit (coffee-ground hematemesis), Jaundice, Flavivirus prototype, *Aedes aegypti*, Torres bodies, Midzone necrosis, 17D vaccine, Sylvatic/Savannah/Urban cycles.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Direct cytopathic viral injury entirely *without* inflammation is pathognomonic for YFV, which uniquely preserves the hepatic reticulin network and allows for complete liver recovery without residual fibrosis. Furthermore, unlike alcoholic steatosis, the fat body formation (microvesicular fatty changes) in the liver during YFV infection is driven by an abrogation of apoprotein synthesis within hepatocytes. Finally, AST levels markedly exceeding ALT levels is characteristic of YFV, contrasting with many other forms of viral hepatitis.
