### Mumps virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** Mumps virus belongs to the family Paramyxoviridae, subfamily Paramyxovirinae, and the genus *Rubulavirus*. The viral genome consists of a single-stranded, linear, nonsegmented, negative-sense RNA of approximately 15 kilobases in length. The specific gene order on the genome is N-P/V/C-M-F-HN-L.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** The virus possesses a helical nucleocapsid that measures 18 nm in diameter. The nucleocapsid protein (N) encases the negative-strand virion RNA.
*   **Envelope Properties:** The virion is enveloped with a lipid bilayer. The viral matrix protein (M) underlies the lipid bilayer, while the hemagglutinin-neuraminidase (HN) attachment glycoprotein and the fusion (F) glycoprotein are inserted through the viral membrane. The envelope is thermolabile and easily inactivated by dryness and acid.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** Virions are spherical and pleomorphic, measuring 150 nm or more in diameter.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** The virus encodes several proteins: nucleocapsid protein (N), phosphoprotein (P), matrix protein (M), fusion glycoprotein (F), hemagglutinin-neuraminidase glycoprotein (HN), and large polymerase protein (L). The V protein is specifically found in rubulavirus virions. The HN protein possesses both hemagglutinin and neuraminidase activities, which facilitate attachment to host cells and virion release, respectively. The F protein mediates membrane fusion and exhibits hemolysin activity. The L and P proteins associate with the nucleocapsid to form a complex possessing RNA-dependent RNA transcriptase activity.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** The virus displays a tropism for the epithelial cells of the respiratory tract. The HN viral attachment glycoprotein binds to sialic acid on host cell glycolipids or proteins.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** Virions penetrate the host cell by fusion of the viral envelope directly with the plasma membrane, a process mediated by the F protein.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** Upon fusion, the virus delivers its negative-strand RNA genome and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase components directly into the host cell cytoplasm.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** The entire viral replication cycle occurs in the cytoplasm of susceptible cells. The virion polymerase (L) transcribes individual messenger RNAs (mRNAs) for each viral protein as well as a full-length positive-sense template from the genome. Replication of new negative-sense genomes from the positive-sense template then proceeds in the cytoplasm.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** Newly synthesized proteins associate with the replicated genomes, and the resulting nucleocapsids associate with the viral matrix protein and glycoprotein-modified areas of the plasma membrane. Mature virions exit the host cell by budding from the plasma membrane, a process that does not kill the host cell.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** The virus induces cell-to-cell fusion, leading to the formation of multinucleated giant cells known as syncytia.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** N/A
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The virus encodes specific proteins to evade type I interferon responses and other innate immunologic protections.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Cell-mediated immunity is absolutely essential for the control of the infection, but it is also responsible for causing immunopathogenesis, specifically the inflammatory responses that lead to glandular swelling and other symptoms. Antibody responses alone are insufficient to clear the infection because of the virus's ability to spread directly from cell to cell via syncytia formation.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Acute, benign viral parotitis, which presents as a painful swelling of the salivary (parotid) glands. However, involvement of the parotid gland is not an obligatory step in the infectious process, and many infections remain entirely asymptomatic.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** The virus spreads systemically via viremia and can cause orchitis, oophoritis, pancreatitis, aseptic meningitis, and meningoencephalitis. Less common eye complications include lacrimal gland swelling (dacryoadenitis), conjunctivitis, keratitis, iritis, scleritis, retinitis, and optic neuritis. Unilateral localized arrested tooth development can also occur.
*   **Incubation Period:** Ranges from 2 to 4 weeks, with typical manifestations appearing 14 to 18 days (or 16 to 20 days) post-exposure.
*   **Classic Prodrome:** N/A
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Parotitis (swelling of the parotid glands).
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Aseptic meningitis is a recognized complication, and prior to the widespread use of vaccines, mumps was the most common cause of viral encephalitis in the United States, occurring in approximately 1 in 6,000 infections. Mumps orchitis in male children prior to puberty frequently leads to sterility. Permanent hearing loss resulting from cochlear nerve involvement is another relatively common complication.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Humans are the only known natural hosts.
*   **Transmission Route:** The virus is highly communicable and spreads by direct person-to-person contact, inhalation of large-droplet aerosols, and exposure to respiratory droplets from the upper respiratory tract of infected individuals.
*   **Vectors:** N/A
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** In the absence of vaccination programs, infection rates reach 90% by the age of 15 years. The incidence of the infection peaks in winter and spring, particularly in crowded living or working conditions. The contagion period precedes clinical symptoms, and asymptomatic shedding can occur, making the spread of the virus virtually impossible to control except through immunization. Virus is actively shed in saliva from approximately 3 days before to 9 days after the onset of salivary gland swelling.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** Microscopic examination may reveal intracytoplasmic inclusions.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), saliva, urine, and nasopharyngeal swabs is the most sensitive diagnostic method for acute infection and can be used to identify specific virus strains for epidemiologic studies.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Serologic assays detecting specific IgM or demonstrating a fourfold rise in antibody titer to mumps antigen provide a decisive diagnosis of acute disease.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** The virus can be isolated in cell culture, with monkey kidney cells being the preferred medium. Because the virus is thermolabile, clinical samples must be inoculated shortly after collection. Mumps virus can be recovered from the urine for up to 2 weeks post-onset. For rapid diagnosis, immunofluorescence using mumps-specific antiserum can detect viral antigens in shell vial cell cultures as early as 2 to 3 days post-inoculation.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** There is no specific antiviral treatment available for mumps or its neurological complications. Clinical management is entirely supportive, typically utilizing analgesics.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** N/A
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** A highly effective, live attenuated mumps vaccine (Jeryl Lynn strain) is widely used. It is routinely administered as a component of the [[Measles virus|measles]]-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccine schedule. The vaccine confers lifelong immunity with an efficiency of 95%, and is administered after 12 months of age, with a recommended booster at 4 to 6 years of age or before junior high school.
