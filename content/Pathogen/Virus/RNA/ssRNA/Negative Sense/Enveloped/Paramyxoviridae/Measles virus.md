### Measles virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** The viral genome consists of a single-stranded, negative-sense, linear, non-segmented RNA molecule of approximately 15 kilobases in length.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** The genome is encased in a helical nucleocapsid measuring 13 or 18 nm in diameter. The nucleocapsid is formed by the association of the viral RNA with the nucleoprotein (N), phosphoprotein (P), and large (L) protein.
*   **Envelope Properties:** The virion is enveloped by a lipid bilayer that is easily inactivated by dryness, acid, heat, ultraviolet light, formalin, lipid solvents, and trypsin. The inner surface of the envelope is lined with the matrix (M) protein.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** The virus particles are large, spherical, and pleomorphic, generally measuring between 100 to 300 nm (typically 150 nm or more) in diameter.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** The virus contains six structural proteins: the nucleoprotein (N) which protects the viral RNA; the phosphoprotein (P) which is part of the transcription complex; the large (L) protein which acts as the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase; the matrix (M) protein which is essential for virion structure and assembly; and two transmembranous envelope glycoproteins—the hemagglutinin (H) viral attachment protein and the fusion (F) protein. Alternative transcripts of the P gene produce the nonstructural V and C proteins, which act as antagonists of innate host immune responses.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** The viral hemagglutinin (H) protein binds to specific host cell receptors: CD46 (a complement regulatory protein) and PVRL4 (nectin 4) on epithelial and endothelial cells, and CD150 (signaling lymphocyte-activation molecule (SLAM)) on [[Dendritic Cell|dendritic cells]], [[Monocyte|monocytes]], and B and T lymphocytes.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** Following attachment, the viral fusion (F) protein mediates the direct fusion of the viral envelope with the host cell plasma membrane at neutral pH, leading to the entry of the nucleocapsid into the cell. This fusion activity also induces cell-to-cell fusion, creating multinucleated giant cells (syncytia).
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** Upon fusion, the nucleocapsid delivers the negative-strand RNA genome and the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase components directly into the host cell cytoplasm.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** Transcription and translation occur entirely in the cytoplasm. The virion-associated L protein (polymerase) transcribes the negative-sense genome into individual messenger RNAs (mRNAs) for each viral protein, as well as a full-length positive-sense RNA template. The positive-sense template is then used to synthesize progeny negative-sense viral genomes. Viral glycoproteins (H and F) are synthesized by membrane-bound ribosomes, processed through the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, and delivered to the plasma membrane.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** New genomes associate with the N, L, and P proteins to form helical nucleocapsids in the cytoplasm. These nucleocapsids migrate to the cell surface, where they bind to the M protein at plasma membrane sites modified by the viral H and F glycoproteins. Progeny virions mature and exit the host cell by budding from the plasma membrane, a process that allows viral release without immediately killing the host cell, though infection ultimately results in cell lysis.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** The virus is cytolytic to B and T lymphocytes, [[Monocyte|monocytes]], and endothelial and epithelial cells. It induces widespread cell-to-cell fusion, resulting in the formation of massive multinucleated giant cells (syncytia) that contain both intranuclear and intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** The virus can establish a slow, persistent infection in the central nervous system. A defective variant of the virus, typically lacking functional M protein production and incapable of viral budding, spreads trans-synaptically to cause Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE) years after the initial infection. Subacute measles encephalitis (SME) can also manifest months after infection in immunocompromised hosts.
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The virus spreads directly from cell to cell via syncytia formation, effectively escaping neutralizing antibodies. The nonstructural V and C proteins antagonize type I interferon induction and signaling. Furthermore, direct viral infection and cytolysis of [[Monocyte|monocytes]] and memory T and [[B Cell|B cells]] erase prior immune memory and depress interleukin-12 (IL-12) and Th1-type [[T Cell|T-cell]] helper responses, leading to a profound, transient systemic immunosuppression that increases susceptibility to secondary infections for weeks, months, or up to two years.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Cell-mediated immunity is absolutely essential for viral clearance, but it is also the primary driver of disease pathogenesis (the characteristic rash is caused by immune T-cells targeting virus-infected endothelial and epithelial cells in the skin). Immunocompromised individuals, especially those with [[T Cell|T-cell]] deficiencies (e.g., severe [[HIV]]/AIDS, leukemia), fail to develop the characteristic rash and are at high risk for fatal giant cell pneumonia and subacute measles encephalitis. Vitamin A is critical for optimal effector [[T Cell|T-cell]] function and regulation; deficiency is linked to severe morbidity, blindness, and high mortality.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Typical measles (rubeola) is an acute, highly contagious systemic febrile illness and one of the classic childhood exanthems. It is characterized by high fever, severe respiratory symptoms, Koplik spots, and a widespread maculopapular rash.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Atypical measles occurs in patients previously immunized with older inactivated vaccines who are exposed to the wild-type virus; it presents abruptly with an intense distal rash, vesicles, petechiae, and purpura. Modified measles occurs in partially immune individuals (e.g., infants with residual maternal antibodies) and presents with a prolonged incubation, diminished prodrome, and mild rash. Giant cell pneumonia presents without a rash in severely immunocompromised patients.
*   **Incubation Period:** Typically 8 to 15 days (most commonly 10 to 14 days) from exposure to the onset of the rash.
*   **Classic Prodrome:** Lasts 2 to 4 days and consists of high fever, and the "3 C's and P": cough, coryza (runny nose), conjunctivitis (red, watery eyes), and photophobia, alongside lymphopenia.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Koplik spots, which are small, bright red spots with a bluish-white or white necrotic center located on the buccal mucosa opposite the lower molars. They typically appear 12 to 24 hours before the onset of the maculopapular rash.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Common complications include otitis media (most frequent, 5-9%), laryngotracheobronchitis (croup), diarrhea, and secondary bacterial pneumonia (the most common cause of death, accounting for 60% of measles fatalities). Ocular complications include keratoconjunctivitis, corneal ulceration, and keratomalacia leading to permanent blindness, particularly in Vitamin A-deficient patients. Neurologic complications include acute postinfectious encephalitis (acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM)), which has a 10-20% mortality rate and leaves residual neurological deficits, and Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE), a fatal degenerative central nervous system disease occurring 5 to 15 years post-infection.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Humans are the only natural host.
*   **Transmission Route:** The virus is highly contagious (attack rates >90% in susceptible individuals) and is transmitted via direct contact with infectious secretions or airborne spread through inhalation of large-droplet aerosols from the respiratory tract. Hematogenous transplacental transmission can also occur if the mother is infected during pregnancy. Patients are highly infectious from 4 days before to 4 to 5 days after the appearance of the rash.
*   **Vectors:** N/A
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** The virus is endemic worldwide, with incidence peaking in the late winter and early spring. Epidemics historically recurred in 1- to 3-year cycles when sufficient susceptible individuals accumulated. A population size of approximately 500,000 is required to sustain endemic transmission. Control relies on herd immunity, which requires >90% vaccine coverage. Measles is a notifiable disease, and contact and airborne precautions are recommended for hospitalized patients for 4 days following the onset of the rash (or for the duration of illness in immunocompromised hosts).

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** Microscopic examination of skin biopsies, respiratory secretions, urinary sediment, or Koplik spot smears reveals multinucleated giant cells (syncytia). Eosinophilic Cowdry type A intranuclear inclusions and intracytoplasmic inclusions are classically observed (e.g., in inclusion-body measles encephalitis or SSPE).
*   **Molecular Testing:** Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) is the most sensitive and preferred method for detecting viral RNA in nasopharyngeal swabs, respiratory secretions, blood, urine, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and brain tissue. Molecular genotyping of isolates is used for epidemiologic surveillance and differentiating wild-type from vaccine strains.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Diagnosis is typically confirmed by the detection of measles-specific IgM antibodies (which appear with the rash, peak at 10 days, and remain detectable for up to 1 month) using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). A four-fold or greater rise in measles-specific IgG titer between acute and convalescent sera (collected 2-3 weeks apart) is also diagnostic. In SSPE, exceptionally high titers of measles antibodies are found in the serum and CSF, often with a notable lack of antibody to the viral M protein.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** The virus can be isolated in monkey or human kidney cell lines, or a lymphoblastoid cell line (B95-a), but it grows slowly, taking 7 to 10 days for characteristic cytopathic effects (syncytia and inclusions) to appear. Shell vial cultures using fluorescent antibody staining can detect antigens in 2 to 3 days. Viral isolation is technically demanding and rarely attempted for routine diagnosis.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** There are no specific antiviral drugs approved for the treatment of measles. [[Ribavirin]] demonstrates in vitro activity against the virus, but its clinical benefit for routine treatment has not been proven.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** Treatment is primarily supportive (hydration, antipyretics). High-dose Vitamin A therapy (administered once daily for 2 days, with doses varying by age) is strictly recommended, especially in malnourished children, to reduce morbidity and mortality, particularly blindness. Antibiotics are prescribed only to treat secondary bacterial infections like otitis media or bacterial pneumonia.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** Passive immunization with human immune globulin can prevent or modify disease if given intramuscularly or intravenously within 6 days of exposure; this is highly indicated for unvaccinated contacts, infants under 1 year of age, pregnant women, and the immunocompromised. Active immunization relies on a live-attenuated viral vaccine (Schwartz or Moraten substrains of the Edmonston B strain) typically administered as part of the MMR or MMRV combination vaccine. The standard schedule includes a first dose at 12 to 15 months of age and a booster dose at 4 to 6 years of age. The live vaccine is strictly contraindicated in pregnant women and severely immunocompromised individuals.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Koplik spots, 3 C's and P (Cough, Coryza, Conjunctivitis, Photophobia), Warthin-Finkeldey giant cells (syncytia), Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE), Vitamin A deficiency, transient immunosuppression, paramyxovirus, morbillivirus, maculopapular rash.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike many other members of the *Paramyxoviridae* family (such as parainfluenza and [[Mumps virus|mumps]]), the measles virus hemagglutinin (H) attachment glycoprotein completely lacks neuraminidase activity. Furthermore, unlike typical RNA viruses that replicate exclusively in the cytoplasm, measles virus induces prominent intranuclear inclusion bodies in addition to intracytoplasmic inclusions (especially visible in central nervous system complications).
