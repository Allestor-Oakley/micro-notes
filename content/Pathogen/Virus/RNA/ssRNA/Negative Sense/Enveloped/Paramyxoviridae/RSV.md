### Respiratory Syncytial Virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** The viral genome is a linear, negative-sense, single-stranded, non-segmented RNA of approximately 15 kilobases in length. The order of the protein-coding regions follows the sequence: 3' NS1 - NS2 - N - P - M - SH - G - F - M2 - L 5'.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** The virus possesses a helical nucleocapsid (13 nm in diameter) that consists of the negative-sense, single-stranded RNA associated with the nucleoprotein (N), phosphoprotein (P), and large protein (L).
*   **Envelope Properties:** The nucleocapsid is surrounded by a fragile, pleomorphic lipid envelope. The viral matrix (M) protein lines the inner surface of the envelope. The envelope contains two large transmembrane glycoproteins (G and F) and two additional small envelope proteins (M2-1 and SH).
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** The virion is large, pleomorphic, and generally measures 150 nm or more in diameter, occasionally ranging up to 700 nm.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** The virus encodes multiple structural and non-structural proteins. The nucleocapsid contains the nucleoprotein (N) which maintains genomic structure, the phosphoprotein (P) which facilitates RNA synthesis, and the large protein (L) which is the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. The envelope contains the matrix (M) protein, the fusion (F) glycoprotein, the viral attachment glycoprotein (G), and small hydrophobic proteins (SH and M2-1). Unlike other paramyxoviruses, the larger surface glycoprotein (G) of RSV lacks hemagglutinating and neuraminidase activities. The F protein exhibits membrane fusion activity but no hemolysin activity. Non-structural proteins include NS1 and NS2.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** Infection is initiated by the binding of the viral G glycoprotein to many different cell-surface proteins and heparan sulfate proteoglycans on the host cell. The virus exhibits a strong tropism for the ciliated respiratory epithelium.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** Upon binding, the viral fusion (F) protein folds over to expose hydrophobic regions and facilitates the direct fusion of the viral envelope with the host cell's plasma membrane, bypassing endosomal internalization.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** Following fusion, the viral nucleocapsid is released directly into the host cell cytoplasm.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** The entire replication cycle takes place in the host cell cytoplasm. The RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (L and P proteins) is carried directly into the cell within the nucleocapsid. The polymerase transcribes the negative-sense genome into individual messenger RNAs (mRNAs) for each gene and synthesizes a full-length positive-sense antigenome template. Viral proteins are synthesized in the cytoplasm, while the viral glycoproteins are synthesized and glycosylated through the host cell's secretory pathway (endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus).
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** Full-length progeny negative-sense genomes are copied from the positive-sense antigenome template. Newly formed genomes associate with N, P, and L proteins to assemble into helical nucleocapsids. These nucleocapsids migrate to the cell surface, where they associate with M proteins at plasma membrane sites modified by the viral G and F glycoproteins. Mature virions exit the host cell by budding from the plasma membrane without lysing the cell.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** The virus induces direct cell-to-cell fusion, resulting in the formation of multinucleated giant cells known as syncytia. The cytopathologic spread of the virus causes necrosis of the bronchi and bronchioles, leading to the formation of physical "plugs" made of mucus, fibrin, and necrotic material within the smaller airways.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** N/A
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** Respiratory syncytial virus is not an effective inducer of host interferon. The virus's ability to spread directly from cell to cell via syncytia formation allows it to evade detection by neutralizing antibodies.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Cell-mediated immunity is essential for recovery but is also largely responsible for mediating the immunopathologic cell injury (bronchiolitis) during infection. Susceptibility to severe bronchiolitis is genetically linked to polymorphisms in host innate immunity genes, and disease severity is correlated with the production of virus-specific IgE antibodies in respiratory secretions. Immunocompromised patients, especially [[bone marrow]] transplant recipients or those with impaired cell-mediated immunity, may become persistently infected, shed the virus for months, and face pneumonia mortality rates ranging from 20% to 80%. Maternal antibodies from unvaccinated mothers are insufficient to protect infants from primary infection.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** RSV is the most important viral cause of severe lower respiratory tract illness in infants and young children, primarily causing bronchiolitis and pneumonia. In older children and adults, it typically causes the common cold, febrile rhinitis, and pharyngitis.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** The virus is an important cause of acute otitis media (accounting for 30% to 50% of wintertime episodes in infants). In the elderly, infections may cause symptoms similar to [[influenza]] virus disease and can progress to severe pneumonia. RSV is also a major trigger for asthma exacerbations and reactive airway disease.
*   **Incubation Period:** 3 to 5 days.
*   **Classic Prodrome:** Mild upper respiratory tract symptoms with prominent rhinorrhea (runny nose) frequently precede lower respiratory tract involvement.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** In infants with bronchiolitis, signs include tachypnea, tachycardia, low-grade fever, expiratory wheezes over the lungs, air trapping, and decreased ventilation caused by mucus and fibrin plugs obstructing the narrow airways.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Severe airway obstruction in infants can rapidly culminate in acute respiratory failure and death, particularly in premature neonates or infants with underlying congenital heart disease or chronic lung disease. Children who recover from severe RSV bronchiolitis frequently exhibit recurrent episodes of wheezing illness for many years.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Humans are the only natural host.
*   **Transmission Route:** The virus is highly contagious and is transmitted via large-droplet aerosols, direct contact, and fomites (the virus can survive on environmental surfaces for up to 6 hours). The main portals of entry are the nose and eyes. Infection is nearly universal; almost all children are infected by 2 years of age. Natural infection does not confer lifelong immunity, and reinfections occur frequently throughout life.
*   **Vectors:** N/A
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** RSV causes devastating nosocomial infections in nurseries and pediatric hospital wards. Strict contact isolation precautions, including handwashing, restriction of visitors, and the wearing of gowns, goggles, and masks by medical personnel, are required to manage outbreaks.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** Histopathologic examination of infected cells reveals syncytia (multinucleated giant cells) containing prominent acidophilic cytoplasmic inclusions.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) genome analysis from nasopharyngeal washes, aspirates, and swabs is the preferred and most sensitive method for diagnosis.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Serologic measurements of serum antibodies are useful for epidemiologic studies but are typically not used for clinical decision-making.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** The virus is extremely labile and difficult to isolate in culture; clinical specimens must be inoculated immediately as freezing causes complete loss of infectivity. Human heteroploid cell lines (HeLa and HEp-2) are the most sensitive. Cytopathic effects (syncytia and giant cells) may take up to 10 days to appear. Because RSV lacks hemagglutinin, it cannot be detected using hemagglutination or hemadsorption assays. Rapid antigen assays (ELISA) from respiratory secretions are also available but are less sensitive than nucleic acid testing.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** Aerosolized [[ribavirin]] (a guanosine analog) is approved for the treatment of lower respiratory tract disease caused by RSV in high-risk infants, though its use is infrequent.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** Clinical management of severe RSV disease relies heavily on supportive care, including the administration of oxygen, intravenous fluids, and nebulized cold steam.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** Prophylactic passive immunization using humanized antiviral monoclonal antibodies (such as Nirsevimab, [[Palivizumab]], or Motavizumab) or high-titer anti-RSV immune globulin is available for premature infants and children at high risk. Active immunization is available via mRNA subunit vaccines expressing a stabilized pre-fusion conformation of the RSV F glycoprotein. The unadjuvanted vaccine (Abrysvo) is administered to pregnant women between 32 and 36 weeks of gestation to passively protect the fetus and newborn, while adjuvanted vaccines (Arexvy) are indicated for adults over 60 years of age. Historically, an experimental formalin-inactivated RSV vaccine in the 1960s resulted in enhanced, frequently fatal lower respiratory tract illness in children upon subsequent natural exposure.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Bronchiolitis, multinucleated giant cells (syncytia), paramyxovirus, pneumovirus, Nirsevimab/[[Palivizumab]] prophylaxis, winter/fall epidemics, pre-fusion F glycoprotein vaccine, mucus and fibrin plugs in airways.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike the closely related respiroviruses and rubulaviruses (which also belong to the Paramyxoviridae family), RSV entirely lacks both hemagglutinating and neuraminidase activities on its attachment glycoprotein (G).
