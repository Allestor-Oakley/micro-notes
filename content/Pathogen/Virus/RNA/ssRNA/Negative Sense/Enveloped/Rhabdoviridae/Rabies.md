### Rabies

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** Contains a linear, single-stranded, negative-sense, nonsegmented RNA genome.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** Features a helical nucleocapsid.
*   **Envelope Properties:** The virion is enveloped. Spikes composed of a trimer of the glycoprotein (G) cover the surface of the virus. The virus is rapidly destroyed by ultraviolet radiation, heating at 56°C for 1 hour, ether treatment, trypsin treatment, lipid solvents, bleach, and phenolic disinfectants.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** Bullet-shaped virion with a diameter of 50 to 95 nm and length of 130 to 380 nm.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** It is a simple virus, encoding only five proteins: Nucleoprotein (N), Phosphoprotein (P), Matrix protein (M), Glycoprotein (G), and a large RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (L). The N protein binds to the RNA genome; the P protein associates with the L protein and has regulatory functions; the M protein lines the inside of the envelope and facilitates assembly; the G protein is a simple glycoprotein with an N-linked glycan that forms surface spikes, acts as the viral attachment protein, binds to receptors on target cells, and generates neutralizing antibodies; the L protein acts as the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** The viral G-glycoprotein serves as the viral attachment protein, binding to specific receptors on target cells. It exhibits a strong tropism for neural tissue (neurotropic) and salivary glands.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** N/A
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** N/A
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** Replicates entirely within the host cell cytoplasm. The virus encodes its own RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (L protein) to transcribe the negative-sense RNA genome into messenger RNAs and replicate the genome.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** The M (matrix) protein facilitates assembly. The virion matures and acquires its envelope by budding from the host cell membrane.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** N/A
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** N/A
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The virus spreads quietly along neurons to the central nervous system (brain) and subsequently to the salivary glands. Infection is largely undetectable by the host immune system until it reaches the brain, which is often too late for effective treatment.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Antibody is produced, but typically only after the virus has already reached the brain. The incubation period depends on the proximity of the initial bite/wound to the central nervous system and the infectious dose received.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Rabies, an acute, fulminant, and fatal encephalitis.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Can present as a paralytic syndrome in approximately 20% of cases.
*   **Incubation Period:** Typically 1 to 3 months, but highly variable; it can range from as short as 1 week to more than a year. It is usually shorter in children than in adults.
*   **Classic Prodrome:** Lasts 2 to 10 days and includes nonspecific symptoms: malaise, anorexia, headache, photophobia, nausea and vomiting, sore throat, and fever. Usually, there is an abnormal sensation (paresthesia) around the wound or bite site.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Following the prodrome, an acute neurologic phase develops, marked by severe agitation, confusion, hallucinations, combativeness, generalized muscle twitching, hand spasms, sweating on the face and trunk, difficulty breathing, hypersalivation, and dysphagia (painful swallowing/hydrophobia). Seizures and cardiac arrest can occur.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** The neurologic phase rapidly progresses to coma, renal failure, respiratory failure, and death. Once clinical symptoms appear, the disease is almost universally fatal. Rabies has the highest case fatality rate of any known infectious disease. Documented survivors of clinical rabies encephalitis are extremely rare.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Widely distributed zoonosis found in all warm-blooded animals. Natural reservoirs include dogs, cats, foxes, badgers, skunks, raccoons, and bats. It does not infect insects.
*   **Transmission Route:** Transmitted to humans primarily through bites from rabid animals or by direct contact with infectious saliva from rabid animals (when the skin integrity has been breached, even without bleeding). Inhalation of aerosolized virus in heavily infested bat caves has also caused infections.
*   **Vectors:** None (Not vector-borne via arthropods).
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Found worldwide, except in a few island nations. Most rabies deaths globally occur in Asia and Africa, overwhelmingly resulting from the bites of infected domestic dogs. In the United States and the Americas, dog-mediated transmission has been largely controlled, and most human cases are now acquired from wild animals; specifically, bat bites have caused the majority of human rabies cases in the United States since the 1990s. Human-to-human transmission is extremely rare but has occurred via organ and corneal transplantation. Due to severe under-reporting and misdiagnosis, global control efforts are frequently insufficiently prioritized.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** Direct microscopic examination of infected neurons in brain tissue obtained at biopsy or autopsy reveals pathognomonic eosinophilic intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies known as Negri bodies.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) is highly sensitive and used to detect viral genomic RNA in tissue extracts, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), or saliva.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Anti-rabies antibodies in the serum or CSF can be detected, but usually only late in the disease after the virus has reached the brain. There are no tests available to diagnose rabies infections in humans before the onset of clinical disease.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** Direct fluorescent antibody (DFA) staining or immunofluorescence is the method of choice for routine diagnosis, detecting viral antigens in brain smears, corneal impressions, or skin biopsies taken from the nape of the neck.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** There is no known antiviral agent effective against clinical rabies. Trials with drugs like [[ribavirin]], acyclovir, and interferons have been attempted but typically fail once clinical symptoms develop.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** There is no successful treatment once clinical symptoms of rabies develop; care is strictly palliative and intensive. Survival is exceptionally rare.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:**
    *   **Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP):** Urgent and nearly 100% successful if administered promptly before the onset of symptoms. The regimen involves thoroughly washing the wound, instilling human rabies immune globulin (passive immunization) directly into and around the wound site, and administering a series of intramuscular or intradermal injections of a killed/inactivated rabies virus vaccine.
    *   **Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP):** Recommended for individuals at high occupational risk, such as veterinarians, animal handlers, and laboratory workers.
    *   **Vaccine Types:** Human vaccines are killed, inactivated virus vaccines grown in human diploid fibroblasts (e.g., MRC-5), rhesus fetal lung diploid cells, or chicken fibroblasts. Animal vaccines include inactivated vaccines for domestic pets and live-attenuated or recombinant vaccinia-rabies glycoprotein virus hybrid vaccines used for wildlife (e.g., distributed in bait for forest mammals).

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Negri bodies (intracytoplasmic eosinophilic inclusions), bullet-shaped virion, hydrophobia (fear of water due to painful swallowing), bat exposure, post-exposure prophylaxis, human diploid cell vaccine.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Rabies exhibits an exceptionally variable and long incubation period (sometimes taking over a year) because the virus must physically travel via retrograde axonal transport along peripheral neurons from the bite site to the brain before it can cause disease or be detected by the immune system. Additionally, unlike many viral encephalitides that have significant survivor populations with neurological sequelae, untreated clinical rabies is virtually 100% fatal.
