### Lassa virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** Contains a double-segmented (designated L and S), circular, single-stranded RNA genome. The overall genome size is 10 to 14 kb. The S genome segment exhibits an unusual ambisense coding strategy, while the L segment is negative-sense.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** The virion uniquely and actively incorporates nonfunctional host cell ribosomes during maturation, which gives the internal core a characteristic "sandy" or grainy appearance under electron microscopy (deriving its family name from the Latin *arenosus* for "sandy").
*   **Envelope Properties:** Enveloped.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** Spherical and pleomorphic, ranging from 50 to 300 nm in diameter, with a mean diameter of 110 to 130 nm.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** The virus encodes four major polypeptides. The virion packages its own transcriptase (RNA-dependent RNA polymerase).

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** Exhibits tropism for the vasculature, liver, and other organs, and spreads via the blood to target tissues including the central nervous system and neurons.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** N/A
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** N/A
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** Replicates entirely in the host cell cytoplasm. The negative-strand RNA genome serves as a template for individual mRNAs, but the full-length positive-strand RNA template is required for genomic replication.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** Assembles by incorporating host ribosomes into the particle and matures by budding from the host cell plasma membrane.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** N/A
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** The virus establishes a persistent, chronic, and asymptomatic infection in its natural rodent hosts (*Mastomys natalensis*), leading to long-term shedding.
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** Fatal infections in acute Lassa fever have been associated with uniquely low levels of interleukin-8 and interferon-inducible protein-10 in the serum.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** The characteristic prodrome of flulike symptoms is heavily driven by the host's interferon and cytokine responses (immunopathogenesis). Neutralizing antibodies can block the disease and viremia.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Lassa fever (an African viral haemorrhagic fever).
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Benign febrile cases can and do occur. It frequently causes severe disease in pregnant women, leading to fetal death in more than 75% of pregnant patients. When infection occurs during the third trimester, maternal mortality is significantly increased (30%), and fetal mortality is extremely high (>90%).
*   **Incubation Period:** Usually 1 to 2 weeks (e.g., 7 to 18 days), up to a maximum of 21 days.
*   **Classic Prodrome:** Gradual onset of an acute febrile illness characterized by severe flulike symptoms, lethargy, malaise, high fever, severe headache, sore throat, myalgia, and swollen [[lymph nodes]], tonsils, and [[spleen]].
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Progresses to gastrointestinal and systemic symptoms including abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, oral ulcers, bleeding gums, vomiting blood (haematemesis), and chest pain. Microvascular instability with capillary leak and impaired haemostasis are the pathogenic hallmarks.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** The disease can progress to severe haemorrhagic diathesis, hypovolemic shock, multi-organ system failure, and death. Long-term sequelae are common in survivors, particularly neurological complications such as deafness (hearing loss) during convalescence. Other neurological features may include frank encephalitis, meningitis, and seizures. Mortality rates are approximately 15% for hospitalized patients, though overall case fatality is estimated at about 1%.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** The principal reservoir is the "multimammate rat" or house rat (*Mastomys natalensis*). This rodent is almost always found in close association with humans in rural villages and surrounding cultivated fields, and less commonly in grasslands or forest edges.
*   **Transmission Route:** Zoonotic transmission occurs primarily via the inhalation of infectious aerosols generated from rodent urine or feces. Infection is also acquired by the consumption of rodents as food, or direct contact with fomites contaminated with mouse saliva, feces, or urine. Human-to-human transmission is well documented and highly contagious, occurring through direct contact with virus-contaminated blood, tissues, and body fluids (frequently causing nosocomial outbreaks in hospital settings).
*   **Vectors:** None.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Highly endemic in West Africa, encompassing countries situated between Senegal and the Republic of Congo (e.g., Sierra Leone, Liberia, Nigeria). Estimates suggest several hundred thousand infections and 5000 deaths annually. Poor-quality housing that allows rodent entry is a major risk factor. Foreign military personnel, peacekeepers, and aid workers are occasionally infected and may import the disease to other countries. The risk of rodent transmission in large urban centers is negligible. Because of its high virulence and transmissibility, isolation of the virus and specimen processing must be performed in Biosafety Level 4 maximum containment facilities. Meticulous barrier nursing procedures and standard precautions are essential in clinical settings to prevent nosocomial spread.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** Immunohistochemistry can be used to detect viral antigens in postmortem tissue specimens.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assays using throat specimens, urine, or blood are highly sensitive for detecting arenavirus RNA in patient specimens and are heavily utilized in research and public health laboratories.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** A combined ELISA Ag/IgM assay is found to be highly sensitive and specific for the diagnosis of Lassa fever. Detection of both IgM and IgG antibodies by ELISA is standard.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** Viral isolation and culture are possible but extremely hazardous; they must strictly be performed in high-containment BSL-4 laboratories due to the severe risk of fatal laboratory-acquired infections.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** Intravenous [[ribavirin]] (a guanosine analog that induces viral hypermutation) is the effective drug of choice and is routinely utilized in the management of Lassa fever in endemic settings. Optimised supportive care, including critical care interventions for shock and volume depletion, is critical.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** Meticulous clinical management of fluid balance is vital due to prominent capillary leak syndrome. [[Ribavirin]] administration must be timely; a 10-day course of intravenous [[ribavirin]] has a case-fatality rate of 5% if started within the first week of fever onset, compared to >20% if initiated later.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** There is no currently licensed, widely distributed commercial vaccine available for Lassa virus. Prevention relies predominantly on rodent control measures (which are often impractical in endemic areas), improving housing quality to prevent rodent entry, and strict adherence to universal precautions and barrier nursing in hospitals. Oral [[ribavirin]] has been evaluated and proposed as post-exposure prophylaxis for Lassa fever exposures.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Sandy" virion appearance, host ribosomes packaged in virion, ambisense S genome segment, *Mastomys natalensis* (multimammate rat), West Africa, deafness during convalescence, [[ribavirin]] efficacy, Old World arenavirus, Lassa fever.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike almost all other virus families, arenaviruses actively incorporate nonfunctional host ribosomes into the mature virus particle during assembly, giving the virion a unique "sandy" appearance under electron microscopy. Additionally, Lassa virus demonstrates a strikingly high fetal and maternal mortality rate during the third trimester of pregnancy (>90% and 30%, respectively), distinguishing its severity in pregnant populations compared to many other viral pathogens.
