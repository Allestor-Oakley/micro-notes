### Ebola virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** The viral genome is a large (19 kb), linear, single-stranded, negative-sense, nonsegmented RNA. The extreme 3' and 5' ends of the genome contain complementary leader and trailer sequences, respectively. The genome organization contains seven genes, which begin with a conserved transcriptional start site and end with a transcriptional stop (polyadenylation) site. Adjoining genes are either separated by an intergenic region or overlap one another.
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** Helical nucleocapsid.
*   **Envelope Properties:** The virion is enveloped with a lipid bilayer membrane. The envelope contains a single type of glycoprotein (GP) that makes up the viral surface spikes in the form of trimers 10 nm in length. Viral infectivity is destroyed by heating for 30 minutes at 60°C, by ultraviolet and gamma irradiation, by lipid solvents, and by bleach and phenolic disinfectants.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** Pleomorphic, appearing as long filamentous threads or odd-shaped forms. Virions are 80 nm in diameter and vary in length from 800 nm to 1400 nm (often averaging around 1000 nm, with a unit length of 805 nm for Ebola), though some filamentous forms can be >10,000 nm. The particles may contain none, one, or multiple genomes.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** Encodes seven proteins with multiple activities: Nucleoprotein (NP) is part of the nucleocapsid and is involved in transcription and replication. VP35 is a polymerase cofactor and inhibits innate immune responses. VP40 is the matrix protein that lines the inside of the envelope and facilitates assembly. GP is the envelope glycoprotein that binds to receptors; an unusual coding strategy requires transcriptional editing (the addition of an 'A' nucleotide) or translational frame-shifting to express the full-length GP. The primary gene product of the GP gene is SGP, a shorter, nonstructural secreted glycoprotein. VP30 is a transcriptional activator. VP24 is associated with the RNA in the nucleocapsid, initiates budding, and inhibits innate immune responses. L protein is the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, which also inhibits innate immune responses.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** Exhibits tropism for cells of the [[Monocyte and Macrophage]] system, [[Dendritic Cell|dendritic cells]], interstitial fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. The viral glycoprotein (GP) binds to Niemann-Pick C1 (NPC1), a cholesterol transfer protein, and [[T-Cell|T-cell]] immunoglobulin and mucin domain 1 (TIM-1), which is also the [[HAV|hepatitis A virus]] receptor.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** Enters the host cell after receptor binding.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** N/A
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** Replicates in the host cell cytoplasm. The RNA-dependent RNA polymerase transcribes the negative-sense RNA genome into mRNAs.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** Virions mature and assemble by budding from the host plasma membrane, a process initiated by the VP24 protein.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** The virus replicates efficiently, producing large amounts of virus and causing extensive cytopathogenesis and tissue necrosis in parenchymal cells of the liver, [[Spleen]], [[Lymph Nodes]], and lungs. Infection of endothelial cells prevents the production of cell adhesion proteins and causes cytolysis, leading to vascular injury, capillary leakage, and microvascular instability.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** Survivors can harbor persistent virus in immune-privileged sanctuary sites, such as the eye (for at least 9 weeks) and semen (up to 61 days), which can serve as a source for secondary transmission.
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The primary gene product of the GP gene is SGP, a secreted glycoprotein that acts as a decoy antigen to inhibit antibody action. Additionally, the VP35, VP24, and L proteins all actively interfere with and inhibit the host's interferon-mediated innate immune responses. Filovirus infections also appear to be directly immunosuppressive, and fatal cases often demonstrate impaired humoral immune responses.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Cell-mediated immunity is essential for the control of infection. Viral replication in [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]], [[Monocyte and Macrophage|monocytes]], and [[Dendritic Cell|dendritic cells]] elicits a "cytokine storm" of proinflammatory cytokines (similar to a superantigen-induced storm), which drives severe systemic inflammatory responses and sepsis-like symptoms. Upon recovery, detectable neutralizing antibodies are produced.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Ebola Virus Disease (EVD), a severe and frequently fatal viral haemorrhagic fever.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Post-Ebola syndrome in survivors, characterized by myalgia, arthralgia, headaches, neurological problems, dry eyes, and uveitis.
*   **Incubation Period:** 2 to 21 days (mean time to onset is typically 8 to 10 days).
*   **Classic Prodrome:** Abrupt onset of fever, malaise, lethargy, headache, sore throat, and muscle pain (myalgia).
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Initial symptoms are followed by severe gastrointestinal manifestations (abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) and a maculopapular rash. Widespread hemorrhage (internal and external bleeding, seen in 30-50% of patients), edema, thrombocytopenia, and bleeding from multiple sites occur.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Rapid progression to severe haemodynamic instability, massive fluid maldistribution (capillary leak), hypovolemic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulopathy (DIC), multi-organ system failure, and death. Mortality rates range from 25% to 90%, depending on the viral species. The mean time to death from the onset of symptoms is 7 to 8 days.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Suspected to be African fruit bats (e.g., Egyptian fruit bat).
*   **Transmission Route:** Zoonotic spillover occurs through contact with infected wildlife (e.g., bats, non-human primates). Highly contagious human-to-human transmission occurs via direct contact with the blood, secretions, organs, or other bodily fluids (urine, sweat, breast milk, semen) of infected individuals. Exposure frequently occurs in healthcare settings (nosocomial transmission) and during customary burial practices involving close contact with deceased relatives.
*   **Vectors:** None known (transmitted directly from animal reservoirs or human-to-human).
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Handled exclusively under Biosafety Level 4 (BSL-4) maximum containment conditions. Requires stringent quarantine of infected patients, specialized viral haemorrhagic fever barrier nursing precautions, extensive use of personal protective equipment (PPE), and proper disposal of infectious items.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** Immunohistochemistry can be utilized for post-mortem analysis to detect viral antigens in skin or tissue biopsies.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) of clinical specimens (blood, secretions) is the most sensitive and primary diagnostic method.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) can detect viral antigens in serum for rapid screening, or detect IgG/IgM antibodies as patients recover. Commercial lateral flow tests are available for near-patient testing with immediate results, though they possess lower sensitivity than RT-PCR.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** Fresh virus isolates can be cultured in Vero and MA-104 monkey cell lines, but this requires extreme BSL-4 biocontainment. Electron microscopy can be used to view the unique filamentous viral structure directly.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:** Specific monoclonal antibody therapies have shown survival benefits and are approved for the Zaire ebolavirus species (e.g., a combination of three monoclonal antibodies or a single anti-glycoprotein monoclonal antibody). Remdesivir and Favipiravir have been evaluated in clinical trials but did not show clear efficacy signals in human disease.
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** Meticulous, optimized supportive care and critical care interventions to manage shock, volume depletion, and electrolyte imbalances are the mainstay of treatment.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** An effective, live-attenuated recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus (rVSV) vaccine is approved for the Zaire strain (ERVEBO, also known as V920 or rVSVΔG-ZEBOV-GP). It substitutes the VSV glycoprotein with the Ebola glycoprotein. It is highly protective and used for healthcare workers, laboratory workers, and at-risk populations older than 1 year.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Filovirus, filamentous/thread-like morphology, haemorrhagic fever, BSL-4, African fruit bat reservoir, Zaire strain, cytokine storm, secreted GP decoy, ERVEBO vaccine.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike most viral glycoproteins that are simply translated, the Ebola virus GP requires a highly unusual transcriptional editing mechanism (the insertion of an extra 'A' residue) or a translational frameshift to produce the full-length functional envelope spike; without this shift, the virus produces a truncated, secreted decoy glycoprotein instead.
