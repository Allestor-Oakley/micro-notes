### Influenza Virus

#### Molecular Virology & Structure
*   **Genomic Architecture:** Contains a segmented, linear, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA genome. Influenza A and B have eight separate segments, while Influenza C has seven. The overall genome size is approximately 13.6 kb. The segments range from 890 to 2340 bases. Each segment encodes one or two proteins (segments 1-6 encode one protein each; segments 7 and 8 encode two proteins each via differential splicing).
*   **Capsid & Nucleocapsid:** Helical nucleocapsid (9 nm in diameter). The genomic RNA segments are associated with the nucleoprotein (NP) and the transcriptase complex (PB1, PB2, PA) to form individual ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes.
*   **Envelope Properties:** Enveloped. The lipid envelope is lined on the inside by the matrix (M1) protein. The envelope contains viral hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) glycoproteins as surface spikes. For Influenza A, it also contains the M2 ion channel protein. The envelope is easily inactivated by dryness, acid, heat (56°C), lipid solvents, and detergents. Virion composition is approximately 1% RNA, 73% protein, 20% lipid, and 6% carbohydrate.
*   **Virion Size & Physical Morphology:** Spherical and pleomorphic, ranging from 80 to 120 nm in diameter. It can sometimes appear tubular or filamentous.
*   **Structural & Non-Structural Proteins:** The virus encodes nine structural proteins and one to two nonstructural proteins.
    *   **PB2, PB1, PA:** Polymerase components that facilitate replication and transcription.
    *   **HA (Hemagglutinin):** Viral attachment protein and fusion protein; forms trimers; elicits neutralizing and protective antibodies.
    *   **NA (Neuraminidase):** Cleaves sialic acid to promote virus release and prevents clumping; forms tetramers.
    *   **NP (Nucleocapsid protein):** Binds RNA.
    *   **M1 (Matrix protein):** Viral structural protein that interacts with the nucleocapsid and envelope to promote assembly.
    *   **M2 (Membrane protein):** Forms a membrane ion channel that facilitates uncoating and proper assembly (target for [[amantadine]] in Influenza A).
    *   **NS1 (Nonstructural protein 1):** Inhibits cellular mRNA translation and counteracts interferon-induced innate protections.
    *   **NS2 (Nonstructural protein 2):** Promotes export of the nucleocapsid from the nucleus to the cytoplasm.

#### Viral Replication Cycle
*   **Attachment, Host Receptors & Tropism:** The viral HA glycoprotein binds to specific sialic acid carbohydrates expressed on the surface of target cells. The virus exhibits strict tropism for the ciliated epithelium of the respiratory tract.
*   **Entry Mechanism:** Enters via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Upon acidification of the endosome (facilitated by the M2 ion channel in Influenza A), the HA protein undergoes a conformational change and acts as a fusion protein, fusing the viral envelope with the endosomal membrane to release the nucleocapsid into the cytoplasm.
*   **Uncoating & Intracellular Trafficking:** Following fusion, the released ribonucleoprotein complexes are transported along microtubules from the cytoplasm into the host cell nucleus.
*   **Transcription & Translation Strategy:** Uniquely for most RNA viruses, transcription and replication occur inside the host cell nucleus. The viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (PB1, PB2, PA) uses host cell mRNA as a primer for viral mRNA synthesis. The PB2 protein steals ("cap-snatches") the 5'-methylated cap region from cellular mRNAs to enable efficient binding to ribosomes. The positive-sense mRNA templates are then exported to the cytoplasm for translation by endoplasmic reticulum-associated ribosomes.
*   **Assembly, Maturation & Egress:** Early viral proteins (NP, PB1, PB2, PA) return to the nucleus for replication. Progeny nucleocapsid segments nest together, attach to recycling endosomes, and are transported to the cell surface where they associate with M1-lined cellular membranes containing HA and NA glycoproteins. The mature virion buds from the plasma membrane. Neuraminidase (NA) cleaves sialic acid residues on the cell surface to release the progeny virions and prevent them from clumping. The process generally kills the host cell.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Direct Cytopathic Effects:** Infection is highly cytolytic. It causes extensive destruction and necrosis of the ciliated epithelium in the respiratory tract.
*   **Latent Infection & Reactivation Triggers:** N/A (Causes acute infections, not latent).
*   **Oncogenesis Mechanisms:** N/A
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The virus evades immunity through constant genetic variation:
    *   **Antigenic Drift:** Minor antigenic changes in HA and NA caused by the accumulation of point mutations during replication. This allows the virus to evade preexisting immunity and causes annual epidemics.
    *   **Antigenic Shift:** Major antigenic changes in HA or NA caused by genetic reassortment (swapping of genome segments) when a cell is co-infected with two different viral strains (e.g., human and avian/swine strains). This creates a completely new virus subtype to which the population is immunologically naive, leading to worldwide pandemics.
    *   The NS1 protein actively inhibits cellular mRNA translation and blocks interferon-induced antiviral pathways.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Massive induction of interferons (IFN-alpha, IFN-beta) and proinflammatory cytokines by the innate immune system is responsible for the classic acute flulike symptoms. Subtype-specific neutralizing antibodies directed against HA and NA are protective. Cell-mediated immunity (CD8 [[T-Cell|T cells]]) is essential for viral clearance. Patients with compromised immune systems, the elderly, pregnant women, and those with underlying cardiac or respiratory problems are at highest risk for severe disease and death.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Acute influenza (classic "flu" syndrome), characterized by abrupt respiratory and systemic illness.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Can cause bronchiolitis, croup in children, and exacerbate asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).
*   **Incubation Period:** 1 to 2 days (can range 1-4 days).
*   **Classic Prodrome:** Abrupt onset of severe systemic symptoms including high fever, chills, severe malaise, severe headache, myalgia (body aches), sore throat, and a dry (nonproductive) cough.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** N/A
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Primary viral pneumonia or secondary bacterial pneumonia (most commonly caused by [[Staphylococcus aureus]], [[Streptococcus pneumoniae]], or [[Haemophilus influenzae]]). Can rapidly progress to severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), multi-organ system failure, and death. Rarely associated with post-infectious immune-mediated neurological diseases.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:**
    *   *Influenza A:* Aquatic birds are the natural reservoir, harboring all subtypes. It also infects domestic poultry, pigs (swine), horses, seals, and humans (zoonosis).
    *   *Influenza B and C:* Exclusively infect humans.
*   **Transmission Route:** Spread via the inhalation of small aerosol droplets expelled during talking, breathing, and coughing. Can also be transmitted via direct contact with respiratory secretions and contaminated fomites.
*   **Vectors:** None.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Worldwide occurrence with high morbidity and economic burden. Epidemics occur annually and are heavily driven by transmission among schoolchildren. Disease incidence strongly peaks during the winter (cool, less humid, home-heating season in temperate climates). Masking, social distancing, and hand hygiene are utilized for outbreak control. Periodic pandemics occur unpredictably when a novel Influenza A reassortant emerges.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Histopathology & Inclusion Bodies:** N/A
*   **Molecular Testing:** Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), multiplex RT-PCR, and rapid viral RNA detection assays from nasopharyngeal swabs, nasal washings, or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) are the "gold standard." These offer high speed, sensitivity, and specificity for detecting and typing/subtyping the virus.
*   **Serology & Seroconversion Windows:** Rapid antigen tests and Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assays (ELISA) are available but are less sensitive than PCR. Hemagglutination tests detect the virus in secretions, and hemagglutination inhibition tests use specific antibodies to identify the strain. Seroconversion (a rise in virus-specific antibodies) can be detected during convalescence.
*   **Cell Culture & Assays:** Can be cultured in primary monkey kidney or Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells. Viral presence can be detected via hemadsorption of red blood cells to the infected cell surface, indicating the expression of the HA protein.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Antiviral Targets & First-Line Agents:**
    *   *Neuraminidase inhibitors:* [[Oseltamivir]], [[zanamivir]], and [[peramivir]] block the NA protein, preventing viral release (effective against Influenza A and B).
    *   *Cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitor:* [[Baloxavir]] marboxil targets the PB2 polymerase component, preventing mRNA synthesis (effective against Influenza A and B).
    *   *M2 Ion Channel inhibitors:* [[Amantadine]] and [[rimantadine]] target the M2 protein to block uncoating (effective against Influenza A only, though clinical use is limited due to widespread resistance).
*   **Resistance Mechanisms & Mutations:** Resistance emerges frequently through point mutations, particularly against M2 ion channel inhibitors, rendering [[amantadine]] and [[rimantadine]] largely obsolete against current circulating strains.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:** Antiviral drugs must be administered early (within the first 48 to 72 hours of symptom onset) to be effective in shortening the duration of illness. Treatment after the virus has peaked and caused extensive tissue damage or ARDS is generally ineffective. Severe cases require intensive supportive care, mechanical ventilation, and antibacterial therapy for secondary infections.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** Vaccination is the primary mode of prevention. The vaccines are reformulated annually based on global surveillance to match the predicted circulating strains (typically two Influenza A subtypes and one or two Influenza B strains).
    *   *Inactivated or Recombinant Subunit Vaccines:* Contain HA and NA antigens. High-dose and adjuvanted versions are recommended for older adults (>65 years).
    *   *Live Attenuated Influenza Vaccine (LAIV):* Administered via nasal spray, approved for healthy individuals aged 2 to 49 years.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Antigenic shift (pandemics/reassortment), antigenic drift (epidemics/point mutations), segmented genome, hemagglutinin (HA), neuraminidase (NA), cap-snatching, nucleus replication, M2 ion channel, 1918 Spanish flu, H5N1 avian flu, H1N1 swine flu.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike the vast majority of RNA viruses that replicate exclusively in the host cell cytoplasm, influenza is a unique exception that must enter the host cell nucleus to transcribe and replicate its segmented RNA genome, largely to scavenge ("cap-snatch") cellular mRNA primers.
