### Corynebacterium diphtheriae

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:** Gram-positive, non-spore-forming cell envelope.
*   **Appendages:** N/A
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:** N/A
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:** Non-spore-forming.

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:** Aerobic. In vitro production of its primary exotoxin depends largely on the concentration of iron in the medium (optimal at 0.14 µg/mL and virtually suppressed at 0.5 µg/mL). Toxin yield is also influenced by osmotic pressure, amino acid concentration, pH, and suitable carbon and nitrogen sources.
*   **Primary Agar:** Enriched blood agar (nonselective).
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:** Cysteine-tellurite blood agar (CTBA), Tinsdale medium, and [[Polymyxin|colistin]]-nalidixic agar (CNA). Tellurite inhibits most upper respiratory tract bacteria and Gram-negative rods and is reduced by *C. diphtheriae*, producing a characteristic gray to black color on the agar. Tinsdale medium is considered the best for recovery; degradation of cysteine by *C. diphtheriae* cystinase activity produces a brown halo around the colonies.
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:** Catalase-positive. Acid is produced from glucose and maltose, but only very rarely from sucrose. Urea and [[Pyrazinamide]] are not hydrolyzed. Produces cystinase. The *gravis* biotype uniquely ferments starch.

#### Virulence Factors & Genetics
*   **Secretion Systems:** N/A
*   **Exotoxins:**
    *   **Toxin Name:** Diphtheria Toxin
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** A 58,300-Da classic A-B exotoxin. It requires two processing steps to become active: proteolytic cleavage of the leader sequence during secretion, and cleavage of the toxin into two polypeptides (A and B subunits) that remain attached by a disulfide bond. The B subunit contains a receptor-binding region and a translocation region. It binds to heparin-binding epidermal growth factor, which is highly present on the surface of heart and nerve cells. The translocation region inserts into the endosomal membrane, facilitating the movement of the A subunit (catalytic region) into the host cell cytosol. The A subunit terminates host cell protein synthesis by inactivating elongation factor-2 (EF-2), a factor required for the movement of nascent peptide chains on ribosomes. Toxin Fragment A inactivates EF-2 by catalyzing a reaction that yields free nicotinamide plus an inactive adenosine diphosphate-ribose-EF-2 complex (ADP-ribosylation). One exotoxin molecule is sufficient to inactivate the entire EF-2 content in a cell.
*   **Endotoxins & Superantigens:** N/A
*   **Enzymes:** Cystinase.
*   **Genetic Elements:** The *tox* gene that encodes the exotoxin is introduced into *C. diphtheriae* strains by a lysogenic bacteriophage (the α lysogenic β-phage). Toxin synthesis is regulated by a chromosomally encoded element called the diphtheria toxin repressor (DTxR). In the presence of high iron concentrations, DTxR binds to the toxin gene operator and prevents toxin production.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:** The bacilli grow on mucous membranes or in skin abrasions after being spread by droplets or contact.
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:** Extracellular.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The potent exotoxin halts host protein synthesis, causing extensive local cellular death and creating a thick necrotic pseudomembrane that shelters the replicating bacteria.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Susceptibility is strictly correlated with a lack of neutralizing antibodies against the diphtheria exotoxin (due to lack of vaccination or lapsed booster shots).

#### Clinical Syndromes
*  **Primary Diseases:**
	- **Faucial Diphtheria:** The most common presentation in endemic areas. It features a slow onset with moderate fever, malaise, sore throat (80%), nausea, vomiting, and difficulty/pain when swallowing. The pseudomembrane typically appears on the tonsils with a dull red inflammatory zone and can extend to the palate, pharynx, or larynx.
	- **Tracheolaryngeal Diphtheria:** Usually secondary to faucial diphtheria (85%). Presents with moderate fever, hoarseness, unproductive cough, and dyspnea. It is highly dangerous, leading to gradual respiratory obstruction over 24 hours with severe agitation, cyanosis, and accessory muscle use. Often fatal without a tracheostomy.
	- **Anterior Nasal Diphtheria:** More common in infancy. Characterized by a unilateral nasal discharge that progresses from thin to purulent and bloody, causing excoriations around the nostril and upper lip. Generally mild unless combined with other forms.
	- **Ocular Diphtheria:** Presents as membranous conjunctivitis with eyelid edema, discharge, and local lymphadenopathy. Removing the grey conjunctival membrane reveals a raw, bleeding surface. It can lead to corneal ulceration.
- **Other forms**
	- **Malignant Diphtheria:** The most severe form, carrying a mortality rate exceeding 50%. Characterized by an acute onset, high fever, rapid pulse, hypotension, cyanosis, and a rapidly spreading pseudomembrane. Patients exhibit a pronounced 'bull neck' and may bleed from the mouth, nose, and skin. Cardiac involvement (heart block) occurs within days.
	- **Cutaneous Diphtheria:** Often seen in unhygienic conditions. A simple papule or pustule progresses into a chronic, non-healing ulcer covered by a dirty grey membrane, typically on the toes or toe clefts. Systemic toxicity is rare, but when it occurs, it manifests as neuritis rather than myocarditis.
	- **Non-toxigenic Infections:** Strains lacking the _tox_ gene (or repressed by DTxR) do not produce the classic systemic toxemia but can cause localized or invasive infections, including pharyngitis, endocarditis, septicemia, septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, and abscesses.
- **Incubation Period:** 2 to 5 days.
- **Pathognomonic Signs:**
    - **The Pseudomembrane:** A thick, greyish-white, leathery, and firmly adherent membrane (a coagulum of leukocytes, bacteria, cellular debris, and fibrin) over the tonsils, pharynx, or other affected areas. Attempting to remove or swab it causes hemorrhage in the underlying tissues.
    - **Foetor:** A distinct sweet and sickly odor.
    - **'Bull Neck':** Massive cervical lymph node enlargement (adenitis) and tissue edema.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** The distal effects of the absorbed diphtheria toxin can cause severe, often fatal, systemic damage. Overall mortality is 5–10%, peaking in infancy, old age, and malignant cases.
	- **Airway Obstruction:** Membrane expansion or sudden detachment can cause fatal suffocation.
	- **Diphtheritic Myocarditis:** Occurs in ~10% of all cases (up to 66% of severe cases), typically in the second week.
	    - _Signs:_ Soft heart sounds, gallop rhythm, congestive heart failure, and dilated, poorly contracting ventricles. 
	    - _ECG Findings:_ Ectopics, tachyarrhythmias, broadened QRS, ST/T wave changes, and heart block.
	    - _Prognosis:_ Complete heart block carries a >80% mortality rate and may require a cardiac pacemaker. Overall myocarditis mortality is ~50%.
	- **Diphtheritic Neuropathy:** Polyneuritis occurs in 10–20% of cases due to segmental demyelination, typically developing late (3 to 8 weeks post-onset).
	    - _Progression:_ Early signs include soft palate paralysis (nasal voice, nasal fluid regurgitation). This is followed by blurred vision (accommodation paralysis) and paralysis of the pharynx, larynx, and respiratory muscles.
	    - _Cranial Nerves:_ CN IX and X are most affected, followed by VII, III, IV, and VI.
	    - _Severe features:_ Quadriparesis and autonomic dysfunction (sudden hypotension).
	- **Systemic Organ Failure:** The toxin can also induce acute tubular necrosis (renal failure), disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), endocarditis, and secondary pneumonia.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Humans are the only known natural reservoir (asymptomatic carriage in the oropharynx or on the skin).
*   **Transmission Route:** Person-to-person spread via infectious respiratory droplets and close physical contact.
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:** Although rare in developed countries due to vaccination, it remains an important disease in tropical parts of the world. Major outbreaks occurred in the countries of the former Soviet Union (1990-1999) and more recently (2017-2019) in Yemen, Bangladesh, Venezuela, Myanmar, and Haiti associated with civil unrest, privation, forced migration, and disasters.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Widespread vaccination coverage in the population must be maintained above 95% to prevent outbreaks.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:** Smears stained with alkaline methylene blue reveal metachromatic granules within beaded rods in typical arrangements. Gram stains show club-shaped, pleomorphic Gram-positive rods. However, microscopy is not specific enough to confirm the diagnosis alone.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) directly on clinical specimens (membrane swabs or biopsy material) or clinical isolates to detect the *tox* gene. MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and species-specific gene sequencing are used for definitive species identification.
*   **Serology & Immunology:** The Elek test is used to demonstrate the production of the exotoxin immunologically.
*   **Diagnostic Nuances:** Physicians must notify the clinical laboratory before collecting samples so proper media can be prepared. Swabs must be collected from *beneath* any visible membrane and placed in a semisolid transport media (e.g., Amies). PCR detection of the *tox* gene does not guarantee clinical toxicity if the gene is repressed by DTxR.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:** Diphtheria antitoxin is administered immediately to neutralize the circulating exotoxin, combined with [[Penicillin G]] or [[Erythromycin]] to eliminate the organism and terminate further toxin production.
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:** [[Ceftriaxone]], [[Nafcillin]], and steroids have been utilized in intensive care settings for severe respiratory presentations alongside macrolides.
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Contraindications:** Specific treatment (antitoxin and antibiotics) must **never** be delayed while waiting for laboratory reports if the clinical picture strongly suggests diphtheria. The antitoxin cannot neutralize toxin that has already entered host cells.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** Vaccination with the diphtheria toxoid (inactivated toxin) is highly effective and must be administered to children as an initial course with regular boosters (Td) every 10 years for adults. Convalescing patients must also be immunized with the toxoid because natural infection does not reliably stimulate protective antibodies.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Pseudomembrane", "Club-shaped / Beaded rods", "Elek test", "Tinsdale medium brown halo", "Metachromatic granules", "EF-2 inactivation", "Black colonies on tellurite".
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** While *C. diphtheriae* is the primary pathogen associated with diphtheria, the disease and toxin can also be produced by some strains of *Corynebacterium ulcerans* (acquired from raw milk, farm animals, or pets) and, very rarely, *Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis*. Furthermore, harmless non-toxigenic strains can be converted into lethal, pathogenic toxigenic strains by infection with the lysogenic β-phage in vivo.
