### Bacillus anthracis

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:** Typical Gram-positive peptidoglycan cell wall structure.
*   **Appendages:** Nonmotile; lacks flagella.
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:** Possesses a prominent polypeptide capsule consisting exclusively of poly-γ-d-glutamic acid, which makes it antigenically uniform (only one serotype). The capsule is encoded by three genes (capA, capB, and capC) located on the pXO2 plasmid. It is produced in vivo but is not typically observed in routine in vitro cultures unless specialized conditions are met (bicarbonate-containing medium with 5-7% CO2).
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:** Forms hardy endospores that are located centrally within the cell and appear as clear or unstained ovoid shapes on Gram stain. Spores consist of a core containing a complete chromosome, essential proteins, and an energy-generating system. The core contains a high concentration of calcium bound to dipicolinic acid, which stabilizes the spore contents and contributes to intense resistance to heat, desiccation, ultraviolet light, and chemical disinfectants. The spore is enclosed by an inner membrane, two peptidoglycan layers (the cortex), an outer keratin-like protein coat, and an exosporium composed of proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates. Spores can survive for decades in the soil and germinate into metabolically active vegetative bacilli upon encountering favorable nutrient conditions or upon ingestion/inhalation by a host (e.g., inside [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]]).

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:** Aerobe and facultative anaerobe. Spores can germinate in soil at a pH of 6.5 and at proper temperatures. Readily cultured on most standard bacteriologic media at 35-37°C.
*   **Primary Agar:** 5% sheep blood agar. Forms large (1-2 mm or more), grey-to-white, non-pigmented, non-hemolytic colonies. Colonies have a dry, rough, "ground-glass" or "cut-glass" appearance with irregular edges. Distinctive comma-shaped outgrowths called "Medusa head" or "curled hair" projections may extend from the colony. The colonies are tenacious, highly sticky, and adherent to the agar; if lifted with a loop, they stand up like "beaten egg whites."
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:** Bicarbonate-containing agar incubated in 5% to 10% CO2 is used specifically to induce and demonstrate capsule formation.
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:** Tyrosine deaminase negative. Carbohydrate fermentation tests are typically not useful for this pathogen.

#### Virulence Factors & Genetics
*   **Secretion Systems:** N/A
*   **Exotoxins:**
    *   **Toxin Name:** Edema Toxin (ET)
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** A binary A-B toxin formed when Protective Antigen (PA, an 83-kDa B subunit) binds to host cell receptors (TEM-8 or CMG2). PA is cleaved by host proteases to a 63-kDa fragment (PA63), which self-associates into a heptameric ring (prepore). The A subunit, Edema Factor (EF), competitively binds to the PA63 heptamer. The complex is endocytosed, and the acidic environment of the endosome triggers a conformational change that translocates EF into the cytosol. EF is a calmodulin-dependent and calcium-dependent adenylate cyclase that profoundly increases intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels, causing severe altered water homeostasis (tissue edema) and the impairment of neutrophil function.
    *   **Toxin Name:** Lethal Toxin (LT)
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** Formed when Protective Antigen (PA) binds with Lethal Factor (LF). After PA-mediated endosomal translocation into the cytosol, LF acts as a zinc-dependent metalloprotease that cleaves mitogen-activated protein (MAP) kinases. This disrupts signal transduction pathways, impairs innate and adaptive immunity, stimulates [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]] to release massive amounts of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-1beta), and causes rapid cytolysis and cell death. LT also causes direct deleterious effects on myocardial and peripheral vascular function.
*   **Endotoxins & Superantigens:** N/A
*   **Enzymes:** N/A
*   **Genetic Elements:** Virulence is strictly dependent on the acquisition of two large plasmids: pXO1 (carrying the genes for PA, LF, and EF) and pXO2 (carrying the genes for the poly-d-glutamic acid capsule). Strains lacking either plasmid (or lacking the capsule) are highly attenuated or avirulent.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:** Infection begins with the introduction of spores into the host via skin abrasions (cutaneous), ingestion of contaminated meat/spores (gastrointestinal), inhalation of 1-5 µm spores into the deep pulmonary alveoli, or direct injection (e.g., contaminated heroin). Alveolar or tissue [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]] phagocytose the spores and carry them via lymphatics to regional [[Lymph Nodes]] (e.g., perihilar and mediastinal [[Lymph Nodes]] in inhalational anthrax).
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:** Survives and germinates intracellularly within [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]] during transit to [[Lymph Nodes]]. After germination, the vegetative bacilli lyse the [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]], escape, and multiply freely in the extracellular environment (bloodstream and tissues).
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The poly-d-glutamic acid capsule is highly antiphagocytic and protects replicating vegetative cells from immune clearance. The secreted EF impairs neutrophil function, while LF directly destroys [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]] and impairs broader innate and adaptive immune responses.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Full expression of disease depends heavily on the action of PA, LF, and EF. There is documented human genetic variation in cellular sensitivity to anthrax toxins due to differences in the genetic expression of host cell surface proteins that function as PA receptors (TEM-8 and CMG2).

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:**
    *   **Cutaneous Anthrax:** Accounts for 95% of natural cases. Characterized by a painless papule that progresses to a serosanguineous fluid-filled vesicle, eventually forming a central black necrotic eschar. Extensive surrounding non-pitting edema and painful lymphadenopathy frequently occur. Sepsis can result in ~20% of untreated cases.
    *   **Inhalational Anthrax (Woolsorters' Disease):** Initial non-specific viral-like prodrome (fever, myalgias, cough, fatigue) lasting a few days, abruptly followed by sudden and severe respiratory distress, stridor, cyanosis, diaphoresis, and shock. Pathogenesis involves hemorrhagic mediastinitis rather than true bronchopneumonia. Meningitis occurs in ~50% of cases. Very high mortality rate (80-90% if untreated).
    *   **Gastrointestinal Anthrax:** Rare; follows ingestion of contaminated meat. Causes hemorrhagic gastroenteritis, severe abdominal pain, hematemesis, bloody diarrhea, hypovolemia, intestinal perforation, and sepsis. Mortality is 25-75%.
    *   **Oropharyngeal Anthrax:** A variant of ingestion exposure causing severe sore throat, neck swelling, cervical adenopathy, dysphagia, and pharyngeal/tonsillar pseudomembrane formation.
    *   **Injection Anthrax:** Associated with contaminated heroin use. Presents with extensive, painless subcutaneous edema (often lacking the classic black eschar) and rapid progression to hemodynamic instability and septicemia.
*   **Incubation Period:** 1 to 6 days typically, though inhalational anthrax can have a prolonged latent period (up to 2 months or more) where spores remain dormant in nasal passages before reaching the lower airways.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** A painless necrotic ulcer with a central black eschar and striking, disproportionate surrounding edema (cutaneous). Markedly widened mediastinum on chest radiograph without typical lobar pneumonia infiltrates (inhalational).
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Hemorrhagic meningitis, refractory hypotension, massive pleural effusions, multiorgan failure, and rapid death. Survivors of inhalational anthrax may suffer severe sequelae for months or years.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Soil is the primary environmental reservoir, containing spores that persist for decades. Herbivores (cattle, sheep, goats, horses, deer, antelope, bison) are the primary animal reservoirs and natural amplification hosts.
*   **Transmission Route:** Human exposure is zoonotic, occupational, or via bioterrorism. Acquired through direct contact with contaminated animal products (wool, hides, goat hair), ingestion of infected meat, inoculation via trauma/injection, or aerosolized inhalation. Person-to-person spread does not occur in inhalational anthrax because replication happens in mediastinal [[Lymph Nodes]] rather than the bronchopulmonary tree.
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:** Worldwide distribution, prominently in Africa, the Middle East, Central America, Asia, and southeastern Australia. Endemic regions in the United States include the Midwest and Southwest (e.g., specific counties in southwest Texas). Notable outbreaks include the 1979 Sverdlovsk accidental aerosol release (Russia) and the 2001 US Postal Service bioterrorism attacks.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** B. anthracis is a major potential biological warfare/bioterrorism agent due to its high lethality, spore stability, and potential for aerosolization. Eradication is unlikely due to spore hardiness in soil. Public health control requires vaccination of animal herds in endemic areas and the destruction of animal carcasses via burning or deep burial in lime pits. Handled in high-level biocontainment facilities.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:** Peripheral blood Gram stains in severe systemic disease may reveal overwhelming numbers of large Gram-positive rods in long chains (unusual for most bacteremias). Spores are visualized using malachite green spore stain. The capsule can be visualized in clinical specimens using India ink, M'Fadyean methylene blue stain, or specific direct fluorescent antibody (DFA) testing.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) are used for definitive identification of toxin and capsule genes. Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) is also utilized.
*   **Serology & Immunology:** Definitive identification requires specific lysis of the bacteria by a gamma bacteriophage or a positive DFA test for specific B. anthracis cell wall polysaccharides. Rapid enzyme-linked immunoassays (ELISA) measuring total antibody to Protective Antigen (PA) are FDA-approved but may not be positive early in the disease course.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:** [[Ciprofloxacin]], [[Doxycycline]], [[Levofloxacin]], or [[Moxifloxacin]]. For severe systemic or inhalational anthrax, intravenous triple therapy is mandated: a [[Fluoroquinolone]] (e.g., [[Ciprofloxacin]]) plus a bactericidal beta-lactam (e.g., [[Meropenem]] or [[Penicillin]]) plus a protein synthesis inhibitor (e.g., [[Linezolid]], [[clindamycin]], or [[Rifampin]]) to halt toxin production.
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:** [[Erythromycin]] and [[vancomycin]]. Adjunctive monoclonal or polyclonal antibody therapies are FDA-approved to neutralize toxins by preventing PA binding: Raxibacumab (monoclonal human IgG1), Obiltoxaximab (Anthim), and Anthrax immune globulin intravenous (AIGIV / Anthrasil, a human polyclonal antiserum).
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:** Naturally occurring resistance to extended-spectrum cephalosporins and sulfonamides. While [[Penicillin]] was historically the drug of choice, penicillin-resistant strains occur naturally, and laboratory-engineered resistance (e.g., for biological warfare) is a critical concern necessitating initial multi-drug empirical regimens.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Contraindications:** Antibiotic therapy must be initiated immediately upon suspicion; if delayed until the onset of sudden respiratory deterioration in inhalational anthrax, the prognosis is universally poor despite sterilizing the blood, because pre-formed toxins continue to exert lethal effects. Cutaneous eschars proceed through their natural evolution and healing phases despite successful antibiotic eradication.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** Post-exposure prophylaxis for aerosolized exposure requires 60 days of oral antibiotics ([[Ciprofloxacin]] or [[Doxycycline]]) combined with three doses of the vaccine. The FDA-licensed human vaccine (AVA BioThrax) uses a cell-free culture supernatant containing Protective Antigen (PA) adsorbed to aluminum hydroxide. The current schedule requires a five-dose intramuscular series (at 0, 4 weeks, 6, 12, and 18 months) with annual boosters. Novel recombinant PA (rPA) vaccines are in development.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Woolsorters' disease", "Medusa head" or "curled hair" colonies, "beaten egg whites" consistency, "widened mediastinum" on CXR, "black eschar", "boxcar-shaped" rods, "PA, EF, LF", "poly-d-glutamic acid capsule".
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike the vast majority of bacterial capsules which are polysaccharide in nature, B. anthracis produces a polypeptide capsule made uniquely of poly-d-glutamic acid. Unlike other clinically relevant Bacillus species (like [[Bacillus cereus|B. cereus]]), B. anthracis is strictly non-motile and non-hemolytic on sheep blood agar.
