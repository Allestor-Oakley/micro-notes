### Streptococcus agalactiae

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:** Contains a peptidoglycan layer typical of Gram-positive bacteria. The cell wall contains the group-specific carbohydrate B antigen (Lancefield group B antigen), which is a dimer of N-acetylglucosamine and rhamnose (rhamnose-glucosamine polysaccharide). Surface proteins are also present, the most common being the c antigen.
*   **Appendages:** N/A
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:** Covered by an outer polysaccharide capsule consisting of 10 type-specific capsular polysaccharides (Ia, Ib, and II to IX). The type-specific capsular polysaccharides of types Ia, Ib, and II have a terminal residue of sialic acid.
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:** Non-spore-forming.

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:** Facultative anaerobe. It grows rapidly and well on nutritionally enriched media.
*   **Primary Agar:** Enriched sheep blood agar. After 24 hours of incubation, it typically produces large colonies with a narrow zone of β-hemolysis (1 to 2 mm in diameter, only slightly larger than the colonies).
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:** A selective enrichment broth medium (e.g., LIM broth containing colistin and nalidixic acid) is recommended to suppress contaminating organisms (such as in vaginal cultures), followed by subculture to nonselective media like a blood agar plate.
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:** Catalase-negative. Hydrolyzes sodium hippurate. Yields a positive response in the CAMP (Christie, Atkins, Munch-Peterson) test.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:** Asymptomatically colonizes the lower gastrointestinal tract and genitourinary tract. Infants become colonized when they pass through the vaginal canal and are exposed to the mother's organisms, especially when the mother is colonized with large numbers of bacteria.
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:** Extracellular.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The major virulence factor is the polysaccharide capsule, which heavily interferes with phagocytosis until the patient develops type-specific antibodies. Furthermore, the terminal sialic acid residues present on the capsular polysaccharides of types Ia, Ib, and II inhibit the activation of the alternative complement pathway, thereby interfering with the phagocytosis of these specific strains.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Functional classical and alternative complement pathways are required to kill group B streptococci (particularly types Ia, III, and V). Premature infants are highly susceptible due to physiologically low complement levels and because receptors for complement or the Fc fragment of IgG are not exposed on their neutrophils. Neonates lack protection if maternal type-specific antibodies are absent. Adults at increased risk include pregnant females and individuals with diabetes mellitus, cancer, advanced age, alcoholism, liver cirrhosis, chronic renal disease, corticosteroid therapy, or [[HIV]] infection.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:**
    *   **Early-onset neonatal disease:** Occurs within 7 days of birth. Presents as fulminant septicemia, pneumonia, meningitis, and respiratory distress syndrome. Most commonly associated with serotypes Ia, III, and V.
    *   **Late-onset neonatal disease:** Occurs between 1 week and 3 months of life. Neonates develop signs and symptoms of bacteremia with meningitis. Most commonly associated with serotype III.
    *   **Infections in pregnant females:** Most commonly presents as postpartum endometritis, wound infections, amnionitis, and urinary tract infections. Bacteremia and disseminated complications may also occur.
    *   **Infections in other adults:** Primarily affects the elderly or immunocompromised. Causes bacteremia, pneumonia, bone and joint infections, skin and soft tissue infections, osteomyelitis, endocarditis, and urosepsis. Most commonly associated with serotypes Ia and V.
*   **Incubation Period:** N/A (Early-onset disease occurs <7 days of birth; late-onset occurs between 1 week and 3 months of life).
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** In neonates with late-onset disease, clinical presentation can include generalized seizures, irregular feeding, and cloudy cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Neonatal meningitis can lead to hydrocephalus (necessitating an atrioventricular shunt), severe neurologic deficits, retardation of psychomotor development, and death. Genital colonization with group B streptococci in pregnant women is associated with an increased risk of premature delivery.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Normal microbiota of the lower gastrointestinal tract and the genitourinary tract (vaginal flora in 5-30% of healthy women).
*   **Transmission Route:** Vertical transmission to the neonate from the mother (in utero, at birth during passage through the colonized vaginal canal, or during the first few months of life).
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:** Worldwide distribution.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Universal screening of pregnant women at 35-37 (or 36-38) weeks of gestation using vaginal and rectal swabs is recommended to identify carriers and guide the use of prophylactic antibiotics, which has dramatically reduced the incidence of early-onset neonatal disease (from 1.7 infections per 1000 live births in 1993 to 0.2 in 2020).

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:** Gram stains of cerebrospinal fluid (for meningitis), lower respiratory secretions (for pneumonia), and wound exudates reveal Gram-positive cocci in short chains, which are morphologically indistinguishable from [[Streptococcus pyogenes]].
*   **Molecular Testing:** Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based assays are FDA-approved and considered the test of choice for detecting rectal/vaginal carriage in pregnant females or diagnosing meningitis (especially in antibiotic-treated patients). For adequate sensitivity in vaginal/rectal screening, swabs must be preincubated in an enrichment broth before the NAAT is performed.
*   **Serology & Immunology:** Isolates can be definitively identified by the demonstration of the group-specific cell wall B carbohydrate. Direct antigen detection tests on urogenital specimens are considered too insensitive to reliably screen mothers and should not be used.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:** [[Penicillin]] G is the drug of choice for susceptible strains. For serious infections or empirical therapy in neonates (until the specific pathogen is identified), broad-spectrum antibiotics (such as a broad-spectrum [[cephalosporin]] combined with an aminoglycoside like [[gentamicin]]) are used.
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:** Cephalosporins or [[vancomycin]] are used for patients who are allergic to [[penicillin]].
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:** Resistance to [[penicillin]] is increasingly common, though specific resistance genes are not detailed.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Contraindications:** Empirical therapy with broad-spectrum antibiotics is critical until the pathogen is identified.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** Intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (intravenous [[ampicillin]] or [[penicillin]]) given to colonized mothers in labor at least 4 hours before delivery effectively prevents colonization of infants and subsequent early-onset disease. A 6-valent conjugate vaccine (GBS6) is currently provisionally approved for pregnant females to stimulate protective antibodies.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Group B Strep/GBS", "early-onset and late-onset neonatal meningitis/sepsis", "CAMP test positive", "hippurate hydrolysis positive", "screening pregnant females at 35-37 weeks".
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Although typically β-hemolytic, approximately 1% to 2% of S. agalactiae strains are nonhemolytic. Their prevalence may be underestimated because nonhemolytic strains are frequently overlooked and not commonly screened for the group B antigen.
