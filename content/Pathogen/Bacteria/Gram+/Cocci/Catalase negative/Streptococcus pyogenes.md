### Streptococcus pyogenes

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:** The cell wall possesses a typical Gram-positive thick peptidoglycan layer. The fundamental serologic marker is the Lancefield Group A-specific carbohydrate (a dimer of rhamnose-N-acetylglucosamine) which constitutes approximately 10% of the dry weight of the cell. The cell wall also contains M proteins (filamentous structures complexed in an alpha helix that are anchored in the cytoplasmic membrane, extend through the cell wall, and protrude above the cell surface), M-like surface proteins, lipoteichoic acid, and F protein.
*   **Appendages:** Hairlike pili project through the capsule. They consist partly of M protein and are covered with lipoteichoic acid.
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:** Strains can produce a prominent outer capsule composed of mucopolysaccharide hyaluronic acid. It is antigenically indistinguishable from hyaluronic acid in mammalian connective tissues, rendering it poorly immunogenic and highly antiphagocytic. The capsule is most noticeable in very young cultures and can impart a mucoid appearance to colonies. Strains lacking a capsule are less virulent, while heavily encapsulated strains are more likely to cause severe systemic infections.
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:** Non-spore-forming.

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:** Facultative anaerobe. Grows best at 37 degrees Celsius. Growth and hemolysis are aided by an incubation atmosphere supplemented with 5-10% CO2 (capnophilic growth). It grows poorly in media with high glucose concentrations due to the rapid production of toxic lactic acid.
*   **Primary Agar:** Requires complex, enriched media supplemented with blood or tissue fluids, specifically 5% sheep blood agar.
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:** Media selective for streptococci (containing antibiotics) can be used to isolate the organism from throat swabs to prevent overgrowth by normal oral flora.
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:**
    *   Catalase-negative.
    *   Produces large (1 cm in diameter) zones of beta-hemolysis around colonies greater than 0.5 mm in diameter.
    *   PYR test positive (hydrolyzes l-pyrrolidonyl-beta-naphthylamide), distinguishing it from most other streptococci.
    *   Susceptible to bacitracin (used for presumptive identification).
    *   Energy is principally obtained from glucose fermentation, with lactic acid as the end product.

#### Virulence Factors & Genetics
*   **Secretion Systems:** N/A
*   **Exotoxins:**
    *   **Toxin Name:** Streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins (SpeA, SpeB, SpeC, SpeF) / Erythrogenic toxins.
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** Heat-labile exotoxins that act as superantigens, interacting with [[Macrophage|macrophages]] and helper [[T-Cell|T cells]] to heavily enhance the release of proinflammatory cytokines. They are responsible for the severe clinical manifestations of necrotizing fasciitis, streptococcal toxic shock syndrome, and the diffuse rash of scarlet fever. SpeB specifically functions as a potent protease that interferes with phagocytosis.
    *   **Toxin Name:** Streptolysin O.
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** A highly immunogenic, oxygen-labile cytolytic exotoxin that degrades host cells.
    *   **Toxin Name:** Streptolysin S.
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** A cytolytic toxin responsible for beta-hemolysis on blood agar.
*   **Endotoxins & Superantigens:** Lacks Gram-negative endotoxin (LPS). However, the streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins (Spe) function as potent superantigens. Peptidoglycan fragments also stimulate the production of endogenous pyrogens and local inflammatory responses.
*   **Enzymes:**
    *   Streptokinase (A and B): Cleaves host plasminogen to form the active protease plasmin, which digests fibrin and allows the bacteria to escape from blood clots and rapidly spread through tissues.
    *   Deoxyribonucleases (DNases A to D): Depolymerize free DNA present in pus, reducing the viscosity of abscess material to facilitate bacterial spread.
    *   C5a peptidase: A surface serine protease that inactivates complement component C5a, preventing the recruitment of neutrophils and mononuclear phagocytes.
    *   Hyaluronidase: Facilitates spread through connective tissues.
*   **Genetic Elements:** Transmissible plasmids can carry genes for [[Macrolide]] resistance. Lysogenic bacteriophages encode the pyrogenic exotoxins (SpeA and SpeC). A complex of more than 20 genes (the emm gene superfamily) encodes the M proteins, M-like proteins, and immunoglobulin-binding proteins.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:** Initial colonization is mediated by a weak interaction between lipoteichoic acid and fatty acid binding sites on fibronectin and epithelial cells. Subsequent firm adherence utilizes M protein, F protein, and other adhesins to interact with specific host cell receptors. The hyaluronic acid capsule binds to the [[CD44]] receptor on human epithelial cells, inducing the disruption of intercellular junctions to allow the bacteria to penetrate the epithelium.
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:** Chiefly an extracellular pathogen, but M protein, F protein, and other antigens mediate the active invasion and internalization into host epithelial cells. This intracellular persistence is believed to be important for maintaining carriage, causing recurrent pharyngitis, and invading deep soft tissues.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The hyaluronic acid capsule masks the bacteria and prevents phagocytosis. M proteins and M-like proteins block the alternative complement pathway by preventing complement C3b binding and by binding factor H. M-like proteins also bind the Fc fragment of antibodies and fibronectin. C5a peptidase degrades C5a to prevent neutrophil chemotaxis. SpeB protease interferes with phagocytosis.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Clearance requires the production of type-specific opsonic antibodies against the bacterial M protein. Because there are over 150 M protein serotypes, individuals can suffer repeated infections with different strains. Prior viral respiratory infections (like [[Influenza]] or [[Measles virus]]) damage the respiratory epithelium and strongly predispose hosts to bacterial superinfection. Immunocompromised patients, diabetics, alcohol abusers, and IV drug users are at a substantially increased risk for severe streptococcal toxic shock syndrome.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:**
    *   Suppurative: Pharyngitis (strep throat), Scarlet fever, Pyoderma (impetigo), Erysipelas, Cellulitis, Necrotizing fasciitis, Streptococcal toxic shock syndrome, Puerperal sepsis, Lymphangitis, Pneumonia, Bacteremia.
    *   Nonsuppurative: Rheumatic fever, Acute glomerulonephritis.
*   **Incubation Period:** Pharyngitis generally develops 2 to 4 days after exposure. Post-streptococcal nonsuppurative diseases have a latent period of 1 to 4 weeks (mean 7 days) following the initial infection.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:**
    *   *Pharyngitis:* Abrupt onset of sore throat, intense redness and edema of mucous membranes, purulent exudate, and enlarged/tender cervical [[Lymph Nodes]].
    *   *Scarlet Fever:* Circumoral pallor, "strawberry tongue" (yellowish-white coating sheds to reveal a raw red surface beneath), "Pastia lines" (rash pronounced in skinfolds), and a diffuse macular blanching erythematous rash on the chest/extremities that spares the palms and soles, followed by desquamation.
    *   *Erysipelas:* Massive brawny edema, rapidly advancing and sharply demarcated margins of erythema, and bullae formation.
    *   *Necrotizing Fasciitis / Toxic Shock:* Extreme, excruciating localized pain out of proportion to initial superficial findings, rapidly progressing to multiorgan failure and shock.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Acute glomerulonephritis (edema, hypertension, hematuria, proteinuria). Rheumatic fever (pancarditis, migratory polyarthritis, subcutaneous nodules, Sydenham's chorea). Severe systemic infections can lead to shock, multiorgan failure, limb loss, and death (bacteremia with toxic shock/necrotizing fasciitis has a mortality rate approaching 40%).

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Humans. Asymptomatic carriage occurs in the oropharynx of healthy children and adults, as well as on the skin.
*   **Transmission Route:** Pharyngitis is spread person-to-person via airborne respiratory droplets. Pyoderma and cutaneous infections spread through direct contact with an infected person, fomites (contaminated clothing/linens), or an arthropod vector, entering via a superficial break in the skin.
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:** Ubiquitous worldwide. Pharyngitis incidence peaks in the winter and cooler months. Pyoderma is highly communicable and most common in warm, moist months or hot, humid climates. Rheumatic fever has declined in the US but remains highly endemic in developing countries (estimated 100 cases per 100,000 children annually).
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Crowded settings (classrooms, day-cares) facilitate rapid respiratory spread. Nasal discharges from colonized individuals are the most dangerous source of transmission. Permanent carriers in sensitive hospital settings (nurseries, operating rooms) may require temporary reassignment if eradication fails.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:** Gram stain of pus, tissue, or sterile fluids reveals Gram-positive cocci in pairs and chains alongside leukocytes. (Note: Gram stains of throat swabs are not useful because identical-appearing [[viridans streptococci]] are normal oral flora).
*   **Molecular Testing:** Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) and nucleic acid probe assays directly from pharyngeal swabs are highly sensitive and are the test of choice for diagnosing pharyngitis where available.
*   **Serology & Immunology:**
    *   *Rapid Antigen Tests:* Enzyme immunoassays (EIA) or agglutination kits detect the Group A-specific carbohydrate directly from throat swabs (60-90% sensitive, 98-99% specific).
    *   *Antibody Titers:* Anti-streptolysin O (ASO) titers are widely used to retrospectively confirm respiratory infections leading to rheumatic fever or glomerulonephritis. Anti-DNase B and antihyaluronidase titers are essential for diagnosing post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis following skin infections (as ASO titers may not rise reliably in pyoderma).

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:** [[Penicillin G]] (intravenous) for severe systemic disease. Oral [[Penicillin V]] or [[Amoxicillin]] for uncomplicated pharyngitis.
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:** Oral cephalosporins or Macrolides ([[Erythromycin]], [[Azithromycin]], [[Clarithromycin]]) for [[Penicillin]]-allergic patients.
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:** The organism remains uniformly susceptible to [[Penicillin]]. Resistance to macrolides is increasing and is mediated by a transmissible plasmid causing alteration (methylation) of the rRNA receptor, or by active drug efflux encoded by the mef and msr genes. Resistance/poor clinical response limits the use of tetracyclines and sulfonamides.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Contraindications:** Severe systemic infections (necrotizing fasciitis, toxic shock) mandate the combined use of intravenous [[Penicillin]] with a protein synthesis-inhibiting antibiotic (e.g., [[clindamycin]]) to shut down exotoxin production, alongside prompt and aggressive surgical debridement of necrotic tissue. Initiating [[Penicillin]] therapy within 10 days of pharyngitis onset speeds symptom relief and effectively prevents rheumatic fever. However, antibiotic therapy does not prevent the progression of acute glomerulonephritis. Asymptomatic prolonged carriage does not require treatment because antibiotics disrupt normal protective flora.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** No vaccine is currently available. Patients with a history of rheumatic fever require long-term continuous antibiotic prophylaxis to prevent recurrent disease from subsequent infections, particularly prior to procedures (e.g., dental work) that can induce bacteremia.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Flesh-eating bacteria", "Strawberry tongue", "Pastia lines", "Circumoral pallor", "Lancefield Group A", "M protein", "PYR positive", "Bacitracin susceptible", "Hyaluronic acid capsule", "Quellung reaction negative" (differentiates from [[Streptococcus pneumoniae|S. pneumoniae]]), "ASO titer", "Anti-DNase B test".
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** While pyoderma is most commonly caused by [[Staphylococcus aureus|S. aureus]], 20% of cases are caused strictly by S. pyogenes. While S. pyogenes pharyngitis can cause both rheumatic fever and acute glomerulonephritis, S. pyogenes skin infections only lead to acute glomerulonephritis, never rheumatic fever. Erysipelas historically manifested on the face, but is now more commonly found on the legs.
