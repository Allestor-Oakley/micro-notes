### Staphylococcus aureus

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:** Thick, rigid peptidoglycan layer (150 to 500 Å) consisting of multiple cross-linked layers of alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid subunits. The peptide cross-links contain a pentaglycine bridge and L-lysine. The cell wall contains teichoic acids (species-specific, phosphate-containing polymers of polyribitol phosphate covalently bound to peptidoglycan) and lipoteichoic acids (anchored in the cytoplasmic membrane). The peptidoglycan provides osmotic stability and has endotoxin-like activity (stimulates endogenous pyrogens, activates complement, produces IL-1 from [[Monocyte|monocytes]], and acts as a leukocyte chemoattractant).
*   **Appendages:** N/A (Nonmotile, lacks flagella).
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:** Produces an outer polysaccharide capsule consisting of 11 identified serotypes. Serotypes 1 and 2 produce very thick capsules and mucoid colonies but rarely cause human disease. Serotypes 5 and 8 are responsible for approximately 75% of human infections. Most strains also produce a loose-bound, water-soluble extracellular film (slime layer or biofilm) consisting of monosaccharides, proteins, and small peptides.
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:** Non-spore-forming.

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:** Facultative anaerobe capable of growing in aerobic and anaerobic atmospheres. Capable of growing at broad temperatures (18°C to 40°C, with most rapid growth at 37°C) and highly resistant to drying, heat (withstands 50°C for 30 minutes), and high salt concentrations (e.g., 10% NaCl). Pigment forms best at room temperature (20–25°C).
*   **Primary Agar:** Enriched sheep blood agar. Colonies are large (3-4 mm), smooth, round, raised, opaque, and glistening after 24 hours of incubation. They typically produce a gray to deep golden yellow pigment (due to carotenoids) and are usually surrounded by a clear zone of β-hemolysis (caused primarily by alpha toxin).
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:** Mannitol salt agar (supplemented with mannitol and 7.5% sodium chloride, allowing isolation from mixed flora because S. aureus ferments mannitol and tolerates high salt); Chromogenic agars (produce characteristic colony colors for rapid identification).
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:** Catalase-positive (differentiates from streptococci). Coagulase-positive (produces both bound and free coagulase, differentiating it from coagulase-negative staphylococci like [[Staphylococcus epidermidis|S. epidermidis]]). Ferments carbohydrates to produce lactic acid but not gas. L-pyrrolidonyl arylamidase (PYR) test negative. Inhibited by certain chemicals like 3% hexachlorophene.

#### Virulence Factors & Genetics
*   **Secretion Systems:** N/A
*   **Exotoxins:**
    *   **Toxin Name:** Alpha toxin
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** 33,000-Da polypeptide that disrupts smooth muscle in blood vessels and is toxic to erythrocytes, leukocytes, hepatocytes, and platelets. Binds to the cell surface, aggregates into a heptamer, forms a 1- to 2-nm pore, and allows rapid efflux of K+ and influx of Na+ and Ca2+, leading to osmotic swelling, cell lysis, and extensive tissue damage.
    *   **Toxin Name:** Beta toxin (sphingomyelinase C)
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** 35,000-Da heat-labile protein that hydrolyzes membrane phospholipids (sphingomyelin and lysophosphatidylcholine) with toxicity proportional to sphingomyelin concentration on the cell surface. Toxic to erythrocytes (mostly at low temperatures), fibroblasts, leukocytes, and [[Macrophage|macrophages]].
    *   **Toxin Name:** Delta toxin
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** 3,000-Da polypeptide with wide-spectrum cytolytic activity affecting erythrocytes, mammalian cells, and intracellular membrane structures. Acts as a surfactant, disrupting cellular membranes via detergent-like action.
    *   **Toxin Name:** Gamma toxin
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** Bicomponent toxin consisting of S (slow-eluting) and F (fast-eluting) chains (e.g., HlgA/HlgB, HlgC/HlgB). Lyses neutrophils and [[Macrophage|macrophages]] by pore formation, increasing permeability to cations and causing osmotic instability.
    *   **Toxin Name:** Panton-Valentine (P-V) leukocidin
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** Phage-encoded bicomponent toxin (LukS-PV/LukF-PV). Lacks hemolytic activity but is highly leukotoxic. Causes pore formation in white blood cell membranes, increasing cation permeability and triggering massive release of inflammatory mediators (IL-8, leukotriene, histamine), leading to severe necrosis and inflammation (heavily associated with CA-MRSA pulmonary and cutaneous infections).
    *   **Toxin Name:** Exfoliative toxins (ETA and ETB)
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** Serine proteases that split desmoglein-1 (a cell adhesion structure/desmosome responsible for forming intercellular bridges in the stratum granulosum epidermis). Causes generalized desquamation (Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome/SSSS) without cytolysis or massive inflammation. ETA is heat stable (phage-encoded), ETB is heat labile (plasmid-encoded). Both are superantigens.
    *   **Toxin Name:** Enterotoxins (A–E, G–X)
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** Superantigens (stimulate proliferation of [[T Cell|T cells]] and massive cytokine release). Heat stable (resist boiling for 30 minutes) and highly resistant to gastric and jejunal enzymes. Stimulate release of inflammatory mediators from mast cells and act on neural receptors in the gut to transmit stimuli to the CNS vomiting center, causing emesis and diarrhea. Enterotoxin A and LukE/LukD leukotoxin are heavily associated with enterocolitis; Enterotoxin B is associated with pseudomembranous enterocolitis and non-menstrual TSS.
    *   **Toxin Name:** Toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 (TSST-1 / Enterotoxin F)
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** 22,000-Da heat- and proteolysis-resistant chromosomal superantigen. Binds directly to MHC Class II molecules and [[T Cell|T-cell]] receptors (V-beta region), stimulating massive [[T Cell|T-cell]] proliferation and release of cytokines (IL-2, interferon-gamma, TNF), resulting in fever, hypotension, diffuse rash, endothelial leakage, and multisystem organ failure.
*   **Endotoxins & Superantigens:** Peptidoglycan acts as an endotoxin-like component. Exfoliative toxins, enterotoxins, and TSST-1 act as potent superantigens.
*   **Enzymes:**
    *   **Coagulase:** Bound coagulase (clumping factor) directly converts fibrinogen to insoluble fibrin; Free coagulase reacts with coagulase-reacting factor to form staphylothrombin, which catalyzes fibrinogen to fibrin, creating a protective wall around abscesses.
    *   **Hyaluronidase:** Hydrolyzes hyaluronic acids in the acellular matrix of connective tissue, promoting the spreading of bacteria.
    *   **Fibrinolysin (staphylokinase):** Dissolves fibrin clots.
    *   **Lipases:** Hydrolyze lipids, enabling survival in the sebaceous areas of the body.
    *   **Thermostable Nuclease:** Hydrolyzes viscous DNA in pus to reduce viscosity and facilitate spread.
    *   **Penicillinase (Beta-lactamase):** Hydrolyzes the beta-lactam ring of [[Penicillin]] and similar antibiotics.
*   **Genetic Elements:** Employs a quorum-sensing system via the accessory gene regulator (agr) operon (AgrA, AgrB, AgrC, AgrD; autoinducing peptide/AIP; RNA II, RNA III), coordinating expression of adhesins at low density and toxins/enzymes at high density. At least 10 two-component systems exist (e.g., sae RS, srrAB, arlSR, lytRS) that respond to environmental cues like oxygen or modulate autolysis. Toxin genes (enterotoxins, TSST-1, exfoliative toxins) are often located on pathogenicity islands (e.g., SaPI1), mobile phages, or plasmids. Staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec, types I-XII) harbors mecA/mecC genes.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:** Infection is initiated by introducing bacteria through breaks in the skin or mucosal membranes (often facilitated by a foreign body like a suture, splinter, or catheter). Bacteria adhere via MSCRAMMs (Microbial Surface Components Recognizing Adhesive Matrix Molecules) covalently bound to the peptidoglycan. Key adhesins include Protein A (binds Fc of IgG), fibronectin-binding proteins A and B, and clumping factor A and B (binds fibrinogen/fibrin).
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:** Extracellular pathogen, but capable of forming intracellular Small Colony Variants (SCVs) that allow persistence, evasion of immune clearance, and chronic infection.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:**
    *   Capsule and slime layer/biofilm inhibit chemotaxis and phagocytosis.
    *   Protein A binds the Fc portion of IgG (IgG1, IgG2, IgG4), preventing antibody-mediated clearance, opsonization, and complement activation, and acts as a leukocyte chemoattractant.
    *   Coagulase produces a fibrin clot around the bacteria/abscess, protecting them from phagocytes.
    *   Cytolytic toxins (gamma toxin, P-V leukocidin) directly lyse neutrophils and [[Macrophage|macrophages]].
    *   Produces catalase to neutralize reactive oxygen species (hydrogen peroxide) generated by phagocytes.
    *   Pyogenic response limits antibody and antibiotic access.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Individuals with congenital diseases associated with impaired chemotactic or phagocytic responses (e.g., Job syndrome, Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome, chronic granulomatous disease) are highly susceptible. Other high-risk groups include patients with intravascular catheters/shunts, neutropenia, diabetes mellitus, severe eczema, antecedent viral respiratory infections (e.g., [[Influenza]]), cystic fibrosis, IV drug abusers, and those with poor personal hygiene.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:**
    *   **Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome (SSSS / Ritter disease):** Toxin-mediated (exfoliative toxins) disseminated desquamation of the epithelium in infants, marked by a positive Nikolsky sign and large clear blisters containing no organisms or leukocytes.
    *   **Bullous Impetigo:** Localized form of SSSS with intact blisters containing S. aureus; Nikolsky sign is negative.
    *   **Staphylococcal Food Poisoning:** Toxin-mediated gastroenteritis causing severe vomiting, diarrhea, and cramping without fever; self-limiting within 24–48 hours.
    *   **Toxic Shock Syndrome (TSS):** Toxin-mediated (TSST-1) multiorgan intoxication causing abrupt fever, hypotension, diffuse macular erythematous rash (with subsequent desquamation of palms/soles), and massive cytokine shock.
    *   **Enterocolitis:** Direct colon infection post-broad-spectrum antibiotic use causing watery diarrhea, abdominal cramps, and fever, with fecal leukocytes and ulcerated white plaques.
    *   **Impetigo:** Localized pyogenic skin infection with pus-filled vesicles on an erythematous base (often crusting).
    *   **Folliculitis, Furuncles (boils), Carbuncles:** Pyogenic hair follicle infections progressing to large, painful subcutaneous tissue extensions with systemic disease signs.
    *   **Surgical Wound Infections:** Erythema, edema, pain, and purulent accumulation at trauma/surgery sites.
    *   **Bacteremia and Endocarditis:** Spread from local foci to blood, causing severe acute endocarditis (high mortality) with rapid destruction of endothelial lining/valves, septic emboli, and right-sided tricuspid valve involvement in IV drug abusers.
    *   **Pneumonia and Empyema:** Aspiration or hematogenous consolidation/abscesses. Severe necrotizing pneumonia with massive hemoptysis and septic shock is associated with CA-MRSA (PVL-positive).
    *   **Osteomyelitis:** Destruction of bone, commonly in the highly vascularized metaphyseal area of long bones in children via hematogenous spread.
    *   **Septic Arthritis:** Purulent, painful, erythematous joints (often shoulder, knee, hip, elbow) in adults receiving intraarticular injections or young children.
*   **Incubation Period:** 1 to 8 hours (mean 4 hours, up to 18 hours) for staphylococcal food poisoning. Variable for cutaneous and systemic infections (e.g., 2-day history for some TSS cases; rapid onset for necrotizing pneumonia).
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** In SSSS, a positive Nikolsky sign (slight pressure displaces the skin) and blisters devoid of leukocytes/bacteria. In food poisoning, rapid projectile vomiting after ingestion of contaminated food (e.g., salted ham, potato salad) without high fever.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Septic embolization, multi-organ failure, shock lung, massive hemoptysis, valvular destruction, progressive structural joint or bone damage.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Permanent and transient normal human microbiota of the anterior nares (persistent in 15-50% of humans), oropharynx, moist skin folds, perineum, gastrointestinal tract, and urogenital tract. Also colonizes the umbilical stump of neonates. Present widely on environmental surfaces.
*   **Transmission Route:** Endogenous spread from carriage sites; direct person-to-person contact; exposure to contaminated fomites (bed linens, clothing, medical equipment). Foodborne transmission via ingestion of preformed enterotoxins in poorly refrigerated protein/carbohydrate-rich foods.
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:** Ubiquitous worldwide. CA-MRSA strains frequently cause community outbreaks of skin and soft tissue infections.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Widespread nosocomial problem necessitating strict hand hygiene, isolation/contact precautions for MRSA patients, and potentially excluding heavily colonized personnel from high-risk wards (ICUs, newborn nurseries, operating rooms).

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:** Gram-stained smears of pus or tissue aspirates reveal Gram-positive spherical cocci in grapelike clusters and an abundance of polymorphonuclear neutrophils (except in SSSS blisters). Gram stain of blood or food is generally not useful due to low bacterial concentration or toxin-mediated pathogenesis. As cells age or die, they may appear Gram-negative.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) are used for direct detection in blood cultures (e.g., Verigene, BioFire FilmArray) and for specific detection of the mecA or mecC genes for rapid MRSA identification. Also used to screen nasal carriage. Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) is used for rapid species identification.
*   **Serology & Immunology:** Rapid latex agglutination tests detect Protein A and clumping factor (bound coagulase). Antibodies to cell wall teichoic acids are rarely used diagnostically today. Serological testing for food poisoning relies on toxin detection rather than antibodies. Enterotoxins and TSST-1 can be detected in specific outbreaks.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:** Beta-lactamase-resistant penicillins (e.g., methicillin, [[Oxacillin]], [[Nafcillin]], [[Dicloxacillin]]) for methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA). For localized abscesses/cutaneous infections, incision and drainage is essential.
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:** For MRSA or penicillin-allergic patients: [[Vancomycin]] (drug of choice for serious systemic MRSA). [[Daptomycin]], [[Tigecycline]], [[Linezolid]], [[telavancin]], newer lipoglycopeptides, [[clindamycin]], or [[Cotrimoxazole|trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole]] (TMP-SMX) for community-acquired or susceptible strains.
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:**
    *   **Beta-lactamase (Penicillinase):** Plasmid-controlled hydrolysis of the beta-lactam ring (confers resistance to [[Penicillin G]], [[Ampicillin]]). Present in >90% of isolates.
    *   **mecA / mecC genes:** Carried on the staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec; types I-XII). Encodes a low-affinity penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a), causing intrinsic resistance to all beta-lactam antibiotics (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems), known as MRSA.
    *   **vanA gene:** Acquired via transposon Tn1546 on a conjugative multi-resistance plasmid from [[Enterococcus faecalis]]. Alters the peptidoglycan precursor to prevent [[vancomycin]] binding, conferring high-level resistance (VRSA).
    *   **Cell Wall Thickening:** Produces a disorganized, thickened cell wall matrix that traps [[vancomycin]] (VISA/low-level resistance).
    *   **Plasmids:** Transmit resistance to tetracyclines, erythromycins, and aminoglycosides.
    *   **Tolerance:** Wide gap between minimal inhibitory and minimal lethal concentrations, often due to lack of autolytic enzyme activation.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Contraindications:** Antimicrobial susceptibility testing is strictly required for all significant isolates. Monotherapy with agents prone to rapid resistance development (like [[Rifampin]]) should be avoided for active infection but may be used in combination. Systemic or prolonged bacteremia necessitates aggressive IV therapy and removal of infected foreign bodies. Food poisoning is intoxication; antibiotic treatment is not indicated (treat with fluids/electrolytes).
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** No successful human vaccine is available. Prophylaxis against carriage/shedding in high-risk patients or outbreaks utilizes intranasal mupirocin (often for 5 days) with or without topical chlorhexidine bathing. [[Rifampin]] combined with a second antistaphylococcal drug is sometimes used for severe carrier eradication.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Golden/yellow pigment", "Grapelike clusters", "Coagulase-positive", "Protein A", "Spider bite-like lesion" (CA-MRSA), "Nikolsky sign" (SSSS), "MRSA", "VRSA", "Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL)", "Toxic Shock Syndrome Toxin-1 (TSST-1)".
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** While generally forming standard colonies with beta-hemolysis, S. aureus can form Small Colony Variants (SCVs) that appear as pinpoint, non-pigmented, non-hemolytic colonies to survive intracellularly and evade immune clearance. In addition, rare anaerobic growth behavior is observed initially in S. aureus subsp. anaerobius. Finally, while TSST-1 drives TSS, Enterotoxin B (and rarely C) causes about half of non-menstrual TSS cases.
