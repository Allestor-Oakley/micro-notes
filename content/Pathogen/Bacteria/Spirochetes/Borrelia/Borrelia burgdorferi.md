### Borrelia burgdorferi

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:** The protoplasmic core of the bacterium is enclosed in a cytoplasmic membrane and a conventional cell wall. This, in turn, is surrounded by an outer envelope or sheath. The outer membrane is similar to that of Gram-negative bacteria but uniquely lacks lipopolysaccharide (LPS). The membrane possesses numerous lipoproteins (including outer surface proteins OspA to F).
*   **Appendages:** Highly motile due to variable numbers (7 to 11) of endoflagella (also called periplasmic flagella or axial fibrils). These are located between the protoplasmic core and the outer sheath, anchored at either end of the bacterium, and wrap around the protoplasmic core.
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:** N/A
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:** Non-spore-forming.

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:** Microaerophilic to anaerobic and extremely sensitive to oxygen. Growth is slow, with a generation time of 18 hours or longer. The organism lacks a tricarboxylic acid cycle and has no genes for catalase or superoxide dismutase to protect it from oxygen toxicity. It is entirely dependent on host cells for all purines, pyrimidines, and most amino acids.
*   **Primary Agar:** Grows most readily in a complex liquid medium known as Barbour-Stoenner-Kelly medium (BSK II).
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:** [[Rifampin]], [[fosfomycin]] (phosphonomycin), and amphotericin B can be added to BSK II medium to reduce the rate of culture contamination by other bacteria and fungi.
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:** Stains readily with acid and aniline dyes (e.g., Giemsa or Wright stains) and by silver impregnation techniques. Because culture is complex, specialized, and yields a low diagnostic return, biochemical testing of isolates is seldom used clinically.

#### Virulence Factors & Genetics
*   **Secretion Systems:** N/A
*   **Exotoxins:**

    *   **Mechanism of Action:** Does not produce recognized toxins.
*   **Endotoxins & Superantigens:** N/A (Lacks lipopolysaccharide/LPS).
*   **Enzymes:** N/A
*   **Genetic Elements:** The genome consists of an unusual linear chromosome of about 950 kb and multiple circular and linear plasmids. These plasmids carry numerous sequences for lipoproteins (OspA to F).

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:** Transmitted to humans by the injection of the organism in tick saliva or by regurgitation of the tick's midgut contents. The tick must typically feed for 24 to 48 hours before transmission occurs. The organism adheres to proteoglycans on host cells, mediated by a borrelial glycosaminoglycan receptor. After injection, the organism migrates outward from the site, producing the characteristic skin lesion, and subsequently disseminates via the lymphatics or blood to other skin, musculoskeletal sites, and major organs.
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:** Phagocytosed by host [[Monocyte|monocytes]], [[Macrophage|macrophages]], and [[Dendritic Cell|dendritic cells]]. The phagosome appears to be the single location where all spirochetal pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), including lipoprotein ligands and nucleic acids, are made available for sampling by host pattern recognition receptors.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** Regulates growth via differential gene expression with the upregulation and downregulation of outer surface proteins. Outer surface protein A (OspA) is expressed in the midgut of unfed ticks (binding to gut proteins); upon tick feeding, OspA is repressed, and outer surface protein C (OspC) is upregulated, which is critical for transmission to mammals. Additionally, the organism depresses the host immune response at the time skin lesions initially develop.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Innate immune cell activation occurs predominantly when the spirochete is phagocytosed and degraded, exposing its PAMPs to endosomal Toll-like receptors (TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 for nucleic acids) and TLR2 (for lipoproteins) via the MyD88 adapter protein. Live intact spirochetes trigger a highly robust inflammatory response. Eventual clearance is complement-mediated and driven by antibodies that develop over months to years.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:**
    *   **Early Localized Lyme Disease:** Characterized by erythema migrans (a small macule at the tick bite site that enlarges over weeks to form a flat border with central clearing, approximately 5 cm or more in diameter). The rash is accompanied by a flu-like prodrome (malaise, fatigue, headache, fever, chills, musculoskeletal pains, myalgias, and lymphadenopathy).
    *   **Early Disseminated & Late Lyme Disease:** Without treatment, infection progresses to include severe intermittent oligoarticular arthritis (recurrent attacks of swelling and pain in a few large joints, often knees), cardiac dysfunction (heart block, myopericarditis, congestive heart failure), and neurologic signs (facial palsy, meningitis, encephalitis). Acrodermatitis chronica atrophicans (bluish-red skin lesions) represents a late disseminated manifestation.
*   **Incubation Period:** Ranges from days to weeks after exposure. In typical presentations, the rash and flu-like illness may appear ~3 to 12 days or up to 4 weeks after the tick bite.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Erythema migrans (expanding "bull's-eye" or target-shaped red rash, often with central clearing).
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Chronic Lyme arthritis, lasting months or longer. Persistent severe neurological and cardiovascular damage if left untreated.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** The white-footed mouse is the primary reservoir and host for the larval and nymphal forms of the tick. The white-tailed deer serves as a host for adult ticks.
*   **Transmission Route:** Zoonotic vector-borne transmission via the bite of infected hard ticks of the genus Ixodes. The nymphal stage (about the size of a poppy seed, ~2 mm) is responsible for most human infections because it is small and easily goes unnoticed.
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:** Temperate zones of the Northern Hemisphere. In the United States, it is highly prevalent in the Northeast, Midwest, and West Coast. In Europe and Asia, it is also highly endemic (with Borrelia afzelii and Borrelia garinii co-circulating). Most cases occur from June through August when nymphal ticks feed. The geographic spread parallels the explosive growth of the white-tailed deer population.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Lyme disease is the most prevalent arthropod-transmitted infection in the USA and Europe. The CDC defines Lyme disease clinically by the presence of erythema migrans or at least one late manifestation (musculoskeletal, nervous system, or cardiovascular) coupled with laboratory confirmation.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:** Microscopic detection of the organism in clinical specimens (blood, skin lesions) is rarely successful because the spirochetes are present in very low numbers.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification to detect bacterial nucleic acids in plasma or skin biopsies is available but generally insensitive in the early phases of the disease.
*   **Serology & Immunology:** Serology is the mainstay of laboratory diagnosis, although unreliable early in the disease before antibodies develop. The CDC recommends a two-stage approach: an initial screening with an Enzyme Immunoassay (EIA) or Immunofluorescence Assay (IFA), followed by a confirmatory Western immunoblot assay to detect IgM and IgG antibodies reactive with specific B. burgdorferi antigens if the screening test is positive or equivocal. A significant increase in antibody titer between acute and convalescent serum samples is also diagnostic.
*   **Culture:** Extremely difficult, complex, and low-yield. If attempted, B. burgdorferi has been most easily isolated from erythema migrans skin biopsies cultured in BSK II medium, but this is seldom used clinically.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:** For adult patients with early localized or early disseminated Lyme disease associated with erythema migrans: Oral [[doxycycline]] (100 mg twice daily) for 14 to 21 days, [[Amoxicillin]] (500 mg three times daily) for 14 to 21 days, or [[cefuroxime]] axetil (500 mg twice daily) for 14 to 21 days.
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:** [[Azithromycin]] or [[clarithromycin]].
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Contraindications:** Patients with advanced neurological or cardiac manifestations typically require intravenous antibiotic therapy with [[ceftriaxone]], [[cefotaxime]], or [[Penicillin G]].
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** No effective human vaccine is currently available. Prevention strictly relies on minimizing tick exposure via the use of protective clothing, tick repellents, and the prompt detection and removal of attached ticks.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Erythema migrans", "Bull's-eye rash", "Ixodes tick", "White-footed mouse", "White-tailed deer", "OspA and OspC", "BSK II medium", "Two-stage serologic testing".
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Although B. burgdorferi possesses an outer membrane functionally structurally similar to those found in typical Gram-negative bacteria, it completely lacks lipopolysaccharide (LPS), differentiating its immune activation profile from traditional Gram-negative pathogens. Unlike other systemic bacterial infections, it utilizes an unusual linear chromosome.
