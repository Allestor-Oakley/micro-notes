### Borrelia recurrentis

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:** The organism possesses an outer membrane similar to that of Gram-negative bacteria, but it stains poorly with standard Gram stain reagents and is functionally considered neither Gram-positive nor Gram-negative.
*   **Appendages:** N/A
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:** N/A
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:** Non-spore-forming.

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:** Microaerophilic or anaerobic, and extremely sensitive to oxygen. The bacteria lack genes for catalase or superoxide dismutase to protect them from oxygen toxicity. They possess complex nutritional needs (requiring N-acetylglucosamine, long-chain saturated and unsaturated fatty acids, glucose, and amino acids) because they lack the tricarboxylic acid cycle and are dependent on host cells for purines, pyrimidines, and most amino acids. The generation time is very slow (18 hours or longer).
*   **Primary Agar:** In vitro culture is generally unsuccessful and difficult; therefore, it is rarely used for clinical diagnosis. When maintained at 4°C, the organisms can survive for several months in infected blood or culture.
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:** N/A
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:** Stains well with aniline dyes (e.g., Giemsa or Wright stains). It does not produce recognized toxins.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:** Transmitted to humans by the human body louse (Pediculus humanus). The spirochete is inoculated into the human host when an infected louse is crushed and its body fluids or feces contaminate the bite wound or skin excoriations from scratching.
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:** Extracellular (replicates freely in the bloodstream).
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** Undergoes profound antigenic shift/variation to escape immune clearance. The antibodies produced initially by the host act as a selective pressure, permitting the survival of only antigenically distinct variants. These new variants then multiply and cause a disease relapse. The relapsing course of the disease is driven by the sequential emergence of these new variants, against which the host must continually develop new antibodies.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Host antibodies develop in high titers during the febrile stage of the illness. The clinical attack is terminated by the agglutinating and lytic effects of these antibodies. Ultimate recovery, which typically occurs after 3 to 10 relapses, requires the host to produce antibodies against several different antigenic variants.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:**
    *   **Epidemic Relapsing Fever (Louse-Borne Relapsing Fever):** A severe febrile illness characterized by recurrent episodes of fever and septicemia separated by afebrile periods.
*   **Incubation Period:** N/A
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** An abrupt rise in temperature that persists for 3 to 5 days, followed by a brief afebrile hiatus, and then a subsequent second attack (relapse).
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Epidemic (louse-borne) relapsing fever carries a significantly higher mortality rate than endemic (tick-borne) relapsing fever.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Humans are the only known reservoir (no animal host).
*   **Transmission Route:** Person-to-person transmission via the human body louse.
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:** Historically found worldwide, particularly in mountainous regions and areas facing civil unrest. Currently, it is endemic in Ethiopia, Eritrea, Somalia, and Sudan. It primarily occurs among individuals living in crowded or unsanitary conditions, such as refugee and homeless populations.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Control of the disease requires improving sanitary conditions, implementing delousing procedures, and treating clothing to decrease exposure to the human body louse vector.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:** Microscopy of the blood is the diagnostic test of choice. Thick and thin peripheral blood smears stained with aniline dyes (such as Wright or Giemsa stains) easily reveal the large spirochetes when collected during the febrile stage of the illness.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays on whole blood.
*   **Serology & Immunology:** Serologic testing is not recommended or useful for diagnosis due to the organism's continual antigenic variation.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:** Tetracyclines (e.g., [[doxycycline]]) or penicillins.
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:** [[Erythromycin]].
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Contraindications:** N/A
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** No vaccine is currently available. Prevention relies entirely on vector control (delousing) and improved hygiene.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Epidemic relapsing fever", "Human body louse vector", "Pediculus humanus", "Antigenic shift/variation", "Thick and thin blood smears", "Giemsa stain", "Recurrent febrile episodes".
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Although it possesses an outer membrane structurally similar to Gram-negative bacteria, it stains so poorly with Gram stain reagents that it is clinically considered neither Gram-positive nor Gram-negative.
