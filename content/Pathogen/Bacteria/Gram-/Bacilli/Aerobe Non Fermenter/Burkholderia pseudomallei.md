### Burkholderia pseudomallei

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:** Possesses a Gram-negative cell wall featuring lipopolysaccharide (LPS).
*   **Appendages:** Actively motile via flagella; also possesses pili.
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:** Produces an antiphagocytic extracellular polysaccharide capsule (a 200 kDa extracellular polysaccharide).
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:** Non-spore-forming.

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:** Aerobic, saprophytic organism that grows at 42°C.
*   **Primary Agar:** Readily grows on standard bacteriologic media (e.g., sheep blood agar). Colonies are initially (at 24–48 hours) mucoid and smooth/creamy, but upon extended incubation, they characteristically become rough, wrinkled (rugose), and change in color from cream to orange. The cultures emit a characteristic sweet, earthy smell.
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:** Ashdown's medium and commercial *[[Burkholderia cepacia]]* selective agars are heavily recommended to increase isolation rates from nonsterile clinical sites by suppressing normal flora.
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:** Oxidase-positive, indole-negative. Oxidizes glucose, lactose, and a variety of other carbohydrates. Exhibits arginine dihydrolase and gelatinase activity.

#### Virulence Factors & Genetics
*   **Secretion Systems:** Utilizes a Type III secretion system that is essential for intracellular growth, survival, and cell-to-cell spread.
*   **Exotoxins:**
    *   **Toxin Name:** Secretes various unidentified toxins, proteases, lipases, and lecithinases.
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** Mediate tissue destruction and abscess formation.
*   **Endotoxins & Superantigens:** Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) possesses endotoxin activity contributing to septic shock.
*   **Enzymes:** Protease, lipase, lecithinase, arginine dihydrolase, and gelatinase.
*   **Genetic Elements:** The genome is extremely large and complex, consisting of two chromosomes (4.07 and 3.17 megabase pairs) associated with core and accessory functions, respectively. A high proportion of genomic islands within the genome contributes to substantial genetic diversity between strains. *Burkholderia mallei* (the agent of glanders) is essentially a clone of *B. pseudomallei* that lost genetic material as it adapted strictly to equine hosts.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:** Infection is acquired from the environment via percutaneous inoculation (cuts/abrasions in the skin), inhalation (especially during severe weather events like typhoons/cyclones), or ingestion of contaminated food and water.
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:** Facultative intracellular pathogen. It has the ability to survive and grow intracellularly within [[Macrophage|macrophages]] and other phagocytes.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The polysaccharide capsule is antiphagocytic. It utilizes its Type III secretion system to escape into and spread within host cells. It can also become metabolically inactive within granulomas, contributing to long-term latency and persistence. It produces a siderophore ("malleobactin") to scavenge host iron. Quorum sensing mechanisms also regulate virulence.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Most exposed individuals remain asymptomatic. However, clinical disease is heavily linked to specific underlying immunocompromising conditions. Diabetes mellitus is the most important risk factor (present in ~46% of cases globally). Other major predispositions include chronic renal failure, chronic lung disease (including cystic fibrosis), alcohol abuse, liver disease, glucocorticoid therapy, thalassemia, and chronic granulomatous disease. Strikingly, [[HIV]] infection does *not* appear to predispose individuals to melioidosis. An exaggerated host inflammatory response (with elevated IL-6 and IL-18) correlates with a fatal outcome.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Melioidosis (Whitmore's disease). It most frequently manifests as community-acquired sepsis and pneumonia (with a pronounced tendency to cavitate, mimicking tuberculosis). It also presents as acute localized suppurative cutaneous infections with regional lymphadenitis.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Disseminated disease frequently causes widespread metastatic abscesses with distinct predilections for the liver, [[spleen]], prostate, and parotid glands. Can also cause osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, meningoencephalitis, keratitis, mastitis, and mycotic aneurysms.
*   **Incubation Period:** Highly variable. Acute infection usually develops 1 to 21 days (mean 9 days) after exposure. Uniquely, the organism can establish latent infections that reactivate months to decades later (nicknamed the "Vietnamese time-bomb").
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Multiple severe abscesses (particularly prostatic abscesses in men or parotid abscesses in children in endemic regions) coupled with cavitary pneumonia or sepsis in a patient with a history of travel to Southeast Asia or Australia.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Septic shock carrying a high mortality rate (historically up to 50%, though lower in advanced ICU settings). Severe structural lung damage and chronic cognitive/functional impairment following CNS or musculoskeletal involvement.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Environmental saprophyte residing in soil, surface water, fresh water, rice paddies, and vegetation. While epizootic infections occur in sheep, goats, swine, and horses, animals are not considered the primary reservoir.
*   **Transmission Route:** Direct contact of skin abrasions with contaminated soil/water, inhalation of aerosolized dust or water, or ingestion. Disease incidence is highly seasonal, peaking during the monsoon wet season (75-85% of cases). Person-to-person spread and maternal-fetal transmission are extremely rare.
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:** Highly endemic in Southeast Asia (especially northeast Thailand) and Northern Australia (Northern Territory). Widely distributed across the tropics and increasingly recognized in the Americas, the Caribbean, and sub-Saharan Africa. Outbreaks have occurred following the 2004 Asian tsunami, severe typhoons, and from contaminated home aquarium water or imported aromatherapy sprays in the US.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Classified as a Tier 1 Select Agent and Category B agent of bioterrorism due to its severe morbidity, mortality, and ease of aerosol dissemination. Laboratory work with cultures must be performed in Hazard Group 3 / Biosafety Level 3 containment facilities to prevent laboratory-acquired infections.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:** Gram stain, Wright's stain, or methylene blue stain of clinical specimens (pus, sputum, urine) may reveal small Gram-negative rods with a prominent bipolar ("safety pin") appearance. Rapid immunofluorescence microscopy of specimens is highly specific.
*   **Molecular Testing:** PCR assays have been developed but lack widespread routine clinical use due to cost and sensitivity issues. MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry can accurately identify the organism if the appropriate reference database is utilized.
*   **Serology & Immunology:** Indirect haemagglutination (IHA) tests are widely used in endemic areas but suffer from poor sensitivity and specificity due to high background seropositivity in the population. Rapid diagnostic tests (lateral flow immunoassays detecting extracellular polysaccharide) and latex agglutination tests for suspect colonies are available.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:** Treatment requires a prolonged, biphasic approach.
    *   *Intensive Parenteral Phase:* Intravenous [[Ceftazidime]] or [[Meropenem]] for a minimum of 10 to 14 days. [[Meropenem]] is preferred for septic shock or neurological melioidosis. Duration is extended to 4–8 weeks for deep-seated abscesses, osteomyelitis, or CNS involvement.
    *   *Oral Eradication Phase:* [[Cotrimoxazole]] ([[Cotrimoxazole|trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole]] (TMP-SMX)) for 12 to 24 weeks to prevent disease relapse.
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:** [[Imipenem]] can be used in the intensive phase. Co-amoxiclav ([[amoxicillin]]-clavulanic acid) or [[Doxycycline]] are second-line alternatives for the oral eradication phase if the patient is intolerant to or the strain is resistant to [[Cotrimoxazole|TMP-SMX]].
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:** The organism is intrinsically resistant to [[penicillin]], [[ampicillin]], first- and second-generation cephalosporins, macrolides, polymyxins ([[Polymyxin E|colistin]]), and aminoglycosides ([[gentamicin]], [[tobramycin]]). Acquired resistance to [[Cotrimoxazole|TMP-SMX]] has been reported (varying geographically from 2% to 16%).
*   **Treatment Nuances & Contraindications:** A simple "3-disc test" in the lab can act as a screening tool: an oxidase-positive Gram-negative rod resistant to aminoglycosides and [[Polymyxin E|colistin]] but susceptible to co-amoxiclav should be treated as *B. pseudomallei* until proven otherwise. Surgical or radiologic drainage of large abscesses (e.g., prostatic, hepatic) is critical for cure. High rates of relapse (6-10%) are most frequently caused by poor patient compliance with the grueling 3- to 6-month oral eradication phase.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** There is currently no available human vaccine. Prevention relies heavily on avoiding exposure to soil and standing water in endemic regions, particularly for diabetics. Post-exposure antimicrobial prophylaxis guidelines exist for accidental laboratory exposures.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Vietnamese time-bomb" (latent reactivation), "Whitmore's disease", "the remarkable imitator", sweet/earthy odor, rugose colonies on Ashdown's medium, bipolar safety-pin staining, parotid/prostatic abscesses in diabetics from Thailand/Australia.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** *B. pseudomallei* is unusual among Gram-negative opportunistic pathogens in that it is intrinsically resistant to both aminoglycosides and [[Polymyxin E|colistin]]/polymyxins (drugs typically reserved for highly resistant Gram-negative infections), yet it frequently remains susceptible to the relatively common oral agent co-amoxiclav. Furthermore, unlike most opportunistic infections which heavily afflict AIDS patients, [[HIV]] infection is not a notable risk factor for melioidosis.
