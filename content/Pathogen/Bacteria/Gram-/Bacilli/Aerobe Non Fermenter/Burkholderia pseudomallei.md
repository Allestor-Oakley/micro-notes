### Burkholderia pseudomallei

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:**
    - Possesses a typical Gram-negative outer membrane bilayer structure containing a lipid-anchored lipopolysaccharide (LPS) complex.
    - Demonstrates intrinsic biochemical barriers within its outer membrane and cell envelope that restrict the penetration of standard detergents, early beta-lactams, polymyxins (such as [[Polymyxin|colistin]]), and aminoglycosides ([[gentamicin]] and [[tobramycin]]).
    - Undergoes phenotypic envelope modifications and phase transition/switching, altering the lipopolysaccharide profile (including LPS modifications) which directly changes colony appearance and correlates with environmental or intracellular adaptation.
*   **Appendages:**
    - Highly motile by means of polar flagella (flagellar motility), permitting active chemotaxis and movement through saturated soils and water.
    - Features surface pili (fimbriae) and non-pilus outer membrane adhesins that facilitate initial contact, cell-surface anchoring, and attachment to eukaryotic host cells.
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:**
    - Encapsulated by a copious, hydrophilic, extracellular polysaccharide capsule (characterized by a 200 kDa extracellular polysaccharide antigen).
    - The capsule forms a protective, well-defined physical matrix surrounding the cell envelope that repels host clearance mechanisms, acts as a primary barrier to toxic hydrophobic molecules, and is a major virulence determinant.
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:**
    - Non-spore-forming (does not produce endospores).
    - N/A

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:**
    - Obligately aerobic organism with a broad metabolic capacity.
    - Capable of growing at elevated temperatures up to 42°C (a key physiological characteristic used to differentiate it from some other environmental Gram-negative rods).
    - Typically visible on standard agar media within 24 to 48 hours, though growth is slower than that of enteric Gram-negative rods, and full colony development can require up to 72 hours.
*   **Primary Agar:**
    - Grows readily on non-selective general-purpose media, including [[5% sheep blood agar]], [[chocolate agar]], and standard enteric differential agars (such as [[MacConkey agar]] or eosin-methylene blue agar), on which it forms non-lactose-fermenting colonies.
    - Colonial morphology is highly variable and dynamic: initially presenting at 24–48 hours as smooth, mucoid, creamy, or dome-shaped colonies, which upon further incubation (3 days or longer) characteristically switch to a rough, dry, wrinkled (rugose) appearance, accompanied by a color transition from cream to orange or deep cream, and emitting a sweet, earthy odor.
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:**
    - **Ashdown's Medium:** The primary selective agar utilized in endemic regions; formulated with crystal violet and [[gentamicin]] to suppress contaminating normal flora, allowing selective recovery of B. pseudomallei.
    - **Burkholderia cepacia selective agar (BCSA):** Highly selective medium recommended in non-endemic areas (e.g., in reference or clinical laboratories screening patients with [[cystic fibrosis]]) and compares favorably to other selective media designed to grow B. pseudomallei.
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:**
    - **Oxidase Reaction:** Positive.
    - **Catalase Reaction:** Positive.
    - **Indole Reaction:** Negative (a critical biochemical differentiator from other oxidase-positive Gram-negative rods like [[Pasteurella multocida]]).
    - **Arginine Dihydrolase:** Positive.
    - **Gelatinase Activity:** Positive.
    - **Carbohydrate Metabolism:** Non-fermentative; metabolizes glucose, lactose, and a wide variety of other carbohydrates oxidatively (oxidizes carbohydrates, does not ferment).
    - **The 3-Disc Screening Test:** Presumptively identified in resource-constrained areas using a simple triple-disk screening array showing intrinsic resistance to [[Polymyxin|colistin]] (polymyxins) and [[gentamicin]] (aminoglycosides) combined with susceptibility to co-amoxiclav.

#### Virulence Factors & Genetics
*   **Secretion Systems:**
    - Employs a specialized Type III Secretion System (T3SS) that is functionally associated with host cell invasion, intracellular replication, phagosomal escape, and direct cell-to-cell spread.
*   **Exotoxins:**
    - **Toxin Name:** N/A
    - **Mechanism of Action:** N/A
*   **Endotoxins & Superantigens:**
    - **Endotoxins:** Contains lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in its outer membrane. Although the lipid A moiety exhibits standard endotoxin activity capable of triggering [[Monocyte and Macrophage]] activation and systemic inflammatory cascades (contributing to severe septic shock), its activity is relatively low compared to some enteric Gram-negative rods.
    - **Superantigens:** N/A
*   **Enzymes:**
    - Secretes extracellular degradative enzymes including proteases, lipases, lecithinase, and gelatinase, which damage host cell membranes, degrade structural proteins, and promote necrotizing tissue destruction and abscess formation.
    - Produces arginine dihydrolase.
*   **Genetic Elements:**
    - Features an exceptionally large, complex, and highly plastic prokaryotic genome totaling approximately 7.24 megabase pairs (Mb) organized into two distinct circular chromosomes: Chromosome 1 (approximately 4.07 Mb, primarily encoding core cellular and housekeeping metabolic functions) and Chromosome 2 (approximately 3.17 Mb, primarily encoding accessory, lifestyle, and virulence-associated functions).
    - Contains a high proportion of genomic islands, insertion sequences, and accessory genetic loci that drive extensive horizontal gene transfer and substantial strain-to-strain genetic diversity.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:**
    - **Entry:** Typically acquired from the environment via percutaneous inoculation (through skin abrasions, lacerations, or micro-trauma during contact with contaminated soil or water), inhalation of contaminated dust, soil, or water aerosols (particularly during severe weather events such as monsoons, typhoons, or cyclones), or ingestion of contaminated water or food.
    - **Attachment:** Surface pili (fimbriae) and non-pilus outer membrane adhesins bind specifically to host cell receptors, initiating adhesion and colonization.
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:**
    - **Intracellular Survival:** A highly adapted facultative intracellular pathogen. Following receptor-mediated endocytosis or phagocytosis, the bacterium utilizes its Type III secretion system to rupture the phagosomal membrane and escape into the host cell cytoplasm before phagolysosomal fusion can occur. Once in the cytosol, it replicates rapidly and uses host actin polymerization to move directionally, forming cell membrane protrusions (filopodia) that fuse with adjacent cell membranes, facilitating direct cell-to-cell spread and forming multinucleated giant cells (syncytia) without entering the extracellular space.
    - **Extracellular Survival:** Highly stable and persistent in the external environment, surviving for prolonged periods as a saprophyte in wet soils, surface waters, rice paddies, and moist environmental niches.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:**
    - **Polysaccharide Capsule:** The hydrophilic capsular antigens physically mask somatic cell wall antigens, repel the hydrophobic surfaces of phagocytic cells, and inhibit complement-mediated opsonization and serum-mediated bactericidal killing.
    - **Intracellular Sequestration:** Living and replicating within the host cell cytoplasm (and potentially becoming metabolically inactive or dormant within chronic granulomatous lesions) shields the pathogen from neutralizing antibodies, complement factors, and public immune clearance, while significantly restricting antibiotic access.
    - **Phenotypic Switching:** Rapidly undergoes colony morphology and phenotypic switching, allowing clonal populations to adapt dynamically to host immune pressures and tissue microenvironments.
    - **Siderophore Production:** Secretes a specialized high-affinity iron-chelating siderophore called malleobactin, which successfully sequesters host iron from iron-binding proteins (such as transferrin or lactoferrin) to support its own growth during intracellular and systemic infection.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:**
    - Host defense depends heavily on intact innate immune cascades, particularly functional neutrophils, [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]], and cell-mediated ([[T-Cell|T-cell]]) immune responses.
    - Widespread systemic disease is strongly correlated with specific host predisposing factors that compromise clearance: **diabetes mellitus** represents the single most powerful clinical risk factor (strongly associated with severe, bacteremic infection). Other major links include chronic renal disease, chronic lung disease, and heavy alcohol use (alcoholism).
    - Notably, there is **no epidemiological or clinical association** between melioidosis and [[HIV]] infection or acquired CD4+ [[T-Cell|T-cell]] immunodeficiencies, though up to 20% of infected individuals present with no identifiable underlying host risk factor.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:**
    - **Melioidosis:** An extremely versatile disease (referred to as the "great mimicker") that can present in acute, subacute, or chronic forms, or remain clinically latent.
    - **Pulmonary Melioidosis (Pulmonary Infection):** The most common clinical manifestation. Can present as a primary pneumonitis or a necrotizing pneumonia following direct inhalation or hematogenous seeding. Acute cases present with high fever, cough, pleuritic chest pain, rales, leukocytosis, and patchy upper lobe consolidation. Over time, the disease can mimic pulmonary tuberculosis, producing progressive cavitation (upper lobe cavities), weight loss, and hemoptysis, with chest X-ray findings showing apical infiltrates and cavitary lesions.
    - **Septicemic Melioidosis (Acute Septicemia):** A fulminant, life-threatening form of community-acquired [[sepsis]] characterized by rapid onset of high fever, shaking chills, severe prostration, [[diarrhea]], and hypotension progressing rapidly to septic shock. It is frequently accompanied by severe metabolic acidosis (presenting clinically with deep, rapid Kussmaul's breathing) and the rapid hematogenous dissemination of the pathogen, seeding multiple organs and producing widespread metastatic abscesses.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:**
    - **Chronic Melioidosis:** Defined as symptoms persisting for longer than 2 months. Manifests as a indolent, chronic suppurative disease featuring multiple metastatic or localized abscesses in the skin, subcutaneous tissues, brain, lungs, myocardium, liver, [[Spleen]], bones (causing chronic [[osteomyelitis]]), and joints (causing septic arthritis). Patients with chronic suppurative infections are frequently afebrile and have slowly progressive, localized pathology.
    - **Suppurative Parotitis:** Acute suppurative infection of the parotid gland, presenting with unilateral or bilateral parotid swelling, pain, and fever. It is particularly common in children in endemic regions of Thailand, where it is linked to the ingestion of contaminated water, but is extremely rare in Australia.
    - **Prostatic Abscesses & Neurological Melioidosis:** These presentations are reported with significantly higher relative frequency in Australia than in Thailand. Neurological disease can manifest as meningoencephalitis, brain abscesses, or cranial nerve palsies.
    - **Cutaneous Melioidosis:** Localized suppurative cutaneous infection presenting as single or multiple localized skin pustules, ulcers, or abscesses at the site of skin abrasion/inoculation, typically accompanied by localized [[cellulitis]], regional [[lymphadenitis]], fever, and malaise.
    - **Mycotic Aneurysms:** A serious and increasingly recognized vascular complication of septicemia, presenting with infected arterial walls and high risk of rupture.
    - **Latent Infection:** The bacterium can persist in a transcriptionally silent, dormant state within host tissues for months, years, or even decades before reactivating. Reactivation is often triggered by intercurrent illness, trauma, or immunosuppression (historically termed the "Vietnamese time bomb" in military veterans).
*   **Incubation Period:**
    - Median incubation period for acute disease is 9 days (overall range of 1 to 21 days), with symptoms developing in as short as 24 to 72 hours following high-dose exposure or near-drowning events; however, latent periods can extend to months, years, or decades.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:**
    - N/A (There are no strictly pathognomonic clinical signs; however, the clinical finding of a severe necrotizing pneumonia with upper lobe cavitation and multiple visceral abscesses (specifically in the liver, [[Spleen]], and prostate) in a diabetic patient from an endemic tropical region is highly suggestive).
*   **Complications & Sequelae:**
    - **Septic Shock:** Carries an extremely high mortality rate (exceeding 50% in resource-constrained areas or if untreated, but reduced to approximately 6% in high-income settings with early intensive management).
    - **Thoracic Complications:** Development of pneumothorax, [[empyema]], and purulent [[pericarditis]] secondary to necrotizing pulmonary lesions.
    - **Facial Nerve Palsy:** A rare focal complication resulting from severe suppurative parotitis.
    - **Neurological and Cognitive Deficits:** Long-term cognitive, motor, or functional impairment following meningoencephalitis or brain abscesses.
    - **Joint and Bone Deformity:** Chronic arthritic symptoms, joint destruction, and impaired mobility following septic arthritis or [[osteomyelitis]].
    - **Premature Death:** Survivors have a significantly elevated long-term risk of premature death, even after successful microbiological cure.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:**
    - **Primary Reservoir:** Saturated soils and surface water in tropical and subtropical regions (a natural environmental saprophyte, particularly concentrated in wet agricultural soils, fresh water, mud, rice paddies, and vegetable produce).
    - **Animal Reservoirs:** Not considered primary reservoirs, but epizootic infections occur commonly in livestock, including sheep, goats, swine, horses, and a variety of wild animals.
*   **Transmission Route:**
    - **Percutaneous Inoculation:** The most common route of exposure; direct contact of damaged skin, abrasions, or open wounds with contaminated mud, soil, or surface water (especially common among rice farmers, construction workers, and indigenous populations).
    - **Inhalation:** Inhalation of contaminated dust, soil particles, or water aerosols, particularly during severe weather events such as monsoons, tropical storms, typhoons, or cyclones.
    - **Ingestion:** Ingestion of contaminated drinking water or food.
    - **Atypical/Zoonotic Transmission:** Occasional cases linked to near-drowning/water immersion (e.g., Asian tsunami survivors), exposure to contaminated potable water supply pipelines, home freshwater aquariums containing imported tropical fish, or imported aromatherapy sprays. Person-to-person transmission, transplacental transmission, and mother-to-infant transmission via breast milk are extremely rare.
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:**
    - Predominantly endemic in Southeast Asia (especially Northeast Thailand), Northern Australia, parts of India, Africa, South America, and Central America. The incidence of disease is highly seasonal, peaking sharply during the wet monsoon season when environmental exposure to soil and surface water is maximized.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:**
    - **Bioterrorism Status:** Classified as a Category B Select Agent (Tier 1 Select Agent) by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) due to its high infectivity, potential for dissemination (via aerosolization), and severe morbidity/mortality if untreated.
    - **Laboratory Biosafety:** Classified as a Hazard Group 3 pathogen. Working with live cultures carries a risk of laboratory-acquired infection through accidental aerosol generation; therefore, all manipulation of viable isolates must be conducted within a certified Biosafety Level 3 (BSL-3) facility using a class II biological safety cabinet.
    - **Isolation Precautions:** Universal precautions are sufficient in healthcare settings; person-to-person transmission is extremely rare, so strict patient isolation is not required unless co-infected with other transmissible pathogens (e.g., active pulmonary tuberculosis).
    - **Prevention and Control:** Public health measures focus on minimizing environmental contact. Diabetics, agricultural workers, and high-risk individuals in endemic areas should wear protective rubber boots and gloves when working in wet soil or water, avoid wading in standing water, and clean skin wounds immediately.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:**
    - **Gram Stain:** Small, thin, Gram-negative rods (bacilli) arranged singly, in pairs, or short chains.
    - **Wright's or Giemsa Stain:** Demonstrates characteristic, irregularly staining Gram-negative bacilli with prominent **bipolar staining** (yielding a classic "safety pin" or "hairpin" appearance).
    - **Methylene Blue Stain:** Also useful for highlighting the characteristic bipolar staining in blood culture or pus smears.
    - **Immunofluorescence Microscopy (IF):** Direct IF utilizing specific monoclonal antibodies against the 200 kDa extracellular polysaccharide is highly specific and rapidly identifies B. pseudomallei in clinical specimens (sputum, pus, urine), but is less sensitive than culture.
*   **Molecular Testing:**
    - **Polymerase Chain Reaction ([[PCR]]):** Standard TaqMan [[PCR]] assays targeting specific genetic loci are highly sensitive and specific for confirming cultured isolates, but are not in routine clinical use for direct blood/serum detection due to variable sensitivity in early paucibacillary states.
    - **MALDI-TOF MS:** Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry reliably and rapidly identifies the organism directly from agar colonies, provided that the reference database includes B. pseudomallei (which may be omitted or locked in standard commercial databases due to select agent restrictions).
*   **Serology & Immunology:**
    - **Serological Assays:** Indirect immunofluorescence assays (IFA) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays ([[Lab/Molecular and Immunological/ELISA]]) measure IgM and IgG antibodies to B. pseudomallei. They are highly valuable for epidemiologic surveys but have poor diagnostic utility for acute individual management in endemic regions due to high baseline seroprevalence (up to 60-70% of healthy individuals in endemic Northeast Thailand have antibodies by age 4). A fourfold rise in paired sera or a single high titer (1:160 or greater) is presumptively helpful.
    - **Latex Agglutination:** Rapid card test utilizing a monoclonal antibody against the 200 kDa extracellular polysaccharide is highly sensitive and specific for the rapid screening of suspect colonies.
    - **Lateral Flow Immunoassay (LFI):** A rapid antigen detection test that identifies extracellular polysaccharide in clinical samples (urine, pus, sputum) with sensitivity and specificity comparable to immunofluorescence.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:**
    - **Initial Intensive Phase (Parenteral):** Minimum of 10 to 14 days of intravenous therapy utilizing either **[[Ceftazidime]]** (2 g IV every 8 hours) or **[[Meropenem]]** (1 g IV every 8 hours). [[Meropenem]] has lower minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) and superior in vitro bactericidal activity, and is the preferred first-line agent in patients with severe [[sepsis]], septic shock, or central nervous system (CNS) involvement.
    - **Eradication Phase (Oral):** Initiated immediately following the intensive phase to completely eliminate the pathogen and prevent relapse. Continued for a minimum of 3 months utilizing oral **[[Cotrimoxazole|Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole]] (TMP-SMX)** (dose adjusted for weight and renal function).
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:**
    - **Intensive Phase Alternatives:** [[Imipenem]] can be used in place of [[meropenem]]. For patients with severe, life-threatening beta-lactam allergy, high-dose parenteral or oral TMP-SMX represents the primary alternative.
    - **Eradication Phase Alternatives:** Oral Co-amoxiclav ([[amoxicillin]]-clavulanic acid) or oral [[Doxycycline]] are recommended only when TMP-SMX is strictly contraindicated (e.g., severe sulfa allergy or drug intolerance), but are associated with significantly higher rates of treatment failure and disease relapse.
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:**
    - **Intrinsic Resistance:** B. pseudomallei is uniformly and intrinsically resistant to penicillin, [[ampicillin]], first- and second-generation cephalosporins (e.g., [[cefazolin]], [[cefuroxime]]), macrolides (e.g., [[erythromycin]]), polymyxins ([[Polymyxin|colistin]] and [[Polymyxin|polymyxin B]]), and aminoglycosides ([[gentamicin]] and [[tobramycin]]).
    - **Acquired Resistance:** Resistance to [[Cotrimoxazole|TMP-SMX]] during therapy can occur (rates vary geographically between 2% and 16% in clinical isolates). Prolonged monotherapy or the use of second-line eradication agents carries a high risk of selection for drug-resistant mutants.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Timing Constraints:**
    - **The Synergy Exception:** The addition of oral [[Cotrimoxazole|TMP-SMX]] to the initial intensive parenteral phase ([[ceftazidime]] or [[meropenem]]) should be considered for patients with deep focal infections involving immunologically privileged or poorly accessible sites (such as brain, prostate, or joint abscesses) to enhance tissue penetration, although standard clinical trials in Thailand did not demonstrate an overall survival benefit in uncomplicated [[sepsis]].
    - **Avoid Contraindicated Drugs:** Broad-spectrum cephalosporins such as [[ceftriaxone]] or [[cefotaxime]] should NOT be used for empirical treatment of melioidosis, even if the isolate appears susceptible in vitro, because clinical outcomes are significantly worse than those obtained with [[ceftazidime]] or carbapenems.
    - **Eradication Compliance:** Non-compliance with the prolonged (minimum 3 months) oral eradication phase is the single most important cause of treatment failure and disease relapse.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:**
    - **Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP):** Indicated immediately (within 24 to 72 hours) following accidental occupational or laboratory exposure (e.g., needle-stick, splash to mucous membranes, or high-risk aerosol generation without appropriate biosafety containment). Recommended regimen is oral **[[Cotrimoxazole|TMP-SMX]]** (or oral **[[Doxycycline]]** if allergic) continued daily for 21 days.
    - **Vaccines:** There are currently no licensed vaccines available for humans; however, active research is focused on developing recombinant subunit, live attenuated, and DNA-based vaccines.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    - "Safety pin" or "hairpin" appearance on stained smears (denoting bipolar staining).
    - "Whitmore's Disease" or "Whitmore's Bacillus" (historical naming).
    - "Vietnamese time bomb" (referring to the potential for extremely long latency periods before sudden, severe reactivation).
    - "Sweet, earthy smell" (describing the characteristic odor of cultures on agar plates).
    - "Rugose / Wrinkled" colonies (denoting the typical dry, wrinkled morphology of mature cultures).
    - "Monsoon wet season" (strongly correlated with peak incidence of acute [[sepsis]] and pneumonia in endemic tropical areas).
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - **The Gram-negative Aminoglycoside/Polymyxin paradox:** Standard aerobic Gram-negative bacilli are highly susceptible to polymyxins ([[Polymyxin|colistin]]) and aminoglycosides ([[gentamicin]]), which are standard empirical agents. B. pseudomallei represents a major exception, being uniformly and intrinsically resistant to both classes.
    - **Intracellular saprophyte:** It is extremely unusual for a free-living, environmental saprophyte (found abundantly in soil and surface mud) to also be a highly adapted, virulent facultative intracellular pathogen capable of invading host [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]], escaping phagosomes, and spreading directly cell-to-cell.
    - **Beta-lactamase producer susceptible to [[Ceftazidime]]:** It produces active beta-lactamases that confer resistance to early penicillins and cephalosporins, yet it remains highly susceptible to [[ceftazidime]] (a third-generation [[cephalosporin]]) and carbapenems.
    - **The non-HIV immunodeficiency link:** Systemic opportunistic disease is strongly correlated with diabetes and chronic organ dysfunction rather than classic [[T-Cell|T-cell]] immunodeficiencies such as [[HIV]] infection.
