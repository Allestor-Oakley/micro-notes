### Salmonella typhi

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:** Possesses a typical Gram-negative cell wall. The lipopolysaccharide (LPS) consists of an outer somatic O polysaccharide, a core polysaccharide (common antigen), and lipid A. Characteristic intense staining at the ends of the bacteria ("bipolar staining") can be observed.
*   **Appendages:** Motile, possessing flagellar (H) antigens.
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:** Produces the Vi capsular polysaccharide antigen. This capsule masks the LPS, reducing the inflammatory response, and prevents opsonization and phagocytosis by the host immune system.
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:** Non-spore-forming.

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:** Facultative anaerobe.
*   **Primary Agar:** Readily grows on routine bacteriologic media. Can be isolated from blood, urine, rectal swabs, [[bone marrow]], and "rose spot" cultures.
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:** Isolation from stool specimens requires selective media. MacConkey, Hektoen enteric agar (where it appears lactose negative and H2S positive), and Bismuth sulfite agar.
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:** Fermenter and oxidase-negative. Tolerant of acids inside phagocytic vesicles.

#### Virulence Factors & Genetics
*   **Secretion Systems:** Possesses a Type III secretion system (T3SS), encoded by *Salmonella* pathogenicity islands (SPI-1 and SPI-2), which injects effector proteins to promote attachment, host cell invasion, and intracellular survival.
*   **Exotoxins:**
    *   **Toxin Name:** Enterotoxin and cytotoxin.
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** The enterotoxin activates adenylate cyclase (having some physicochemical characteristics in common with cholera toxin) and induces secretory mechanisms causing water and electrolyte transport defects. The cytotoxin contributes to tissue damage.
*   **Endotoxins & Superantigens:** The lipid A component of the LPS functions as a potent endotoxin.
*   **Enzymes:** N/A
*   **Genetic Elements:** *Salmonella* pathogenicity islands (SPI-1 and SPI-2) encode the T3SS and associated effectors. SPI-7 contains the *viaA* and *viaB* genes required for the expression of the Vi capsular polysaccharide.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:** Infection begins with the ingestion of the organism via contaminated food or water (fecal-oral route). The bacteria invade the intestinal mucosa by attaching to and passing through M cells located in Peyer patches. SPI-1 effectors facilitate host cell invasion.
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:** Facultative intracellular pathogen. After crossing the intestinal epithelium, the bacteria are engulfed by [[Macrophage|macrophages]]. They survive, multiply within these phagocytic cells, and disseminate systemically to the liver, [[spleen]], and [[bone marrow]].
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The SPI-2 T3SS allows the bacteria to evade the phagocyte NADPH oxidase, ensuring survival within the [[macrophage]]. Additionally, the Vi capsular antigen masks the bacterial LPS, preventing complement receptor 3-mediated clearance, opsonization, and phagocytosis.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Invasive non-typhoidal and typhoidal diseases are profoundly exacerbated in immunocompromised individuals (e.g., [[HIV]], [[Plasmodium sp.]], malnutrition). Patients with reduced gastric acid levels are also at a significantly higher risk for infection.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Typhoid fever (also known as enteric fever). Characterized by an insidious onset of malaise, anorexia, myalgias, headache, and a high remittent fever. It often presents generally as an invasive bacteremia without initial diarrhea.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Hepatosplenomegaly occurs in about 50% of patients. The bacteria can spread from the liver to the gallbladder and re-enter the intestines, prompting diarrheal disease later in the infection. It frequently establishes an asymptomatic long-term colonization (carrier state). Can also present with metastatic extraintestinal suppurative infections.
*   **Incubation Period:** Typically 10 to 14 days.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** "Rose spots" can appear on the skin, from which the bacteria can be directly cultured.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** The chronic carrier state is associated with a higher risk of hepatobiliary (gallbladder) cancer. Acute complications include hemolytic uremic syndrome and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Strict human pathogen; humans are the only natural reservoir.
*   **Transmission Route:** Spread person-to-person via the fecal-oral route, commonly associated with the consumption of fecally contaminated food or water, or exposure from household contacts and sewage workers.
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:** Highly endemic (>100/100,000 per annum) in parts of South Asia, South-east Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa. Occurs at low-to-medium incidence in Latin America and other parts of Asia.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Disease control relies on high hygienic standards, clean water, and the identification and treatment of asymptomatic carriers.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:** Gram stain of positive blood cultures reveals Gram-negative rods with characteristic "bipolar staining" (intense staining at the ends of the bacteria).
*   **Molecular Testing:** Multiplex nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs / PCR), Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST), and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) are used for diagnosis and tracking epidemiologic outbreaks.
*   **Serology & Immunology:** Diagnosis can be supported by serologic tests evaluating paired serum samples. Blood and bone-marrow cultures offer high sensitivity.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:** Fluoroquinolones (e.g., [[ciprofloxacin]]), third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., [[ceftriaxone]], [[cefotaxime]]), or [[azithromycin]] (20 mg/kg/day for 7 days). [[Azithromycin]] is an ideal first-line treatment for intracellular infections where [[fluoroquinolone]] resistance is established.
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:** [[Ampicillin]], [[chloramphenicol]], or [[Cotrimoxazole|trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole]] (TMP-SMZ).
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:** Antimicrobial resistance among *S.* Typhi is an emerging global problem, notably marked by increasing resistance to fluoroquinolones and cephalosporins.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Contraindications:** Antimicrobial treatment is strictly required for *S.* Typhi (unlike self-limiting enteritis caused by non-typhoidal *Salmonella* where antibiotics might prolong shedding). Carriers must be identified and aggressively treated to halt transmission.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** Two vaccines are available and recommended for travelers to endemic areas, household contacts, and sewage workers: an oral live attenuated vaccine (Ty21a) and a parenteral Vi capsular polysaccharide vaccine (Vi CPS).

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Enteric fever", "rose spots", "bipolar staining", "Vi capsular antigen", strict human pathogen, gallbladder carrier state.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike the vast majority of non-typhoidal *Salmonella* ([[Salmonella non-typhoidal|NTS]]) serovars which have a broad host range and typically cause self-limiting enterocolitis, *S.* Typhi is strictly host-restricted to humans and consistently produces a severe, invasive systemic disease rather than a localized intestinal infection.
