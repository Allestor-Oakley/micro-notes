### Shigella

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:** Possesses a typical Gram-negative cell wall. The lipopolysaccharide (LPS) consists of an outer somatic O polysaccharide, a core polysaccharide (common antigen), and lipid A. The outer membrane contains specific Ipa proteins (IpaA, IpaB, IpaC, IpaD) encoded by the virulence plasmid.
*   **Appendages:** Non-motile. They do not possess flagella or H antigens.
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:** N/A
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:** Non-spore-forming.

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:** Aerobic and facultatively anaerobic.
*   **Primary Agar:** Readily grows on non-selective bacteriologic media in pure growth.
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:** Isolation from clinical fecal specimens requires selective media such as MacConkey agar (forms pale, colorless, lactose-negative colonies), Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar, Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar (forms pink/red colonies), or Hektoen enteric agar (green/colorless colonies).
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:** Fermenter and oxidase-negative. It is generally non-lactose-fermenting (though S. sonnei can ferment lactose slowly) and does not produce hydrogen sulfide (H2S). With rare exceptions (some S. flexneri and S. boydii strains), they do not produce gas from glucose. They are lysine-decarboxylase-negative. On Triple Sugar Iron (TSI) agar slants, they produce an alkaline slant and an acid butt without gas or H2S. S. dysenteriae is mannitol-negative, while the others are mannitol-positive.

#### Virulence Factors & Genetics
*   **Secretion Systems:** Utilizes a specialized Type III secretion system to release four effector proteins (IpaA, IpaB, IpaC, and IpaD) into target epithelial cells and [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]].
*   **Exotoxins:**
    *   **Toxin Name:** Shiga toxin (Stx). Produced by S. dysenteriae type 1.
    *   **Mechanism of Action:** It is a classic A-B exotoxin. The five B subunits bind to specific globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) glycolipid receptors on the host cell (abundant on intestinal villi and renal endothelial cells). The A subunit is internalized, cleaved into two molecules, and inactivates 28S ribosomal RNA. This inhibits protein synthesis and leads to host cell death and severe endothelial damage.
*   **Endotoxins & Superantigens:** The lipid A component of the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) functions as a potent endotoxin.
*   **Enzymes:** N/A
*   **Genetic Elements:** The hallmark of virulent Shigella strains is a large extrachromosomal virulence plasmid (>200 kb) that codes for cellular invasion, although these genes are regulated by the bacterial chromosome. Genetically, DNA analysis reveals that Shigella species are technically biogroups within the species [[Escherichia coli]], though historical nomenclature is retained.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:** Infection is highly communicable and acquired via the fecal-oral route. The infectious dose is remarkably low (10 to 100 organisms). The bacteria pass through the stomach and do not attach to differentiated mucosal cells; instead, they attach to and invade M cells located in Peyer patches. The Type III secretion system injects Ipa proteins, which activate small Rho family GTPases and c-src to induce massive cytoskeletal actin rearrangement and membrane ruffling, leading to bacterial engulfment by macropinocytosis.
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:** Intracellular pathogen. After entry, the organisms quickly escape and lyse the phagocytic vacuole to replicate freely within the host cell cytoplasm.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** To evade immune clearance, the bacteria rapidly spread from cell to cell without entering the extracellular space. The bacterial surface protein IcsA initiates actin nucleation and filament assembly at one pole of the bacterium, propelling it through the cytoplasm and into adjacent epithelial cells via protrusions in a cadherin-dependent process. Shigella also survives phagocytosis by inducing caspase 1-dependent apoptosis (programmed cell death) in [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]].
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Apoptosis of infected [[Monocyte and Macrophage|macrophages]] releases potent pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-18, IL-8) that attract massive numbers of polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs). This intense inflammatory transmigration disrupts the permeability of the intestinal epithelium, thereby promoting deeper bacterial invasion and widespread mucosal necrosis.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Gastroenteritis (shigellosis) and bacillary dysentery. The disease typically begins with watery diarrhea, fever, and lethargy, which progresses within 1 to 2 days to severe lower abdominal cramps, tenesmus, and frequent small stools containing gross blood, mucus, and pus.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Extraintestinal complications can occur, including seizures, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, reactive arthritis, encephalopathy, and rarely, septicemia/bloodstream invasion.
*   **Incubation Period:** 1 to 3 days (typically 24 to 72 hours).
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** Diffuse mucosal erythema, microabscesses in the wall of the large intestine and terminal ileum, leading to necrosis, superficial ulceration, bleeding, and the formation of a "pseudomembrane" consisting of fibrin, leukocytes, cell debris, and necrotic mucosa.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) and acute renal failure, primarily associated with Shiga toxin-producing strains like S. dysenteriae type 1. Severe dehydration and death can occur, especially in malnourished pediatric patients in developing countries.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Humans are the only recognized reservoir.
*   **Transmission Route:** Person-to-person spread via the fecal-oral route (often described as "food, fingers, feces, and flies"). Sexual transmission has also been documented among men who have sex with men (MSM).
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:** Worldwide distribution with an estimated 75 to 90 million cases annually. It is a major cause of morbidity and epidemic outbreaks in crowded or resource-poor settings, day-care centers, nurseries, and custodial institutions. S. sonnei is responsible for the vast majority (up to 85%) of infections in developed/industrialized nations like the United States. S. flexneri is the predominant cause in developing countries. Epidemics of S. dysenteriae occur periodically in regions like West Africa and Central America and carry high mortality rates.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Control depends heavily on improved hygiene, sanitary management of water, food, and milk, strict sewage and fly control, proper disposal of soiled linens, isolation of infected patients with disinfection of excreta, and detection and restriction of subclinical carriers (especially food handlers).

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:** Gram stain or methylene blue stain of fecal smears frequently reveals sheets of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs) alongside Gram-negative bacilli.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Enteric multiplex nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs / PCR) are highly sensitive and specific, and are now considered the gold standard for rapid clinical detection directly from stool specimens.
*   **Serology & Immunology:** Suspect colonies isolated on culture media can be typed and confirmed by slide agglutination utilizing specific Shigella antisera. Note that Shigella species and E. coli cannot be reliably differentiated using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:** Appropriate oral or intravenous rehydration and electrolyte replacement is the most crucial intervention. Antibiotic therapy is indicated, particularly for severe cases, and empirical treatment can be initiated with fluoroquinolones (e.g., [[Ciprofloxacin]]) or [[Cotrimoxazole|trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole]].
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:** [[Ampicillin]], [[Ceftriaxone]], or [[Azithromycin]].
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:** Antimicrobial resistance is a rapidly increasing problem globally. Extremely drug-resistant (XDR) strains of Shigella—resistant to all commonly recommended empiric and alternative drugs—are emerging and caused an estimated 5% of US infections in 2022.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Contraindications:** Unlike simple gastroenteritis caused by Salmonella where antibiotics are generally withheld, antimicrobial therapy for Shigella is highly beneficial. It shortens the clinical course of symptomatic illness, curtails fecal shedding, and limits the infectivity/transmission of the disease. Therapy must be guided by local in vitro antimicrobial susceptibility testing.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** There is currently no effective vaccine available for Shigella.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Bacillary dysentery, Shiga toxin, M cell invasion, cell-to-cell spread via actin tails, extremely low infectious dose, bloody/mucoid stools, "food, fingers, feces, and flies".
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Despite distinct clinical presentations and historical classifications as four separate species, DNA analysis firmly positions all Shigella as biogroups within the species [[Escherichia coli]]. Furthermore, while most enteric pathogens require massive infectious doses (10^5-10^8 organisms) to survive gastric acid, Shigella requires an exceptionally low inoculum (10-100 organisms) to cause severe disease.
