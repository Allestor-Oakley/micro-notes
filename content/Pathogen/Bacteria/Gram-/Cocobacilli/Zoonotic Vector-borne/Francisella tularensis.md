### Francisella tularensis

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:** Possesses a typical Gram-negative cell wall and a thin lipid capsule.
*   **Appendages:** Nonmotile.
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:** Produces an antiphagocytic thin lipid capsule.
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:** Non-spore-forming.

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:** Strict aerobe. Extremely fastidious and slow-growing, requiring 3 or more days (up to 2-5 days) of incubation in a CO2-enriched atmosphere at 35-37°C before growth is visually detected.
*   **Primary Agar:** Will not grow reliably on common laboratory media. Requires sulfhydryl-containing substances (e.g., cysteine) for growth. Cysteine-supplemented media such as chocolate agar, buffered charcoal yeast extract (BCYE) agar, or historically, glucose-cysteine-blood agar, are required.
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:** BCYE agar or modified Thayer-Martin agar can be used with antibiotics to selectively suppress the more rapidly growing normal flora from respiratory specimens.
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:** Does not ferment carbohydrates.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:** Acquired via the bite of an infected arthropod (e.g., hard ticks, deerflies), direct contact with infected animal tissue (e.g., skinning rabbits, cat bites), inhalation of infectious aerosols, or ingestion of contaminated food and water.
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:** Obligate intracellular pathogen capable of infecting and replicating within macrophages, neutrophils, epithelial cells, and endothelial cells.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** The thin lipid capsule is antiphagocytic. Once engulfed, the bacterium secretes proteins that prevent phagolysosome fusion, allowing the bacteria to escape from the phagosome and replicate freely within the host cell cytosol. It is highly resistant to killing in serum and by phagocytes.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Clearance requires protective cellular immunity (inactivated vaccines fail to elicit this, rendering them ineffective). Opportunistic species (e.g., F. novicida and F. philomiragia) have a strong predilection for causing disease in patients with immunologic deficiencies, such as chronic granulomatous disease or myeloproliferative diseases.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:** Tularemia (also known as rabbit fever, tick fever, glandular fever, or deerfly fever). The clinical presentation depends heavily on the route of exposure:
    *   *Ulceroglandular:* A painful papule develops at the site of inoculation, progressing to an ulcer with localized lymphadenopathy.
    *   *Oculoglandular:* Painful conjunctivitis with regional (cervical) lymphadenopathy following direct inoculation into the eye.
    *   *Glandular:* Primarily swollen lymph nodes with no obvious localized skin lesions.
    *   *Typhoidal:* Systemic signs of sepsis.
    *   *Pneumonic:* Pneumonitis with signs of sepsis developing rapidly after aerosol exposure.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Oropharyngeal and gastrointestinal tularemia can occur following ingestion of the bacteria. Disseminated disease is especially prominent with Type A-east strains.
*   **Incubation Period:** Rapid; clinical case studies describe symptoms developing 3 days after exposure, but it can be highly variable depending on the infectious dose and route.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** A painful, ulcerated skin lesion accompanied by massive regional lymphadenopathy, especially following a recent tick bite or exposure to wild rabbits.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Pneumonic and typhoidal tularemia carry a high mortality rate unless promptly diagnosed and aggressively treated. Type A-east strain infections are generally associated with higher dissemination and mortality rates.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Wild mammals, domestic animals, birds, fish, and blood-sucking arthropods. Type A strains are heavily associated with lagomorphs (rabbits, hares) and cats. Type B strains are associated with rodents and cats, but rarely lagomorphs.
*   **Transmission Route:** Highly infectious zoonosis. Transmitted via the bite of infected hard ticks (Ixodes, Dermacentor, Amblyomma) or deerflies, direct contact with tissues from infected animals, inhalation of aerosols, or ingestion. The infectious dose is extremely small for arthropod, cutaneous, and inhalation exposures, but large numbers must be ingested to cause gastrointestinal infection. It is not spread person-to-person.
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:** Widespread throughout the Northern Hemisphere. Type A strains are strictly found in North America. Type A-east strains expanded along the Atlantic Coast after infected rabbits were transported to East Coast hunting clubs in the early 20th century.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Classified as a Tier 1 Select Agent and a major potential agent of bioterrorism due to its high infectivity via the aerosol route. Extreme caution must be taken in clinical laboratories; working with live cultures requires strict Biosafety Level III (BSL III) practices to avoid laboratory-acquired infections.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:** Direct microscopy (Gram stain) of clinical aspirates or tissue is highly insensitive and almost always unsuccessful because the organisms are extremely small and stain very faintly.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs / PCR) are sensitive and specific but are largely restricted to specialized research or reference laboratories.
*   **Serology & Immunology:** Serology is the standard for clinical diagnosis. Tubed agglutination or microagglutination testing is used. A fourfold rise in paired sera collected 2 weeks apart, or a single tubed titer >1:160 (microagglutination >1:128) is highly suggestive. Antibodies can cross-react with Brucella, so both should be tested; the titer for the true infecting agent is typically fourfold higher.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:** Gentamicin is the antibiotic of choice. Streptomycin is also highly effective when administered for 10 days.
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:** Doxycycline or fluoroquinolones (e.g., ciprofloxacin) can be used to treat milder infections.
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:** Inherently resistant to all beta-lactam antibiotics (penicillins and cephalosporins) due to the production of beta-lactamase.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Contraindications:** The microbiology laboratory must be explicitly notified if tularemia is suspected so that media can be supplemented with cysteine, cultures can be held for extended incubation, and BSL III precautions can be enacted. Tetracycline therapy is associated with a higher frequency of clinical relapse compared to aminoglycosides.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** Persons with a high-risk exposure (e.g., infectious aerosols) should receive prophylactic antibiotics. A live attenuated vaccine (LVS) exists but is rarely used and no longer available to high-risk individuals. Prevention relies heavily on wearing protective clothing, promptly removing ticks, and wearing gloves when skinning or eviscerating animals.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Rabbit fever", "deerfly fever", cysteine-requiring, BCYE agar, ulceroglandular, Tier 1 select agent, tiny faintly-staining coccobacillus.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike most intracellular bacteria that modify and remain inside the phagolysosome, F. tularensis completely escapes the phagosome to replicate freely in the host cell's cytoplasm. Furthermore, unlike most Gram-negative rods, it will not grow on standard blood or MacConkey agars without exogenous sulfhydryl (cysteine) supplementation.
