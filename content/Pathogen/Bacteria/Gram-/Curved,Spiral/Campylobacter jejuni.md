### Campylobacter jejuni

#### Molecular Microbiology & Morphology
*   **Cell Wall / Membrane Features:** Possesses a Gram-negative cell wall structure. Instead of cell wall lipopolysaccharides (LPS) with typical endotoxin activity found in other Gram-negative bacteria, it expresses lipooligosaccharides.
*   **Appendages:** Polar flagella that provide rapid, darting motility.
*   **Capsule Composition & Structure:** Encapsulated with an outer polysaccharide capsule. The capsular polysaccharides contribute to the virulence of the bacteria, share antigenic cross-reactivity with host neural tissues, and are the targets of vaccine development.
*   **Spore Characteristics & Germination:** Non-spore-forming.

#### Growth & Biochemical Profile
*   **Optimal Growth Conditions:** Microaerophilic (requires 5% to 7% oxygen and 5% to 10% carbon dioxide). Thermophilic, growing optimally at an elevated incubation temperature of 42°C (which helps inhibit gut commensals). Slow-growing, usually requiring incubation for 48 hours or longer.
*   **Primary Agar:** Columbia Blood Agar base, Butzler’s medium, and Preston medium. A blood-free medium containing charcoal, cefoperazone, and amphotericin B is also used. The selective media must contain blood or charcoal to remove toxic oxygen radicals.
*   **Selective / Enriched Agar:** Selective media containing antibiotics (such as [[trimethoprim]]) are generally used to recover the organisms from stool by suppressing nonpathogenic enteric bacteria.
*   **Metabolic Profile & Biochemical Testing:** Oxidase-positive and catalase-positive. Characterized by an inability to ferment or oxidize carbohydrates.

#### Pathogenesis & Host Immune Evasion
*   **Entry & Attachment Mechanism:** The infection is acquired by the oral route. The organism is susceptible to gastric acid, so a relatively high inoculum (estimated between 500 colony-forming units up to 10,000 organisms) is usually necessary to produce infection unless gastric acids are neutralized or absent. Factors that regulate adhesion and motility allow the organisms to multiply in the small intestine, invade the intestinal epithelium, and produce inflammation that results in the appearance of red and white blood cells in the stools.
*   **Intracellular vs Extracellular Survival:** Extracellular, but capable of localized host cell invasion into the intestinal mucosa.
*   **Immune Evasion Strategies:** Antigenic cross-reactivity between oligosaccharides in the bacterial capsule and glycosphingolipids on the surface of host neural tissues is believed to induce an autoimmune response, leading to Guillain-Barré syndrome.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Gastric acid acts as a primary innate barrier. Immunocompromised patients (e.g., patients with AIDS) are at risk for chronic enteric infections.

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Diseases:**
    *   **Gastroenteritis (Enteritis):** Acute onset of crampy abdominal pain, colicky lower abdominal pain, profuse watery diarrhea that may be grossly bloody, headache, malaise, myalgia, and fever. It can mimic acute appendicitis. Usually self-limited.
    *   **Systemic Infection:** Occasionally, the bloodstream is invaded, and a clinical picture of enteric fever or septicemia develops.
*   **Incubation Period:** 2 to 10 days (typically 2 to 5 days).
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:** "Gull's wing" appearance of the bacteria on Gram stain.
*   **Complications & Sequelae:** Guillain-Barré syndrome (an autoimmune ascending paralytic disease), reactive arthritis, Reiter’s syndrome, and chronic enteric infections.

#### Epidemiology & Transmission
*   **Reservoir:** Zoonotic. Commensal bacteria in the gastrointestinal tracts of wild and domesticated animals; common reservoir hosts include poultry, cattle, sheep, and birds.
*   **Transmission Route:** Ingestion of contaminated food (particularly improperly prepared/undercooked commercial poultry), unpasteurized (raw) milk, or contaminated water. Fecal-oral person-to-person transmission can occur (especially among young children in nurseries) but is less frequent.
*   **Endemic Regions & Outbreaks:** Worldwide. It is the most common cause of bacterial gastroenteritis in both developed and developing countries, including the United States (estimated 2 million cases per year). No strict seasonal limitations, though bacterial levels in contaminated waters increase during warm months.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Outbreaks are often linked to slaughterhouse procedures that amplify contamination of poultry meats. Prevention depends on proper food preparation (completely cooking poultry), preventing cross-contamination of other foods with uncooked poultry products, consuming pasteurized milk, and safeguarding water supplies.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Visualization & Stains:** Microscopic detection (Gram stain or dark-field microscopy of fecal smears) can provide a rapid presumptive diagnosis but is highly insensitive because the bacteria are thin and at the resolving limit of light microscopy. When visible, they appear as "gull wing-shaped" Gram-negative rods with polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs) and red blood cells.
*   **Molecular Testing:** Commercial multiplex nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) are the gold standard diagnostic tool and are highly sensitive and specific.
*   **Serology & Immunology:** Serologic testing for IgM and IgG is useful for epidemiologic surveys but not for individual patient diagnosis.
*   **Diagnostic Nuances:** Culture requires highly specialized microaerophilic environments, elevated temperature (42°C), and extended incubation (48+ hours). Furthermore, culture plates and swabs must be kept out of the light prior to use because the bacteria are rapidly killed by free radicals generated by ultraviolet irradiation. The organism's extremely small size allows it to pass through 0.45-µm filters (which retain other bacteria), a physical property historically used to isolate it from stool. Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization (MALDI) mass spectrometry is used for definitive species identification.

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **First-Line Treatment:** Most cases resolve spontaneously and are managed with fluid and electrolyte replacement. For severe enteritis, septicemia, or to shorten the duration of fecal shedding, macrolides (e.g., [[erythromycin]], [[azithromycin]], [[clarithromycin]]) are the antibiotics of choice.
*   **Alternative / Second-Line Treatment:** Tetracyclines or fluoroquinolones (e.g., [[ciprofloxacin]]). [[Imipenem]], aminoglycosides, or [[chloramphenicol]] can be used for systemic infections. [[Amoxicillin]]/clavulanic acid can be used in place of [[tetracycline]], which is contraindicated in young children.
*   **Mechanisms of Resistance & Resistance Genes:** Most isolates are naturally resistant to penicillins, cephalosporins, and sulfonamide antibiotics. Resistance to fluoroquinolones has increased, rendering them less effective in some regions.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Contraindications:** Antibiotic treatment is reserved for severe infections to reduce the bacterial burden, exotoxin production, and duration of diarrhea; routine empirical treatment is often not necessary.
*   **Prophylaxis & Vaccine Protocols:** No human vaccines are currently available. Experimental vaccines targeting the outer capsular polysaccharides are promising for controlling infections in animal reservoirs.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Gull's wing appearance", "microaerophilic", "growth at 42°C", "undercooked poultry", "Guillain-Barré syndrome", "thin comma-shaped rods".
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Unlike most typical Gram-negative enteric rods (like Enterobacteriaceae), it does not possess standard endotoxic lipopolysaccharide (LPS), but rather lipooligosaccharide; it cannot ferment or oxidize carbohydrates; and it requires a unique elevated temperature (42°C) and microaerophilic atmosphere for isolation.
