## Aspergillus flavus

### Mycology & Structural Characteristics
*   **Cell Wall Composition & Membrane:** The fungal cell wall contains 1,3-beta-D-glucan, chitin, and galactomannan. The fungal cell membrane contains ergosterol as its major sterol component.
*   **Thermal Dimorphism & Environmental Triggers:** *Aspergillus* species are not thermally dimorphic; they are monomorphic and exist exclusively as a mold both in the environment and in host tissues.
*   **Microscopic Morphology (Yeast Form):** N/A.
*   **Microscopic Morphology (Mold / Hyphal Form):** Characterized by septate, dichotomously branched hyaline hyphae of uniform width (3-6 µm) that typically branch at acute (approximately 45-degree) angles. *Aspergillus* produces complex conidial heads consisting of a long conidiophore terminating in a vesicle, which bears one or two layers of flask-shaped phialides (sterigmata) that produce basipetal chains of spheric conidia.
*   **Macroscopic & Colony Morphology:** *Aspergillus flavus* forms rapidly growing colonies with aerial hyphae that exhibit a characteristic yellow-green color on routine culture media.

### Virulence Factors & Pathogenesis
*   **Enzymes & Proteases:** Secretes various degradative enzymes, including elastase, phospholipases, acid proteases, and catalase, which facilitate host cell invasion, connective tissue degradation, and angioinvasion leading to thrombosis and infarction.
*   **Host Immune Evasion:** Inhaled conidia bind to human fibrinogen and laminin in the alveolar basement membrane to establish tissue residence. The fungus utilizes melanin and superoxide dismutase to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), impairing [[macrophage]] killing capabilities. Its secretion of catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide, allowing it to evade the oxidative burst of host neutrophils. *Aspergillus* species also form biofilms that act as mechanical barriers against host immune responses and antifungal therapies.
*   **Thermal Tolerance Mechanisms:** Readily proliferates at human body temperature (37°C), a requirement for systemic pathogenesis.
*   **Toxins:** *A. flavus* produces aflatoxins as a secondary metabolite (mycotoxin), particularly on contaminated peanuts and cereal grains; ingestion causes acute and chronic hepatitis, liver failure, and hepatic cancer. *Aspergillus* species may also secrete gliotoxin, which inhibits [[macrophage]] phagocytosis and [[T Cell|T-cell]] activation/proliferation.
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** Innate host defenses rely heavily on alveolar [[Macrophage|macrophages]] to phagocytize and kill conidia, while neutrophils adhere to and kill germinating hyphae. Severe invasive infections are tightly linked to profound immunodeficiencies such as severe or prolonged neutropenia (e.g., from cytotoxic chemotherapy), chronic granulomatous disease, stem cell or solid organ transplantation, and prolonged high-dose corticosteroid therapy.

### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Infection:** Inhalation of conidia by hypersensitized or atopic individuals (such as those with asthma or cystic fibrosis) can trigger allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA), allergic sinusitis, or lower respiratory tract allergic reactions. Invasive pulmonary aspergillosis is the most common frankly invasive form in severely immunodeficient patients.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** Can colonize obstructed paranasal sinuses or pre-existing pulmonary cavities (caused by tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, or emphysema) to form an aspergilloma (fungus ball), which is often asymptomatic but can cause severe hemoptysis. It may also cause localized superficial infections such as mycotic keratitis (corneal infection), endophthalmitis, otitis externa, or infections at wounds and catheter sites.
*   **Disseminated Disease:** Invasive aspergillosis is highly angioinvasive, leading to thrombosis, hemorrhage, and localized tissue infarction. Hematogenous dissemination frequently spreads from the lungs to the central nervous system (brain abscesses), heart (endocarditis), kidneys, liver, [[spleen]], and gastrointestinal tract, leading to a high mortality rate.
*   **Opportunistic Presentations:** Chronic necrotizing pulmonary aspergillosis and chronic fibrosing aspergillosis may occur in patients with underlying structural pulmonary disease or mild immunosuppression. It can also present as multiple purpuric or necrotic skin nodules in profoundly immunosuppressed patients.
*   **Pathognomonic Lesions:** Radiographic imaging may show a fungus ball within a cavity or lung nodule. Histopathologic examination demonstrating uniform, 45-degree dichotomously branching septate hyphae invading tissue planes and blood vessels (angioinvasion) is characteristic.

### Epidemiology & Exposures
*   **Geographic Endemicity:** Ubiquitous and globally distributed.
*   **Environmental Reservoir:** Environmental saprobe found abundantly in soil, air, water, decaying vegetation, pepper, and construction or demolition sites. Highly prevalent in the hospital environment, contaminating unfiltered air, showerheads, water storage tanks, and potted plants.
*   **High-Risk Activities & Occupations:** Consumption of aflatoxin-contaminated peanuts or cereal grains. Exposure to dusty environments, construction, and demolition sites.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Transmission occurs predominantly via the inhalation of airborne conidia; infection is not communicable from person to person. Prevention relies heavily on isolating highly susceptible patients in rooms with high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters to minimize exposure to aerosolized conidia.

### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Microscopic Visualization & Special Stains:** Hyphae stain poorly with routine Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) but are excellently visualized in tissues using Gomori Methenamine Silver (GMS), Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS), and Gridley fungal stains.
*   **Wet Mounts:** Can be visualized in sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid, or tissue scrapings using potassium hydroxide (KOH) and calcofluor white.
*   **Culture Media & Growth Speed:** Grows readily within a few days on routine mycologic media (such as potato dextrose agar) at room temperature. However, media containing cycloheximide must be avoided, as it inhibits the growth of *Aspergillus* species.
*   **Serology & Antigen Testing:** Invasive disease is frequently diagnosed via an enzyme immunoassay (EIA) detecting the circulating cell wall galactomannan antigen in serum, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid, and cerebrospinal fluid. The 1,3-beta-D-glucan (Fungitell) limulus lysate assay is also positive but lacks genus specificity. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays for *Aspergillus* DNA are sensitive and specific, especially when combined with antigen testing.

### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Targeting Membrane / Ergosterol:** [[Voriconazole]] is the treatment of first choice for invasive aspergillosis and is superior to [[Amphotericin B]] for survival. [[Isavuconazole]] and [[Posaconazole]] are effective alternatives. Lipid formulations of [[Amphotericin B]] (a polyene) may be used if azoles fail or are contraindicated; however, breakthrough resistance has been noted in some leukemia centers for *A. flavus* (and intrinsically in *A. terreus*). [[Itraconazole]] is considered a second-line agent for invasive disease.
*   **Targeting Cell Wall / Glucan:** The [[Echinocandin]] (caspofungin, micafungin, and anidulafungin) inhibit 1,3-beta-D-glucan synthase and exhibit fungistatic activity against *Aspergillus*. They are approved for refractory cases or in patients intolerant of standard therapies, and are often used as salvage therapy.
*   **Alternative / Cutaneous Treatments:** Surgical resection or debridement is recommended for aspergillomas causing severe hemoptysis, or for localized refractory lesions in the sinuses or lungs. Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) is managed with corticosteroids and/or long-term azole therapy.
*   **Treatment Nuances & Formulations:** Reversal of underlying immunosuppression (e.g., resolving neutropenia) is a crucial component of successful management. Combination therapy (e.g., triazoles plus [[Echinocandin]]) has shown in vivo benefit in animal models, but robust clinical data proving superiority over monotherapy in humans are currently limited.
*   **Prophylaxis & Prevention:** Primary prophylaxis with [[Posaconazole]] is recommended to improve survival and decrease the incidence of invasive mycoses in high-risk patients, such as those with prolonged neutropenia, acute myelogenous leukemia, or stem cell transplant recipients with graft-versus-host disease (GVHD).

### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Fungus ball" (aspergilloma), 45-degree acute-angle dichotomous branching, "halo sign" surrounding lung nodules, aflatoxicosis (contaminated peanuts/corn), "sick building syndrome".
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** Despite the fungus causing severe hematogenously disseminated angioinvasive disease, blood cultures are virtually always negative in invasive *Aspergillus* infections (a stark contrast to *Fusarium* or *A. terreus* adventitious sporulation). Furthermore, routine mycologic media containing cycloheximide will actively inhibit *Aspergillus* growth and should be avoided for diagnostic culture.
