### Microsporum audouinii

#### Mycology & Structural Characteristics
*   **Cell Wall Composition & Membrane:** The fungal cell wall contains chitin and glucans. The cell membrane contains ergosterol as its major sterol component.
*   **Thermal Dimorphism & Environmental Triggers:** Not thermally dimorphic; exists exclusively as a filamentous mold. Growth is naturally inhibited by human body temperature (37°C) and serum, which restricts the fungus to the nonviable, outermost keratinized layers of the host.
*   **Microscopic Morphology (Yeast Form):** N/A
*   **Microscopic Morphology (Mold / Hyphal Form):** Characterized by septate, filamentous hyphae. Invades hair shafts using an "ectothrix" pattern, meaning the fungus grows on the outside surface of the hair shaft. Uniquely within its genus, it rarely produces the numerous, large, thick, and rough-walled macroconidia or the microconidia that are characteristic of most other *Microsporum* species.
*   **Macroscopic & Colony Morphology:** N/A

#### Virulence Factors & Pathogenesis
*   **Enzymes & Proteases:** Highly keratinophilic and keratinolytic; produces keratinases and elastases capable of breaking down the keratin surfaces of the skin and hair.
*   **Host Immune Evasion:** Evades deep host immune responses by remaining strictly confined to the nonviable stratum corneum of the epidermis and the keratinized layers of the hair shaft. As an anthropophilic dermatophyte, it has adapted to the human host and elicits a relatively chronic, noninflammatory host immune response.
*   **Thermal Tolerance Mechanisms:** N/A (Growth is actively inhibited by core body temperature and serum, preventing deep tissue invasion).
*   **Toxins:** N/A
*   **Host Defense & Immunodeficiency Links:** N/A

#### Clinical Syndromes
*   **Primary Infection:** Tinea capitis (ringworm of the scalp, eyebrows, and eyelashes). Presents clinically as circular bald patches with short hair stubs or broken hair within hair follicles.
*   **Secondary, Atypical & Alternate Presentations:** N/A
*   **Disseminated Disease:** True deep dissemination is extremely rare; penetration below the granular layer of the epidermis into deeper subcutaneous tissues or blood does not typically occur.
*   **Opportunistic Presentations:** N/A
*   **Pathognomonic Lesions:** Circular scaling patches with central clearing and hair loss. Microscopic examination of infected hairs reveals an ectothrix pattern (spores coating the outside of the hair shaft).

#### Epidemiology & Exposures
*   **Geographic Endemicity:** Ubiquitous and globally distributed worldwide.
*   **Environmental Reservoir:** Strictly anthropophilic (infects humans); the primary reservoir is infected humans.
*   **High-Risk Activities & Occupations:** Close contact with infected individuals or sharing contaminated fomites. School children are at uniquely high risk.
*   **Public Health & Isolation:** Highly contagious and may assume epidemic proportions in selected settings (e.g., epidemics of tinea capitis among school children). Transmission occurs directly from person to person or indirectly through exposure to shed skin scales and hair containing viable hyphae or conidia, which can persist for long periods on clothing, hats, combs, brushes, and towels.

#### Advanced Laboratory Diagnostics
*   **Direct Microscopic Visualization & Special Stains:** Infected hairs fluoresce when examined under an ultraviolet Wood lamp. Fungal elements can also be seen in tissue biopsies using standard Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), Gomori Methenamine Silver (GMS), and Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) stains.
*   **Wet Mounts:** Microscopic examination of plucked hairs or skin scrapings mounted in a 10% to 20% potassium hydroxide (KOH) preparation, often supplemented with calcofluor white, allows for the visualization of branching, septate hyphae and ectothrix hair invasion via fluorescence.
*   **Culture Media & Growth Speed:** Cultured on routine mycologic media. Media are frequently supplemented with antibacterial antibiotics and cycloheximide to inhibit the growth of contaminating bacteria and rapid-growing saprophytic environmental molds. Special dermatophyte test media can be used to assess the nutritional requirements of the fungus.
*   **Serology & Antigen Testing:** N/A

#### Pharmacology, Prevention & Prophylaxis
*   **Targeting Membrane / Ergosterol:** Localized dermatophyte infections of the smooth skin (if present) can be treated topically with azoles, [[terbinafine]], or haloprogin. However, infections involving the hair follicles (tinea capitis) require systemic oral antifungal therapy to reach the infection site. Effective oral agents include [[terbinafine]], [[itraconazole]], [[fluconazole]], and [[griseofulvin]].
*   **Targeting Cell Wall / Glucan:** N/A
*   **Alternative / Cutaneous Treatments:** N/A
*   **Treatment Nuances & Formulations:** Because *M. audouinii* is an anthropophilic dermatophyte, it tends to cause chronic, relatively noninflammatory infections that are notoriously more difficult to cure than the acute, highly inflammatory infections caused by geophilic or zoophilic dermatophytes.
*   **Prophylaxis & Prevention:** Good personal hygiene and avoiding the sharing of potentially contaminated clothing, hats, or hair grooming tools.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Tinea capitis epidemics in school children, anthropophilic, ectothrix hair invasion, fluoresces under Wood lamp, keratinolytic.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:** While the genus *Microsporum* is canonically defined by the production of numerous, large, thick, and rough-walled macroconidia, *Microsporum audouinii* is a morphologic exception and essentially lacks macroconidia (they are extremely rare). It also lacks the numerous microconidia typical of many dermatophytes.
