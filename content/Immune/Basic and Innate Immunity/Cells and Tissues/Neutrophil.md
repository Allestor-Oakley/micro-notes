### Neutrophil (PMN)
#### Origin & Maturation
*   **Lineage & Precursors:** Neutrophils arise from hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) in the bone marrow. During hematopoiesis, HSCs differentiate into common myeloid-megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors (specifically committed progenitors with myeloid potential). Under the influence of colony-stimulating factors, these progenitors differentiate along the granulocyte lineage starting with committed myeloblast precursors (colony-forming unit granulocyte, CFU-G), which ultimately mature into neutrophilic granulocytes. Granulopoiesis and neutrophil production are stimulated primarily by granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF). An adult human produces more than 1 x 10^11 neutrophils per day under steady-state conditions.
*   **Site of Development & Maturation:** Adult bone marrow (within the spongelike reticular framework of red marrow of flat bones such as the sternum, vertebrae, ribs, and iliac bones). Fetal hematopoiesis initially occurs in the yolk sac blood islands, Para-aortic mesenchyme, and fetal liver before transferring exclusively to the bone marrow.
*   **Key Transcription Factors for Differentiation:** N/A (The sources do not specify any master transcriptional regulators unique to neutrophil differentiation, unlike lymphoid lineages or other myeloid subsets. Their lineage development is documented primarily as cytokine-driven by G-CSF and GM-CSF).
*   **Selection & Tolerance Mechanisms:** Central tolerance selection (clonal deletion or receptor editing) does not apply to neutrophils as they do not express somatic-recombinase-generated antigen receptors. Instead, self-tolerance is maintained non-clonally by the expression of inhibitory receptors (ITIM-containing molecules) that recognize healthy host cell structures and transmit inhibitory signals. Neutrophils also express CD47, which acts as a "don't eat me" signal to protect healthy host cells. In general, healthy host cells are not recognized, or they express molecules that actively prevent innate immune activation.
*   **Circulation & Extravasation Dynamics:** Neutrophils are the most abundant circulating white blood cells (comprising 40% to 60% of total leukocytes). They circulate as spherical cells (12 to 15 μm in diameter) in the blood for a few hours up to 5 days. Following tissue infection or injury, neutrophils are the first leukocytes recruited to sites of inflammation, typically dominant within the first 24 to 48 hours. Extravasation occurs through postcapillary venules via a highly regulated, multistep adhesion cascade:
    1. *Margination and Rolling:* TNF and IL-1 (secreted by sentinel tissue macrophages/DCs) activate endothelial cells to express E-selectin. Histamine (released from mast cells) or thrombin triggers the rapid translocation of P-selectin from endothelial cell granules (Weibel-Palade bodies) to the cell surface. Sialyl Lewis X carbohydrate ligands on neutrophil surface molecules (such as PSGL-1/CD162) bind E- and P-selectins with low affinity, slowing blood-borne neutrophils and forcing them to roll along the endothelium.
    2. *Integrin Activation by Chemokines:* CXCL8 (IL-8), produced abundantly by tissue sentinel cells, binds to G protein-coupled chemokine receptors (CXCR1 and CXCR2) on rolling neutrophils. This GPCR signaling activates leukocyte integrins, transforming them from a low-affinity, bent conformation into a high-affinity, extended conformation.
    3. *Firm Adhesion:* High-affinity integrins on neutrophils, mainly leukocyte function-associated antigen 1 (LFA-1; CD11a/CD18) and Mac-1 (CD11b/CD18), bind firmly to intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and ICAM-2 on the activated endothelial cells. This firm attachment induces cytoskeletal reorganization and cell flattening.
    4. *Transmigration (Diapedesis):* Neutrophils migrate out of blood vessels primarily between endothelial cells (paracellular transmigration). This step depends on homophilic CD31 (PECAM-1) interactions and a transient, reversible disruption of endothelial adherens junctions (principally the VE-cadherin complex), triggered by kinases activated when integrins bind ICAM-1.

#### Receptors & Surface Markers
*   **Defining CD Markers:** CD11b (Mac-1 α chain), CD18 (integrin β2 chain common to LFA-1, Mac-1, and CR4), CD15, CD66b, CD16b (GPI-linked low-affinity FcγRIIIB), and CD10 (neprilysin).
*   **Antigen Recognition Receptors:** Neutrophils express cell-associated pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) to recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). These include cell surface Toll-like receptors (TLRs 1, 2, 4, 5, 6) and endosomal TLRs (TLR3, 7, 8, 9), as well as cytosolic NOD-like receptors (NLRs like NOD1 and NOD2). To recognize opsonized pathogens, they express complement receptors (CR1/CD35, which binds C3b/C4b; CR3/Mac-1, which binds iC3b; and CR4/CD11cCD18, which binds iC3b) and IgG-specific Fc receptors (high-affinity FcγRI/CD64; and low-affinity FcγRIIA/CD32, FcγRIIC/CD32, and FcγRIIIB/CD16b). They also express C-type lectin-like receptors (e.g., Dectin-1 for fungal β-glucans) and scavenger receptors.
*   **Co-stimulatory & Inhibitory Receptors:** Express CD47 ("don't eat me" marker) and inhibitory receptors containing immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibition motifs (ITIMs) in their cytoplasmic domains. ITIM phosphorylation recruits tyrosine phosphatases (like SHP-1 and SHP-2) that remove phosphate groups from activating signaling intermediates, attenuating cell activation.
*   **Cytokine & Chemokine Receptors:** CXCR1 and CXCR2 (specific GPCRs for CXCL8/IL-8), CD88 (C5a complement fragment receptor GPCR), C3a receptor, IL-1 receptor (IL-1R1), TNF receptors (TNFRI/CD120a and TNFRII/CD120b), IL-18R, and the G-CSF receptor (CD114).
*   **Tissue Homing & Adhesion Molecules (Integrins/Selectins):** LFA-1 (CD11a/CD18), Mac-1 (CD11b/CD18), CR4 (CD11c/CD18), CD29 (β1 integrin), CD49d (VLA-4), CD162 (PSGL-1), and CD31 (PECAM-1).

#### Activation & Differentiation
*   **Primary Activation Signals (Signal 1, 2, 3):**
    - *Signal 1 (Antigen / Opsonin Binding):* Direct binding of microbial structures to PRRs, or binding of opsonized microbes to Fcγ receptors (FcγRI, FcγRII) and complement receptors (CR1, CR3).
    - *Signal 2 (GPCR Chemoattractant Signaling):* Binding of CXCL8 (IL-8), bacterial formyl peptides, or complement anaphylatoxins (C5a, C3a) to GPCRs.
    - *Signal 3 (Inflammatory Cytokines):* Binding of local sentinel-derived cytokines, such as TNF, IL-1, or IFN-γ, to their receptors.
*   **Signal Transduction Cascades:**
    - *PRR/TLR and IL-1R Signaling:* TIR domains recruit MyD88, IRAK4, and IRAK1, initiating a signaling cascade that activates the IκB kinase (IKK) complex. IKKβ phosphorylates the inhibitor IκBα, targeting it for polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby allowing the active NF-κB transcription factor (typically p50/RELA heterodimers) to translocate into the nucleus. MAP kinase pathways are also activated, generating the AP-1 transcription factor.
    - *FcγR Activation:* Receptor clustering by IgG-coated particles activates Src-family tyrosine kinases (e.g., LYN, FYN, BLK), which phosphorylate cytoplasmic ITAMs on receptor tails or the associated common FcR γ chain. This recruits and activates Syk kinase, leading to tyrosine phosphorylation of adaptor proteins like SLP-65 and the subsequent recruitment of phospholipase C γ (PLCγ) and PI3K/Akt survival signaling.
    - *Anaphylatoxin/Chemokine GPCR Signaling:* Ligand binding to seven-transmembrane GPCRs (like CXCR1/2 or CD88) induces exchange of GDP for GTP on associated heterotrimeric G-proteins. This activates PLCβ, which hydrolyzes membrane PIP2 into IP3 and DAG. IP3 triggers calcium release from the endoplasmic reticulum, and the calcium sensor STIM1 opens membrane ORAI channels (CRAC channels) for extracellular calcium influx. Elevated cytosolic calcium activates the calmodulin-dependent phosphatase calcineurin, which dephosphorylates cytoplasmic NFAT, causing it to translocate to the nucleus. DAG activates protein kinase C (PKC), which contributes to NF-κB activation and cytoskeletal rearrangements.
*   **Polarization & Subsets:** Neutrophils do not polarize into stably differentiated helper T-cell-like subsets. They exit the bone marrow as a single, fully mature, terminally differentiated effector population.

#### Effector Functions & Secretory Profile
*   **Primary Effector Mechanisms:**
    - *Phagocytosis and Degranulation:* Microbes opsonized with IgG or complement fragments are rapidly bound and internalized into phagosomes. Fusion with lysosomes forms phagolysosomes. Neutrophil degranulation is rapidly induced by cytoskeletal rearrangements, releasing specific granules (containing lysozyme, collagenase, and elastase) and azurophilic granules (containing myeloperoxidase, defensins, and cathelicidins) directly into the phagolysosome.
    - *Respiratory Burst (ROS Generation):* Activation stimulates the assembly of the multisubunit phagocyte NADPH oxidase (phagocyte oxidase) in the phagolysosomal membrane. This enzyme reduces molecular oxygen into superoxide free radicals. Superoxide is dismutated into hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which myeloperoxidase (MPO) uses to convert halide ions (like chloride) into highly toxic hypohalous acids (like hypochlorous acid/bleach), destroying bacteria and fungi.
    - *Nitric Oxide (NO) Generation:* Phagocyte activation upregulates inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), converting arginine to NO. In phagolysosomes, NO reacts with superoxide to generate highly reactive peroxynitrite radicals that damage microbial lipids, proteins, and DNA.
    - *Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs):* Neutrophils extrude nuclear chromatin threads associated with histones and high concentrations of antimicrobial granule proteins (elastase, MPO, defensins) to trap and kill extracellular bacteria and fungi. Histone citrullination by peptidylarginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) and serine proteases decompresses the chromatin. This active lytic process typically results in neutrophil cell death (NETosis).
*   **Key Cytokines Secreted:** While neutrophils are not major cytokine producers on a per-cell basis, their sheer abundance in acute inflammation leads to significant cumulative production of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF, IL-1β, IL-6) and chemokines (CXCL8) to recruit more leukocytes and amplify the inflammatory response.
*   **Target Cells & Pathogens:** Extracellular bacteria (especially pyogenic, or pus-forming, species) and fungi (such. as Aspergillus fumigatus), as well as damaged or necrotic host tissues.
*   **Memory Generation & Lifespan:** Neutrophils do not generate immunological memory. After entering inflamed tissues, they perform their effector functions and die by apoptosis within 1 to 2 days.

#### Pathologic Relevance
*   **Role in Hypersensitivity or Autoimmunity:** Neutrophils are the principal cellular mediators of tissue damage in Type III (immune complex-mediated) hypersensitivity reactions. In diseases like systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or acute serum sickness, circulating antigen-antibody (IgG or IgM) complexes deposit in the basement membranes of blood vessels (especially in the renal glomeruli and synovia). This deposition activates the classical complement pathway, generating anaphylatoxins (C5a, C3a) that recruit neutrophils. Neutrophils bind the deposited immune complexes via their Fcγ receptors and complement receptors, triggering a massive respiratory burst and lysosomal enzyme release. Because the complexes are deposited on vessel walls, this "frustrated phagocytosis" results in neutrophil degranulation and ROS release into the vascular intima, causing endothelial necrosis, thrombus formation, ischemic tissue damage, and acute vasculitis. Neutrophils also participate in tissue injury in Type II antibody-mediated hypersensitivity and are recruited early (around 4 hours) to initiate delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH) reactions. Excess NET release is implicated in several autoimmune and chronic inflammatory diseases.
*   **Microbial Evasion of this Cell Type:**
    - *Antigenic Variation:* Many pathogens (e.g., Neisseria gonorrhoeae, E. coli, Salmonella typhimurium) alter surface antigens, preventing antibody-mediated opsonization and subsequent Fc receptor-mediated neutrophil phagocytosis.
    - *Inhibition of Complement Activation:* Bacteria express surface molecules that degrade complement proteins or inhibit complement assembly, preventing opsonization by C3b/iC3b.
    - *Resistance to Phagocytosis:* Encapsulated bacteria (e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis) express thick hydrophilic polysaccharide capsules that resist opsonin binding and prevent phagocyte receptor engagement, avoiding engulfment.
    - *Scavenging of Reactive Oxygen Species:* Catalase-positive pathogens (such as Staphylococci) produce catalase to convert hydrogen peroxide to water, neutralizing the toxic respiratory burst.
    - *Inhibition of Phagolysosome Fusion:* Some intracellular pathogens evade killing by preventing the fusion of lysosomes with the phagosome (e.g., Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Legionella pneumophila) or escaping the phagosome into the host cytosol (e.g., Listeria monocytogenes).

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs)", "Acute inflammatory infiltrate", "Respiratory burst / Oxidative burst", "Pyogenic (pus-forming) infections", "NETosis", "Dihydrorhodamine (DHR) flow cytometric assay", "leukocytosis" (elevated blood neutrophil counts).
*   **Key Experimental Markers:** Multilobed nucleus (3 to 5 lobules), CD11b/CD18 (Mac-1), CD15, CD66b, CD16b, myeloperoxidase (MPO), and cytoplasmic specific/azurophilic granules.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    - *Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency Type 1 (LAD-1):* An autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency caused by mutations in the CD18 gene (encoding the common β2 integrin subunit), compromising expression of LFA-1, Mac-1, and CR4. Characterized by severe recurrent bacterial and fungal infections, a lack of neutrophil accumulation at infection sites (complete failure to form pus or make an inflammatory infiltrate), and pronounced leukocytosis because neutrophils cannot exit the blood vessels.
    - *Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency Type 2 (LAD-2):* Caused by autosomal recessive mutations in the Golgi GDP-fucose transporter-1, preventing fucose transport and blocking synthesis of sialyl Lewis X (the carbohydrate selectin ligand). This results in defective leukocyte rolling, recurrent infections, and leukocyte migration failure.
    - *Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency Type 3 (LAD-3):* Caused by mutations in the KINDLIN-3 gene, which is essential for chemokine-stimulated integrin activation. Neutrophils fail to adhere firmly to the endothelium, presenting with recurrent infections, leukocytosis, and a severe bleeding disorder due to platelet integrin dysfunction.
    - *Chronic Granulomatous Disease (CGD):* A genetic immunodeficiency caused by mutations in the genes encoding the phagocyte NADPH oxidase complex (the gp91phox subunit is mutated in the prototypic X-linked form; other subunits are mutated in autosomal recessive forms). CGD neutrophils can phagocytose microbes but cannot generate reactive oxygen species (the respiratory burst is absent). This leads to recurrent, life-threatening pyogenic and fungal infections, particularly by catalase-positive organisms (e.g., Staphylococcus). CGD is diagnosed using the dihydrorhodamine (DHR) flow cytometric assay (DHR is nonfluorescent but converts to fluorescent rhodamine upon oxidation by H2O2; CGD neutrophils fail to fluoresce when stimulated with PMA).
    - *Chédiak-Higashi Syndrome:* Caused by autosomal recessive mutations in the LYST gene, causing defective vesicle fusion and lysosomal trafficking. Neutrophils contain giant lysosomal granules, show impaired degranulation and chemotaxis, and patients are highly susceptible to recurrent infections with pyogenic bacteria.
