## Anatomy and Histology
Lymph nodes are encapsulated, vascularized secondary lymphoid organs numbering approximately 500 in the human body. Structurally, a lymph node is enveloped by a fibrous capsule, beneath which lies a network of sinuses supported by collagen fibrils and other extracellular matrix proteins. These subcapsular sinuses are lined by reticular cells and are filled with various cell types including lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells (DCs). Afferent lymphatic vessels empty directly into this subcapsular sinus, and the fluid (lymph) subsequently drains into the connected medullary sinus before exiting the lymph node entirely through the efferent lymphatics. The internal architecture of the node is highly compartmentalized into distinct cortical and medullary regions.
![[Lymph-Node-Anato-1.png]]
Beneath the inner floor of the subcapsular sinus is the lymphocyte-rich cortex, which is anatomically segregated into an outer cortex and an inner cortex. The outer cortex is organized into distinct cellular aggregates called follicles, which serve as the B-cell zones. These follicles can be classified into two types based on their histological features and cellular populations. Primary follicles lack germinal centers and are primarily composed of mature, naive B lymphocytes. Secondary follicles possess lightly staining central areas called germinal centers, which arise during active immune responses and house proliferating [[B Cell|B cells]]. A germinal center is further divided into a dark zone, densely packed with rapidly proliferating [[B Cell|B cells]] known as centroblasts, and a light zone containing non-proliferating [[B Cell|B cells]] termed centrocytes. The light zone also contains specialized stromal cells called follicular dendritic cells (FDCs). Surrounding the germinal center is a ring of naive B lymphocytes known as the mantle zone.
![[Lymph-Node-Histo-1.png]]

Adjacent to and deeper than the outer follicular region is the inner cortex, also referred to as the parafollicular cortex, paracortex, or T-cell zone. This region is organized into cords featuring abundant extracellular matrix proteins and fibers, and it is densely populated by T lymphocytes, of which approximately 70% are typically CD4+ helper [[T-Cell|T cells]], intermingled with CD8+ [[T-Cell|T cells]] and DCs. A critical anatomic feature of the T-cell zone is the presence of specialized postcapillary blood vessels known as high endothelial venules (HEVs). HEVs are especially abundant in the outer part of the T-cell zone closely bordering the B-cell follicles. Histologically, HEVs are uniquely lined by plump, cuboidal endothelial cells, contrasting sharply with the flat endothelial cells that normally line typical venules.

The structural integrity and highly organized microarchitecture of the lymph node are actively maintained by a specialized stroma composed of fibroblastic reticular cells (FRCs) and FDCs. FRCs are mesenchymally derived myofibroblasts that express the glycoprotein podoplanin, which facilitates their adhesion to the connective tissue scaffold. FRCs are located in multiple regions and are classified into functional subtypes: marginal reticular cells that form the floor of the subcapsular sinus, perivascular reticular cells that sheath the HEVs, T-cell zone FRCs, and B-cell zone FRCs. In the T-cell zones, FRCs construct an intricate network of specialized, tube-like structures known as FRC conduits. These conduits range from 0.2 to 3 μm in diameter and possess a core of parallel collagen bundles embedded within a meshwork of fibrillin microfibers. This core is completely enveloped by a basement membrane and an outer sleeve formed by the FRCs themselves. These conduit networks originate at the subcapsular sinus and extend deeply into the node, reaching the HEVs in the cortex and the lymphatic vessels in the medullary sinus. Within the B-cell follicles, FDCs serve as the key structural cells. Unlike the major histocompatibility complex class II-expressing dendritic cells found in the T-cell zone, FDCs are not derived from bone marrow progenitors; they are a distinct stromal lineage derived from an FRC precursor. FDCs possess extraordinarily long cytoplasmic processes that weave together to form the dense architectural meshwork upon which germinal centers develop.

Finally, the anatomical components of the lymph node, along with the [[Spleen]] and mucosal immune tissues, receive direct innervation from the autonomic nervous system. This nerve supply is predominantly comprised of sympathetic catecholamine-releasing neurons.

## Physiology
### 1: Antigen Delivery and Capture
The lymph node is highly compartmentalized to handle different sizes and types of incoming threats. Antigens and antigen-presenting cells (APCs) arrive via the afferent lymphatic vessels, which drain interstitial fluid from peripheral tissues and empty directly into the subcapsular sinus of the lymph node.
- **Large Antigens and Intact Microbes:** High-molecular-weight antigens and whole microbes are trapped immediately upon entering the lymph node by a specialized layer of subcapsular sinus macrophages. Unlike typical tissue macrophages that rapidly destroy ingested targets, these macrophages are specialized for antigen presentation to B cells. They capture the antigens but do not heavily degrade them, instead preserving their native, three-dimensional conformational epitopes. They then physically hand these intact antigens directly over to [[B Cell|B cells]] or Follicular Dendritic Cells (FDCs) located in the adjacent underlying cortical follicles to initiate humoral (antibody) responses.
- **Small Soluble Antigens:** Low-molecular-weight soluble antigens (such as small microbial toxins or protein fragments) are swept out of the subcapsular sinus into specialized, tube-like structural networks called Fibroblastic Reticular Cell (FRC) conduits. Lymph node-resident dendritic cells (DCs) associate closely with these conduits, extending their dendrites directly into the conduit lumen to extract, internalize, and process these small soluble antigens for presentation to T cells.
- **Dendritic Cell Transport:** Peripheral tissue-resident DCs (like Langerhans cells in the skin) act as sentinels. Upon capturing microbial antigens at the epithelial barriers, they mature and migrate into the lymphatic vessels. During maturation, these DCs upregulate the chemokine receptor CCR7. The lymph node stroma and high endothelial venules constitutively secrete the chemokines CCL19 and CCL21. The CCR7-expressing DCs follow this chemokine gradient straight into the paracortex (the T-cell zone) of the lymph node, where they settle onto the FRC network to display their processed peptide-MHC complexes.

### 2: Lymphocyte Homing and Entry
Naive T and B lymphocytes do not enter the lymph node through the afferent lymphatics; instead, they arrive via the arterial blood supply and enter the lymph node stroma through specialized postcapillary vessels called High Endothelial Venules (HEVs). This transendothelial migration is a highly choreographed multistep process:
- **Step 1: Selectin-Mediated Rolling:** HEVs are lined by plump endothelial cells that display carbohydrate ligands collectively known as Peripheral Node Addressins (PNAd). Naive T and B lymphocytes express the adhesion molecule L-selectin ([[Selectin#CD62L (L-selectin)|CD62L]]). As lymphocytes flow through the HEV, L-selectin binds to PNAd. This is a low-affinity, rapid on/off interaction, causing the rapid blood flow to push the cells, making them "roll" along the endothelial surface.
- **Step 2: Integrin Activation via Chemokines:** Rolling slows the lymphocytes down enough to sample the local environment. Chemokines (CCL19 and CCL21 for [[T-Cell|T cells]]; CXCL13 for [[B Cell|B cells]]) are displayed on the luminal surface of the HEV. When these chemokines bind to their respective receptors (CCR7 on [[T-Cell|T cells]]; CXCR5 on [[B Cell|B cells]]), an intracellular signaling cascade is triggered. This inside-out signaling rapidly alters the conformation of leukocyte integrins (specifically [[Integrin#CD11a/CD18 (LFA-1 / αLβ2)|LFA-1]]) from a bent, low-affinity state to an extended, high-affinity state.
- **Step 3: Stable Adhesion:** The now high-affinity LFA-1 integrins bind firmly to Intercellular Adhesion Molecule-1 ([[CD54|ICAM-1]]), a member of the Ig superfamily heavily expressed on the HEV endothelium. This entirely arrests the rolling process, tethering the lymphocyte stably to the vessel wall.
- **Step 4: Transmigration (Diapedesis):** The arrested lymphocyte must now cross the endothelial barrier. This occurs primarily by squeezing through the junctions between adjacent endothelial cells. This step requires the transient, reversible disruption of adherens junction proteins, primarily the VE-cadherin complex, which is mediated by kinases activated when integrins bind [[CD54|ICAM-1]]. [[CD31]] (PECAM-1), expressed on both the leukocyte and the endothelial cell, also plays a crucial role in pulling the leukocyte through the gap into the lymph node stroma.

### Part 3: T-Cell Activation and Clonal Expansion
Once inside the paracortex, naive [[T-Cell|T cells]] must quickly locate a DC displaying their specific, cognate antigen. Because a given T-cell clone is incredibly rare, the lymph node infrastructure accelerates this search.
- **Anatomic Scanning:** Naive [[T-Cell|T cells]] crawl rapidly along the physical scaffold provided by the FRC conduit network. Because the antigen-loaded DCs are also firmly attached to these FRCs, the [[T-Cell|T cells]] are guided directly into physical contact with the DCs, maximizing the efficiency of the search.
- **The Activation Checkpoints (The Multi-Signal Hypothesis):** If a naive T cell finds its target, activation proceeds via rigorous biochemical signaling:
	- _Signal 1 (Antigen Recognition):_ The T-cell receptor (TCR) αβ heterodimer binds to the specific peptide-MHC complex on the DC. Simultaneously, the CD4 or CD8 coreceptor binds to the invariant region of the MHC (Class II or Class I, respectively). The TCR is physically associated with CD3 proteins (γε and δε) and the ζζ homodimer, all of which contain Immunoreceptor Tyrosine-based Activation Motifs (ITAMs) in their cytoplasmic tails. SRC family kinases (such as Lck) phosphorylate these ITAMs. The enzyme ZAP-70 then binds to the phosphorylated ITAMs, becomes activated, and phosphorylates various adaptor proteins. This initiates massive downstream cascades: PLCγ1 generates IP3 (triggering calcium release and calcineurin-mediated NFAT activation) and DAG (activating PKC-θ and NF-κB), while RAS/RAC pathways activate MAP kinases (like AP-1), and PI3K activates survival signals via AKT.
    - _Signal 2 (Costimulation):_ The massive TCR signaling is entirely insufficient to activate a naive [[T-Cell]] on its own; it requires a simultaneous second signal to avoid entering a state of unresponsiveness (anergy). This is provided by the activating costimulatory receptor CD28 on the T cell binding to its ligands, B7-1 (CD80) or B7-2 (CD86), which are upregulated on the surface of mature, activated DCs. This costimulation synergizes with TCR signals to drastically increase the transcription of survival proteins and the growth factor IL-2.
- **Clonal Expansion and Differentiation:** Upon receiving complete activation signals, the [[T-Cell]] stops crawling and undergoes a massive burst of proliferation, a process called clonal expansion. Following this, they differentiate into short-lived effector [[T-Cell|T cells]] (which may downregulate CCR7 and L-selectin while upregulating S1PR1 to leave the lymph node and home to inflammatory sites) and long-lived memory [[T-Cell|T cells]]. Additionally, activated CD4+ helper [[T-Cell|T cells]] may migrate toward the B-cell follicles to become T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, providing crucial activating signals to [[B Cell|B cells]] to initiate the germinal center reaction.

### 4: B-Cell Activation and the Extrafollicular Response
The initiation of a T-dependent humoral immune response requires a highly coordinated spatial reorganization of both B and T lymphocytes within the lymph node.

- **Antigen Capture and Processing:** Naive [[B Cell|B cells]] residing in the outer cortical follicles first recognize native, intact protein antigens. These antigens are often displayed by specialized subcapsular sinus macrophages or follicular dendritic cells (FDCs). The B cell internalizes the specific antigen via its B-cell receptor (BCR), degrades it in endosomal/lysosomal vesicles, and presents the resulting peptide fragments on major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) molecules.
- **Chemokine-Driven Migration:** To receive critical survival and activation signals, the antigen-loaded B cell must physically interact with a CD4+ helper [[T-Cell]] that has been independently activated by a dendritic cell in the paracortex. The activated [[B Cell]] downregulates CXCR5 (the receptor for follicular chemokine CXCL13) and upregulates CCR7 (the receptor for T-cell zone chemokines CCL19/CCL21) and EBI2. This causes the [[B Cell]] to migrate to the boundary between the follicle and the T-cell zone. Simultaneously, activated helper [[T-Cell|T cells]] reduce their expression of CCR7 and upregulate CXCR5, directing them toward the follicle edge to meet the [[B Cell|B cells]].
- **The Cognate T-B Interaction:** At the boundary, the helper T cell's antigen receptor recognizes the specific peptide-MHC-II complex on the B cell. This recognition triggers the T cell to express CD40 Ligand (CD40L), which binds to CD40 on the B cell, and to secrete specific activating cytokines.
- **The Extrafollicular Focus:** Following this interaction, a subset of these activated [[B Cell|B cells]] rapidly proliferates just outside the follicle, creating an extrafollicular focus. Here, driven by transcription factors, the [[B Cell|B cells]] differentiate into short-lived plasmablasts and plasma cells. These extrafollicular cells secrete the first wave of early, low-affinity antibodies (such as IgM) to provide immediate, albeit less specialized, protection.

### 5: The Germinal Center Reaction
To generate high-affinity antibodies, long-lived plasma cells, and memory [[B Cell|B cells]], some activated [[B Cell|B cells]] and specialized [[T-Cell|T cells]] migrate back into the center of the follicle to form a germinal center. The differentiation of the helper [[T-Cell|T cells]] into T follicular helper (Tfh) cells is driven by ICOS–ICOSL interactions, SLAM family receptors, and the SAP adaptor protein, which stabilizes the essential BCL-6 transcription factor.
- **The Dark Zone and Somatic Hypermutation (SHM):** [[B Cell|B cells]] that re-enter the follicle undergo massive proliferation, forming the dark zone. Here, they are subjected to somatic hypermutation, a process driven by the enzyme Activation-Induced Cytidine Deaminase (AID). AID selectively targets single-stranded DNA loops (R-loops) that form during the transcription of the rearranged heavy and light chain V(D)J genes. AID biochemically converts cytosine (C) residues into "unnatural" uracil (U) residues through deamination.
    - This uracil can be read as a thymine (T) during DNA replication, creating a C-to-T point mutation.
    - Alternatively, the enzyme uracil-DNA glycosylase (UNG) excises the uracil to create an abasic site.
    - Endonucleases like APE1 nick the DNA, and error-prone DNA mismatch repair proteins (such as MSH2 and MSH6) recruit low-fidelity polymerases that introduce massive point mutations across the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of the antibody variable genes.
- **The Light Zone and Affinity Maturation:** After multiple divisions, these heavily mutated [[B Cell|B cells]] stop proliferating, downregulate the CXCR4 chemokine receptor, and follow the CXCL13 gradient into the adjacent light zone. The light zone contains FDCs displaying intact antigen in immune complexes bound to complement and Fc receptors. The mutated [[B Cell|B cells]] must compete to capture this limited antigen. [[B Cell|B cells]] that successfully generated high-affinity BCRs efficiently capture the antigen, internalize it, and present it via MHC-II to the Tfh cells residing in the light zone. Tfh cells then provide vital survival signals, primarily through CD40L and Interleukin-21 (IL-21). [[B Cell|B cells]] whose mutations resulted in low affinity fail to capture antigen and undergo apoptosis.
- **Cyclic Re-entry:** Positively selected, high-affinity [[B Cell|B cells]] re-express CXCR4 and migrate back into the dark zone for additional rounds of proliferation and AID-driven mutation, systematically driving up the antibody's affinity over time (Affinity Maturation).
- **Heavy-Chain Class (Isotype) Switching:** Also within the germinal center, Tfh cytokines dictate heavy-chain class switching. Cytokines (e.g., IL-4) induce the germline transcription of specific downstream constant (C) regions (such as C-epsilon for IgE). This transcription creates an open DNA state, allowing AID to deaminate cytosines in the highly repetitive "switch (S)" regions located upstream of the C genes. The resulting double-stranded DNA breaks in two different switch regions are repaired by nonhomologous end joining, looping out the intervening DNA and fusing the original V(D)J variable region to the new constant region.
- **Differentiation and Exit:**
    - _Memory B Cells:_ Relatively early in the germinal center response, after only a few rounds of selection, moderate-affinity [[B Cell|B cells]] exit to become memory [[B Cell|B cells]]. This early exit ensures the host retains "immunological breadth"—a pool of memory cells with slightly broader specificity capable of recognizing mutated or variant strains of the pathogen in the future.
    - _Long-Lived Plasma Cells:_ Late in the germinal center reaction, after extensive selection, the highest-affinity [[B Cell|B cells]] shut down BCR expression, upregulate the transcription factor BLIMP-1, and terminally differentiate into long-lived plasma cells.

### 6: Lymphocyte Egress
Following activation and differentiation, lymphocytes must efficiently exit the lymph node to execute their effector functions systemically. Plasmablasts and memory [[B Cell|B cells]] migrate out of the secondary lymphoid organs and enter the circulation. While memory [[B Cell|B cells]] recirculate continuously between the blood and secondary lymphoid organs, the newly minted long-lived plasma cells migrate specifically through the blood to the bone marrow or mucosal sites. In the bone marrow, they reside in specialized survival niches where they can secrete massive amounts of high-affinity antibodies continuously for decades.

Egress is fundamentally controlled by a lipid chemoattractant gradient of Sphingosine 1-Phosphate (S1P), which is maintained at higher concentrations in the efferent lymph and blood than in the lymphoid tissue parenchyma. Naive lymphocytes that fail to encounter antigen, as well as newly differentiated effector [[T-Cell|T cells]], memory [[B Cell|B cells]], and migrating plasmablasts, upregulate the receptor S1PR1 on their surface. Following the S1P gradient, these cells are drawn out of the paracortex or follicles, through the medullary sinuses, and into the efferent lymphatic vessels, ultimately returning to the systemic circulation to home to infection sites or bone marrow niches.
