## Anatomy
The spleen is a highly vascularized, encapsulated secondary lymphoid organ located in the left-upper quadrant of the abdominal cavity, weighing approximately 150 g in adults. The organ is directly innervated by the autonomic nervous system, primarily consisting of sympathetic catecholamine-releasing neurons.

**Vascular Supply** Blood enters the spleen through a single splenic artery that pierces the organ's fibrous capsule at the hilum. Upon entry, the splenic artery branches into progressively smaller vessels that are continuously surrounded and supported by protective fibrous connective tissue septa known as trabeculae. Unlike [[Lymph Nodes]], the spleen completely lacks high endothelial venules (HEVs); instead, lymphocytes enter the splenic tissue through an open circulation system before being directed into their respective zones. The splenic parenchyma is structurally divided into two distinct regions: the red pulp and the white pulp.

### The Red Pulp 
The red pulp makes up the vascular portion of the spleen and is composed primarily of extensive, blood-filled vascular sinusoids. Specific arteriolar branches of the splenic artery terminate within these extensive sinusoids, which are densely packed with erythrocytes and lined by resident [[Macrophage|macrophages]] and other cells. These sinusoids eventually terminate into venules that converge to drain into the splenic vein, effectively carrying blood out of the spleen and into the portal circulation.

### The White Pulp
The white pulp constitutes the lymphocyte-rich regions of the spleen, appearing visually as densely packed white nodules against the dark background of the red pulp sinusoids. The white pulp is anatomically organized around central arteries (or arterioles), which are branches of the splenic artery that are distinct from those supplying the red pulp sinusoids. Similar to the cortex of [[Lymph Nodes]], the white pulp features a specialized network of conduits lined by fibroblastic reticular cells (FRCs). The white pulp is highly compartmentalized into distinct [[T-Cell|T-cell]] and [[B Cell|B-cell]] zones:
- **Periarteriolar Lymphoid Sheaths (PALS):** The central arteries are intimately surrounded by cylindrical cuffs of lymphocytes, the vast majority of which are [[T-Cell|T cells]]. Because of this specific anatomic relationship with the vasculature, these [[T-Cell|T-cell]] zones are termed periarteriolar lymphoid sheaths.
- **[[B Cell|B-Cell]] Follicles:** The primary [[B Cell|B-cell]] zones consist of lymphoid follicles that occupy the anatomic space located between the PALS and the marginal sinus. During active adaptive immune responses, these follicles can develop highly structured germinal centers. Additionally, extrafollicular foci—which are transient clusters of activated [[B Cell|B cells]], short-lived plasmablasts, and helper [[T-Cell|T cells]]—can form in the outer edges of the PALS or at the anatomic junction between the [[T-Cell|T-cell]] zone and the red pulp.

### The Marginal Zone 
As the central arteries traverse the lymphocyte-rich white pulp, several smaller branches diverge from the main vessel and drain into a surrounding marginal sinus. Just outside this marginal sinus lies the marginal zone, a distinct and specialized region of cells that forms the precise anatomic boundary separating the white pulp from the surrounding red pulp. This zone is uniquely populated by a specialized subset of marginal zone [[B Cell|B cells]] and distinct [[Macrophage]] populations. The structural architecture of the white pulp and marginal region is notably more complex in humans compared to mice, featuring distinct inner and outer marginal zones as well as an additional perifollicular zone.

## Physiology
The spleen is a highly vascularized secondary lymphoid organ that serves as the body's primary blood filter and the central site for initiating adaptive immune responses against blood-borne antigens. Its physiologic processes are anatomically segregated into the red pulp, the white pulp, and the marginal zone, each executing distinct immunological and homeostatic functions.

**Blood Filtration and Immune Clearance (Red Pulp)** The red pulp consists of extensive, blood-filled vascular sinusoids lined by resident [[Macrophage|macrophages]] that act as a crucial biological filter. These highly phagocytic [[Macrophage|macrophages]] continuously monitor the bloodstream to remove senescent or damaged erythrocytes, circulating microbes, and immune complexes. The clearance of immune complexes relies heavily on the complement system and erythrocyte-mediated transport. Circulating immune complexes become covalently coated with the complement fragments C3b and C4b. Erythrocytes express high levels of the type 1 complement receptor (CR1, or [[CD35]]), which binds to these C3b- and C4b-tagged complexes with high affinity. The erythrocytes safely transport these bound complexes through the systemic circulation to the spleen and liver. As the blood percolates through the splenic red pulp, resident [[Macrophage|macrophages]] physically strip and internalize the immune complexes from the erythrocyte surfaces via their own complement and Fc receptors, allowing the cleansed erythrocytes to return to the circulation.

The red pulp [[Macrophage|macrophages]] are also absolutely essential for clearing antibody-coated (opsonized) microbes. For example, encapsulated bacteria, such as pneumococci and meningococci, heavily resist direct phagocytosis due to their polysaccharide capsules. Neutralization and clearance of these organisms require specific IgM antibodies that bind to the capsular polysaccharides and activate the classical complement pathway, leading to massive phagocytic clearance exclusively within the spleen.

**Antigen Capture and Handling** Unlike [[Lymph Nodes]], which sample antigens from lymphatic fluid, the spleen is specialized to capture antigens directly from the blood. Blood-borne antigens and immune complexes enter the spleen and are delivered into the marginal sinus. [[Macrophage|Macrophages]] located in the marginal zone are uniquely specialized to trap circulating multivalent antigens, such as bacterial polysaccharides introduced intravenously, and retain them on their cell surfaces for prolonged periods without fully degrading them. These preserved antigens are then directly recognized by specific [[B Cell|B cells]].

Additionally, resident [[Dendritic Cell|dendritic cells]] (DCs) in the spleen sample the bloodstream for cell-free antigens, which they process and present to T lymphocytes. Marginal zone [[B Cell|B cells]] also play a direct role in antigen transport. They express high levels of the complement receptor CR2 (CD21), which strongly binds immune complexes coated with C3d and iC3b. Upon capturing these complexes, marginal zone [[B Cell|B cells]] shuttle between the marginal zone and the adjacent [[B Cell|B-cell]] follicles, effectively transferring the complement-coated immune complexes to follicular [[Dendritic Cell|dendritic cells]] (FDCs) within the white pulp for presentation to follicular [[B Cell|B cells]].

**Lymphocyte Recirculation and Egress** Lymphocytes continuously recirculate through the spleen at an extraordinarily high rate, with approximately half of the body's total circulating lymphocyte pool passing through the organ every 24 hours. Unlike [[Lymph Nodes]], the spleen completely lacks high endothelial venules (HEVs); instead, naive T and B lymphocytes enter the splenic parenchyma through an open circulation system before migrating to their respective zones.

The exit of naive and mature effector lymphocytes from the spleen is governed by a precise chemotactic gradient of the lipid mediator sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) and the cellular expression of the sphingosine 1-phosphate receptor 1 (S1PR1). S1P is maintained at high concentrations in the blood and low concentrations within the lymphoid tissue. If a naive lymphocyte fails to recognize its specific antigen in the spleen after several hours, it upregulates S1PR1 to sense the S1P gradient. Naive [[T-Cell|T cells]] follow this gradient from the white pulp into the red pulp to exit, while [[B Cell|B cells]] migrate to the marginal zone and are subsequently carried by fluid flow through the red pulp sinusoids back into the venous circulation.

**Adaptive Immune Responses (White Pulp)** The white pulp contains anatomically segregated T-cell and B-cell zones where adaptive immune responses to blood-borne antigens are initiated. The anatomical segregation of these cells relies heavily on specific chemokines produced by local nonlymphoid stromal cells, a process that is actively stimulated by the cytokine lymphotoxin. Naive [[T-Cell|T cells]] are drawn into the periarteriolar lymphoid sheaths (PALS) around central arterioles because they express the CCR7 receptor, which binds to the chemokines [[CCL19]] and [[CCL21]] secreted in the [[T-Cell|T-cell]] zone. Naive [[B Cell|B cells]] are directed into the adjacent lymphoid follicles via their expression of CXCR5, which binds to the chemokine [[CXCL13]] produced by FDCs.

The spleen initiates two major types of adaptive humoral responses:
- **T-Independent (TI) Responses:** The spleen is the primary site for generating rapid, T-independent antibody responses against multivalent nonprotein antigens, such as polysaccharides and glycolipids. This response is driven largely by marginal zone [[B Cell|B cells]], which are abundant in the spleen and possess limited receptor diversity. Marginal zone [[B Cell|B cells]] are rapidly activated by extensive B-cell receptor (BCR) cross-linking and concurrent stimulation via CR2 binding to complement-coated antigens, completely circumventing the need for CD4+ [[T-Cell|T-cell]] help. In humans, marginal zone [[B Cell|B cells]] (specifically the MZB2 subset) rely on NOTCH signaling for their development, express high levels of the lipid-presenting molecule CD1c to collaborate with NKT cells, and rapidly differentiate into short-lived [[Plasma Cell & Memory B Cell|plasma cells]] producing primarily low-affinity IgM.
- **T-Dependent Responses:** Protein antigens elicit T-cell-dependent responses involving follicular [[B Cell|B cells]] and CD4+ helper [[T-Cell|T cells]]. Following antigen recognition, these cells alter their chemokine receptor profiles to migrate toward one another and interact. This initial interaction often forms an extrafollicular focus (also called a PALS focus) in the outer portions of the PALS or at the boundary between the T-cell zone and the red pulp. Within these PALS foci, CD40-mediated signals induce [[B Cell|B-cell]] proliferation and early isotype switching, creating a wave of short-lived plasmablasts. Concurrently, specific T follicular helper (Tfh) cells and activated [[B Cell|B cells]] migrate back into the lymphoid follicles to form germinal centers. Here, [[B Cell|B cells]] undergo massive proliferation, activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID)–dependent somatic hypermutation, and rigorous selection by FDCs and Tfh cells, ultimately generating high-affinity antibodies, long-lived [[Plasma Cell & Memory B Cell|plasma cells]], and memory [[B Cell|B cells]].

**Peripheral Tolerance** The spleen also serves as a critical site for the enforcement of peripheral immunological tolerance. Maturing [[B Cell|B cells]] that have recently emerged from the bone marrow, termed transitional [[B Cell|B cells]], migrate to the spleen. If these transitional [[B Cell|B cells]] express highly self-reactive antigen receptors and encounter their specific self-antigens within the splenic environment, they receive strong, unopposed biochemical signals that trigger apoptotic cell death. This negative selection process within the spleen eliminates potentially dangerous autoreactive clones before they can fully mature and enter the recirculating pool.

**Neurological Regulation** The physiological functions of the spleen are subject to direct modulation by the autonomic nervous system. The spleen is heavily innervated by sympathetic catecholamine-releasing neurons. Molecularly, both cholinergic and adrenergic signals delivered by these neural networks directly innervate the splenic tissue and dynamically regulate antibody production and local inflammatory states.

**Pathophysiology and Genetic Defects of the Spleen** The absence or profound dysfunction of the spleen (asplenia) causes a severe acquired or congenital immunodeficiency, specifically compromising the host's ability to clear opsonized microbes and mount antibody responses via marginal zone [[B Cell|B cells]].
- **Acquired Asplenia:** The spleen may be surgically removed following traumatic rupture or as a therapeutic intervention for hematologic conditions such as autoimmune hemolytic anemia and thrombocytopenia, where splenic [[Macrophage|macrophages]] pathologically destroy autoantibody-coated red blood cells and platelets. It can also be destroyed progressively by autoinfarction, a classic complication of sickle cell disease.
- **Congenital Asplenia and Genetics:** The failure of the spleen to develop can occur as a primary immunodeficiency. Isolated congenital asplenia is an autosomal dominant or sporadic genetic condition that has been closely linked to heterozygous missense mutations in the _RPSA_ and _NBX2.5_ genes. Furthermore, the spleen may be absent or develop abnormally into multiple, hypofunctional small splenules due to mutations in genes that control embryonic left-right laterality, a condition clinically known as heterotaxy.

Because of the severe defect in opsonic and phagocytic clearance, asplenic patients are exceptionally susceptible to life-threatening, disseminated septicemia caused by encapsulated bacteria such as _[[Streptococcus pneumoniae]]_ and _[[Neisseria meningitidis]]_. Clinical management requires vigilant antibiotic prophylaxis and early, frequent administration of vaccines targeting these specific polysaccharide-encapsulated organisms.
