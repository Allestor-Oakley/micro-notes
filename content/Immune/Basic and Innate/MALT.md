Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) consists of unencapsulated but highly organized secondary lymphoid structures situated directly beneath the epithelial barriers of the gastrointestinal, bronchopulmonary, and genitourinary tracts. Because the physical features of these distinct mucosal surfaces differ greatly—ranging from the soft, wet, and warm environment of the gut to the ciliated surfaces of the respiratory tree—the regional MALT structures are anatomically tailored to sample antigens and initiate adaptive immune responses against the specific environmental microbes encountered at each barrier.

**General Anatomic Organization of Mucosal Barriers** The tissues harboring MALT share a foundational anatomic hierarchy. The outermost layer is an epithelial barrier that rests upon a basement membrane. Immediately beneath this basement membrane lies the lamina propria, a layer of loose connective tissue. The lamina propria contains blood vessels, lymphatic drainage vessels, the organized MALT structures, and a vast, diffuse population of effector lymphocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), and macrophages. Deeper still is the submucosa, a dense connective tissue layer situated above the smooth muscle.

### Gut-Associated Lymphoid Tissue (GALT) 
The gastrointestinal immune system is the largest and most complex regional immune system in the body, harboring an estimated 50 × 10^9 lymphocytes. The organized subepithelial lymphoid collections in this region are specifically referred to as GALT.

- **Peyer's Patches and Lymphoid Follicles:** The most prominent GALT structures are Peyer's patches, which are heavily concentrated beneath the epithelium in the distal ileum. The lamina propria of the colon and appendix also contains numerous isolated or aggregated lymphoid follicles.
- **Follicular Histology:** Unlike systemic [[lymph nodes]], GALT structures completely lack a surrounding fibrous capsule. They are structurally divided into [[B Cell|B-cell]] follicles and [[T Cell|T-cell]] zones. The [[B Cell|B-cell]] follicles frequently contain prominent, lightly staining germinal centers where massive [[B Cell|B-cell]] proliferation, somatic hypermutation, and selection occur. These germinal centers are populated by proliferating B lymphocytes, T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, follicular dendritic cells (FDCs), and macrophages. Surrounding the germinal center is a mantle zone composed of naive follicular [[B Cell|B cells]] expressing IgM and IgD.
- **Parafollicular Zones and Cellular Ratios:** Between the distinct [[B Cell|B-cell]] follicles are [[T cell]]–rich parafollicular areas. A defining histological feature of GALT compared to systemic [[lymph nodes]] is its heavy skew toward humoral immunity; the ratio of [[B Cell|B cells]] to [[T Cell|T cells]] in GALT is approximately five times higher than the ratio found in [[lymph nodes]].

### The Follicle-Associated Epithelium and M Cells 
The specialized layer of epithelium that directly overlies Peyer's patches and other GALT structures is known as the follicle-associated (or dome) epithelium. The area immediately beneath this epithelium and above the lymphoid follicles is the subepithelial dome region, which is heavily populated by B and T lymphocytes, macrophages, and DCs awaiting antigen delivery.
- **Microfold (M) Cells:** Situated within this dome epithelium are highly specialized antigen-transporting cells called M cells. While they likely share a common cellular precursor with neighboring absorptive epithelial cells, M cells are morphologically distinct. They appear physically sunken and entirely lack the tall, dense microvilli characteristic of absorptive enterocytes. Instead, their apical surfaces feature short, irregular microvilli (often termed microfolds) and a very thin glycocalyx.
- **Fenestrations and Barrier Modifications:** The cellular membranes of M cells contain large fenestrations to facilitate the efficient endocytosis, pinocytosis, and phagocytosis of intact microbes and macromolecules from the intestinal lumen. To ensure these M cells have direct, unobstructed physical contact with luminal antigens, the surrounding follicle-associated epithelium is anatomically modified: it has a distinct paucity of mucus-secreting goblet cells and defensin-secreting Paneth cells, alongside a drastically reduced capacity to transport secretory IgA into the local lumen. Once M cells capture antigens, they transport them transcellularly in vesicles and release them via exocytosis at their basolateral membranes directly to the DCs and [[B Cell|B cells]] waiting in the subepithelial dome.

### The Epithelial Compartment and Intraepithelial Lymphocytes (IELs)
Beyond the specialized dome regions, the standard mucosal epithelial barrier is tightly packed with intraepithelial T lymphocytes (IELs).
- In the human gut, these are predominantly CD8+ [[T Cell|T cells]] (which outnumber CD4+ IELs by four to six times).
- Notably, 1% to 20% of these IELs express the γδ [[T Cell|T-cell]] receptor (TCR) rather than the conventional αβ TCR, a percentage much higher than found in blood or systemic lymphoid tissues. These γδ [[T Cell|T cells]] exhibit a highly restricted antigen receptor repertoire, thought to be evolutionarily tailored to recognize commonly encountered microbial structures at the epithelial boundary.
- Interspersed among the epithelial cells are mucus-secreting goblet cells, cytokine-secreting tuft cells, and Paneth cells (located at the bottom of small intestinal crypts) that secrete antimicrobial defensins and REGIII lectins.

### Waldeyer’s Ring (Oropharyngeal and Nasopharyngeal MALT)
In the oral cavity and upper respiratory tract, MALT is organized into a protective ring of tissues collectively termed Waldeyer’s ring.

- **Tonsils and Adenoids:** This ring comprises the lingual tonsils (base of the tongue), the palatine tonsils (oropharynx), and the nasopharyngeal tonsils (adenoids).
- **Histology:** Unlike the single-layered columnar epithelium of the gut, the lingual and palatine tonsils are situated beneath a robust, multi-layered stratified squamous epithelial mucosa. This surface squamous epithelium forms numerous deep, narrow invaginations called crypts that plunge directly into the underlying tonsillar tissue. The bulk of this unencapsulated tissue is composed of dense lymphoid follicles with prominent germinal centers.

**Respiratory MALT** The mucosa of the lower respiratory system (the bronchial tree) is lined by a pseudostratified, ciliated columnar epithelium containing mucus-secreting cells and specialized tuft cells. Compared to the gastrointestinal tract, the lower airways contain relatively few aggregated or isolated lymphoid follicles within the lamina propria, relying more heavily on draining mediastinal and peribronchial [[lymph nodes]] for the initiation of adaptive immune responses.

**Development and Organogenesis of MALT** The physical formation and maintenance of these highly organized, unencapsulated lymphoid structures—including Peyer's patches and the isolated follicles in the gut lamina propria—are strictly dependent on a specific cellular population known as lymphoid tissue-inducer (LTi) cells. LTi cells are a specialized developmental subset of group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s). They orchestrate the structural organization of MALT primarily by secreting the cytokines lymphotoxin-β (LTβ) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF), which are absolutely required for normal lymphoid organogenesis.
