## Anatomy
The thymus is a bilobed primary lymphoid organ situated in the anterior mediastinum. It possesses a rich vascular supply and contains lymphatic vessels that drain outward into the mediastinal lymph nodes. A defining gross anatomic feature of the thymus is its age-dependent involution; it reaches its peak size before puberty, after which it progressively involutes and becomes virtually undetectable in adults, leaving behind only a remnant of tissue.

Structurally, each of the two thymic lobes is partitioned into multiple smaller lobules by fibrous connective tissue septa, also referred to as trabeculae. Histologically, each individual lobule is strictly compartmentalized into two distinct zones: a darker-staining outer cortex and a paler, lighter-staining inner medulla.

**The Corticomedullary Junction and Cellular Entry** Bone marrow-derived multipotent progenitor cells enter the thymic architecture from the bloodstream by crossing the endothelium of specialized postcapillary venules that are located strictly at the corticomedullary junction. Upon entry, the most immature thymocytes immediately migrate outward to the subcapsular sinus and the outer cortical regions. Bone marrow–derived dendritic cells are also notably concentrated at this corticomedullary junction, as well as deeper within the medullary space.

**The Thymic Cortex** The outer cortex is densely packed with a massive collection of bone marrow–derived T lymphocytes (thymocytes), which accounts for its characteristically dark, basophilic appearance on commonly used histological stains. Interspersed among these densely packed thymocytes are cortical thymic epithelial cells (cTECs). Embryologically, these thymic epithelial cells are derived from invaginations of the ectoderm in the developing neck and chest, specifically originating from the branchial pouches. Morphologically, cTECs construct an extensive, three-dimensional structural meshwork made of long cytoplasmic processes. The developing thymocytes must physically weave through and interact with this epithelial meshwork as they migrate inward from the outer cortex toward the medulla.

**The Thymic Medulla** The inner medulla is far more sparsely populated with lymphocytes, accounting for its lighter microscopic staining. The thymocytes that successfully reach this compartment are mature single-positive T cells. Alongside these lymphocytes, the medulla is the primary site of residence for thymic macrophages and medullary dendritic cells.

Crucially, the medulla features a distinct population of epithelial cells called medullary thymic epithelial cells (mTECs), which are anatomically restricted to this region and are not found in the cortex. A subset of these mTECs differentiates into highly specialized "mimetic cells". These cells undergo transcriptional alterations to anatomically and functionally mimic various peripheral tissue cells, such as gut tuft cells, myocyte-like cells, or skin keratinocyte-like cells.

A unique, identifying histological feature of the thymic medulla is the presence of **Hassall's corpuscles**. These prominent structures appear as tightly packed, concentric whorls of keratinized epithelial cells. Hassall's corpuscles are the physical, degenerative remnants of the specific subset of mTECs that differentiated into keratinocyte-like mimetic cells.
### Anatomic and Histologic Developmental Anomalies 
The physical formation of the thymic stroma and anlage relies on specific transcription factors and the proper embryonic development of the pharyngeal pouches.

- **22q11.2 Deletion Syndrome (DiGeorge Syndrome):** This disorder results from a chromosomal deletion that eliminates genes essential for thymus development, specifically affecting structures that arise from the third and fourth pharyngeal pouches during fetal life. This deletion, which often involves the _TBX1_ (T-box 1) transcription factor gene, leads to complete agenesis or severe hypoplasia of the thymic tissue alongside an absence of the parathyroid glands.
- **FOXN1 Mutations:** FOXN1 is a Forkhead family transcription factor that is strictly required for the differentiation of the thymic epithelial anlage and other ectodermal structures. Recessive mutations in _FOXN1_ completely halt the differentiation of the necessary thymic epithelial cells, resulting in a physical absence of the thymus and hair follicles, a phenotype classically modeled by the "nude" mouse strain.

## Physiology

### 1. Thymic Entry and Lineage Commitment
T-cell development is initiated when Hematopoietic Stem Cells (HSCs) in the bone marrow differentiate into Common Lymphoid Progenitors (CLPs). These CLPs must relocate to the thymus to commit entirely to the T-cell lineage. This directional migration is governed by chemokines, specifically the interaction between [[CCL25]] secreted by the thymus and the CCR9 receptor expressed on the progenitors. Once the progenitors arrive in the outer cortex of the thymus, they interact with cortical thymic epithelial cells (cTECs) that express NOTCH ligands, definitively committing the progenitors to the T-cell lineage

Within the thymus, the survival and differentiation of these early progenitors into early "Pro-T" cells is absolutely dependent on specific cytokine signals. The most critical signaling axis involves Interleukin-7 (IL-7); the receptor for this cytokine is a heterodimer comprising the IL-7R$\alpha$ chain and the common $\gamma$ chain ($\gamma_c$, also known as [[CD132]]). Binding of IL-7 to this receptor activates the JAK3 kinase. Genetic mutations in the common $\gamma$ chain, the IL-7R$\alpha$ chain, or the JAK3 enzyme abolish these essential developmental signals, arresting T-cell maturation and causing Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID). Furthermore, these early lymphoid progenitors fundamentally require the nucleotide salvage pathway enzymes adenosine deaminase (ADA) and purine nucleoside phosphorylase (PNP) for survival.

### 2. The Double-Negative (DN) Stages and Gene Rearrangement 
As Pro-T cells begin their maturation, they lack the surface expression of the T-cell receptor (TCR) as well as both the [[CD4]] and [[CD8]] coreceptors, categorizing them into the "double-negative" (DN) stage. This DN phase is classically subdivided into four functional stages—DN1, DN2, DN3, and DN4—based dynamically on the surface expression of [[CD44]] and [[CD25]].

During this phase, the cell must successfully undergo somatic V(D)J recombination to assemble a functional TCR $\beta$ chain. This process unfolds in several highly regulated, sequential steps:
- **Synapsis and Cleavage:** The DNA segments for the TCR $\beta$ chain (Variable, Diversity, and Joining segments) are made accessible by localizing to open euchromatin. The lymphocyte-specific V(D)J recombinase complex, formed by RAG1 and RAG2 (Recombination-activating genes), mediates the DNA cleavage. RAG2 binds specifically to hypermethylated chromatin (H3K4 sites) and subsequently activates RAG1, which recognizes specific Recombination Signal Sequences (RSSs) flanking the gene segments. RAG1 introduces a single-strand nick, and the DNA strands react to form a covalently closed hairpin at the coding ends.
- **Hairpin Opening and End Processing:** A critical endonuclease known as ARTEMIS is recruited to precisely open these DNA hairpins. If ARTEMIS is defective, the hairpins remain permanently closed, halting T-cell maturation and causing a rare form of SCID. Once the hairpins are opened, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) adds random non-templated nucleotides (N nucleotides) to the coding ends, while DNA polymerase fills in single-stranded overhangs to create P nucleotides. This introduces massive junctional diversity that is localized to the third complementarity-determining region (CDR3), the most highly variable component of the eventual antigen receptor.
- **Joining:** Finally, DNA repair enzymes, specifically including DNA-PKcs and DNA ligase 4, ligate the processed gene segments together.

Once a structurally functional TCR $\beta$ chain is synthesized, it associates with an invariant pre-T$\alpha$ chain and the CD3 signaling complex (which includes the $\gamma, \delta$, and $\epsilon$ chains) to form the pre-TCR complex. This triggers the crucial "Pre-TCR checkpoint". If a developing cell fails to assemble and express a functional pre-antigen receptor at this checkpoint, it is targeted for cell death via apoptosis. Cells that successfully signal through the pre-TCR survive, undergo massive proliferation, and become "cycling pre-T cells".

### 3. The Double-Positive (DP) Stage and Positive Selection
Following pre-TCR signaling and cellular expansion, the developing thymocytes rapidly upregulate the expression of both [[CD4]] and [[CD8]] coreceptors, transitioning into the Double-Positive (DP) stage. At this stage, the cell initiates V-to-J recombination of the TCR $\alpha$ chain. The completion of the $\alpha$ chain rearrangement results in the surface expression of a fully assembled, mature $\alpha\beta$ TCR.

The DP thymocyte must now rigorously test its newly assembled TCR for its ability to physically recognize the body's own Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) molecules, an evolutionary process known as positive selection. This positive selection occurs primarily through direct cellular interactions with cortical thymic epithelial cells (cTECs), which prominently display arrays of self-peptides bound to MHC class I and class II molecules).
- **Survival by Weak Recognition:** If the newly formed TCR binds to a self-peptide-MHC complex with weak or moderate affinity, the cell is rescued from default cell death and receives vital survival signals.
- **Death by Neglect:** If the TCR entirely fails to recognize any self-MHC molecules, the cell receives no survival signals and undergoes apoptosis.

Concurrently with positive selection, the DP thymocyte undergoes lineage commitment, losing one coreceptor to become a single-positive (SP) cell. Committing to the [[CD4]]+ lineage fundamentally requires the TCR to interact with MHC class II molecules and heavily relies on intracellular signaling components including the SRC-family kinase LCK and the adapter protein UNC119. Alternatively, committing to the [[CD8]]+ lineage requires the TCR to recognize MHC class I molecules and depends on signaling from the kinase ZAP70, alongside antigen-processing machinery like TAP1, TAP2, and Tapasin. The [[CD4]] and [[CD8]] coreceptors function to stabilize these restricted interactions by directly binding to nonpolymorphic regions of MHC class II and MHC class I molecules, respectively.

### 4. The Single-Positive (SP) Stage and Negative Selection (Central Tolerance)
Following positive selection, thymocytes must commit to a single coreceptor lineage and undergo rigorous tolerance testing.
- **Lineage Commitment (The Instructional Model):** As Double-Positive (DP) thymocytes interact with self-MHC, specific signal durations and strengths actively instruct the cell to become either [[CD4]]+ or [[CD8]]+. If the T-cell receptor (TCR) is MHC class II-restricted, it receives a stronger, more prolonged signal because [[CD4]] levels are high and [[CD4]] associates highly effectively with the LCK tyrosine kinase. These prolonged signals activate the transcription factor GATA3, which commits the cell to the [[CD4]] fate, and simultaneously induces the repressor ThPoK, which actively silences [[CD8]] lineage genes. Conversely, an MHC class I-restricted TCR receives a weaker, shorter signal (because [[CD8]] associates less well with LCK), which activates the transcription factor RUNX3 to maintain [[CD8]] expression while silencing [[CD4]].
- **Migration to the Medulla:** As these newly generated Single-Positive (SP) thymocytes mature, they upregulate the chemokine receptor CCR7. They are drawn inward from the cortex to the thymic medulla by gradients of the chemokines [[CCL19]] and [[CCL21]], which are secreted by medullary stromal cells and endothelial cells.
- **Negative Selection (Clonal Deletion):** Within the medulla, SP thymocytes interact closely with medullary thymic epithelial cells (mTECs), bone marrow-derived dendritic cells, and macrophages, all of which present a vast array of self-antigens. If an SP thymocyte binds to these self-peptide-MHC complexes with high avidity, it receives active death-promoting signals. This intense TCR signaling induces the expression of the proapoptotic protein BIM, which triggers the mitochondrial pathway of apoptosis, completely deleting the highly self-reactive cell.
- **AIRE and Tissue-Restricted Antigens (TRAs):** To ensure T cells do not attack organs outside the thymus, mTECs utilize the Autoimmune Regulator (AIRE) protein. AIRE regulates the transcriptional expression of thousands of peripheral tissue-restricted antigens (TRAs). If a thymocyte binds strongly to these AIRE-dependent TRAs, it is deleted. Genetic mutations in the _AIRE_ gene cause the severe systemic disease Autoimmune Polyglandular Syndrome Type 1 (APS1), characterized by immune destruction of multiple endocrine organs because these self-reactive T cells escape the thymus.
- **Thymomimetic Cells:** In addition to AIRE-driven expression, some mTECs utilize distinct lineage-determining transcription factors to physically differentiate into "mimetic cells". These cells transcriptionally resemble peripheral tissue cells—such as gut tuft cells, myocytes, or skin keratinocytes. By transforming into these specific lineages, mimetic cells produce highly specialized peripheral proteins to strictly enforce central tolerance against those tissues. In humans, the keratinocyte-like mimetic cells eventually degenerate to form the tightly packed keratin whorls known as Hassall's corpuscles.

### 5. Generation of Regulatory T Cells (Tregs)
Not all self-reactive [[CD4]]+ T cells are destroyed during negative selection; a critical subset is instead diverted into a suppressive lineage.
- **Lineage Diversion:** Among the developing [[CD4]]+ SP thymocytes that recognize self-antigens with high affinity, some escape clonal deletion and instead differentiate into FOXP3+ Regulatory T cells (thymic Tregs). It is theorized that the decision between deletion and Treg development may depend on specific parameters such as intermediate TCR signal strengths, the specific type of antigen-presenting cell, or the local cytokine environment.
- **Phenotypic Profile:** These developing Tregs are characterized by their constitutive, high-level expression of the transcription factor FOXP3, the IL-2 receptor $\alpha$ chain ([[CD25]]), and the inhibitory receptor CTLA-4. The absolute necessity of FOXP3 for generating these cells is demonstrated by IPEX (immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, X-linked) syndrome, a fatal systemic autoimmune disease caused by loss-of-function mutations in the _FOXP3_ gene.
- **Developmental Requirements:** The generation and survival of thymic Tregs are strictly dependent on the cytokine Interleukin-2 (IL-2), which activates STAT5 to enhance FOXP3 expression. Furthermore, their development in the thymus requires the cytokine Transforming Growth Factor-$\beta$ (TGF-$\beta$) to stimulate FOXP3 expression, and uniquely requires costimulatory signals from the CD28 receptor. Once developed, these cells leave the thymus to actively suppress responses against self-antigens in the periphery.

### 6. Exit from the Thymus
Once a T cell has successfully completed all genetic rearrangements, passed the pre-TCR checkpoint, undergone positive selection for MHC restriction, and survived negative selection to establish self-tolerance, it is fully mature and must enter the systemic circulation.
- **The S1P Gradient:** The egress of mature naive T cells from the thymus is fundamentally governed by a lipid chemoattractant called sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P). S1P is maintained at relatively high concentrations in the blood and lymph, but at much lower concentrations within the thymic tissue itself.
- **S1PR1 Upregulation:** As T cells finalize their maturation, they upregulate the expression of the cell-surface receptor sphingosine 1-phosphate receptor 1 (S1PR1).
- **Egress Mechanics:** Capable of sensing the S1P concentration gradient, the S1PR1-expressing mature T cells migrate out of the thymic medulla, enter the medullary sinus draining into efferent lymphatics and postcapillary venules, and are carried into the peripheral bloodstream.
- **Intracellular Regulators of Egress:** The physical mechanism of leaving the thymus also requires highly specific intracellular machinery. The _CORONIN1A_ gene (which encodes a regulator of the actin cytoskeleton) and the _MST1_ gene (which encodes a serine/threonine protein kinase) are both absolutely required for T cells to emigrate. Rare genetic mutations in either of these molecules completely trap the mature naive T cells inside the thymus, leaving the peripheral blood devoid of T cells and resulting in a severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID).
