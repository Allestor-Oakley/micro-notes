### Complement Pathway

#### Initiation & Triggers
*   **Primary Stimulus / Trigger:**
    *   **Classical Pathway**: Triggered by the binding of the C1q globular heads of the C1 complex (C1qr2s2) to the Fc regions of IgG (specifically IgG1 and IgG3 in humans; IgG2 is less efficient, and IgG4 cannot activate complement) or IgM antibodies that have bound to multivalent antigens. It can also be initiated by soluble pentraxins (such as C-reactive protein or serum amyloid P) binding to C1q on microbial surfaces or apoptotic bodies.
    *   **Alternative Pathway**: Triggered in an antibody-independent manner by the spontaneous, low-level hydrolysis of C3 in plasma ("C3 tickover") or by the direct recognition and binding of C3b to foreign activating surfaces (such as bacterial cell walls, lipopolysaccharide, or yeast cell wall components) that lack complement regulatory proteins.
    *   **Lectin Pathway**: Triggered in an antibody-independent manner by the binding of circulating pattern-recognition collectins, such as mannose-binding lectin or ficolins (L-, H-, or M-ficolin), to terminal mannose, fucose, or N-acetylglucosamine residues on microbial glycoproteins and glycolipids.
    *   **Granzyme K Pathway (Tissue Activation)**: An auxiliary pathway where cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and Th1-like CD4+ T cells constitutively secrete Granzyme K at tissue sites, which directly cleaves C4 and C2 to initiate convertase formation in an antigen-independent and antibody-independent manner.
*   **Anatomic Location of Pathway:**
    *   Occurs throughout systemic blood circulation and extravascular tissue spaces where plasma proteins transudate. It is localized specifically to the surface of invading pathogens, damaged cells, or antigen-antibody deposits, focusing the immune attack and avoiding damage to neighboring innocent host cells.
*   **Initial Sensor / Receptor:**
    *   **C1q**: A large 460 kD multimeric sensor made of 18 polypeptide chains forming an umbrella-like radial array of six globular heads connected by collagen-like arms to a central stalk. The globular heads bind specifically to the CH2 domains of antigen-bound IgG or the CH3 domains of antigen-bound pentameric IgM.
    *   **Mannose-Binding Lectin (MBL)**: A soluble collectin family hexameric protein structurally homologous to C1q, containing N-terminal collagen-like domains and C-terminal C-type carbohydrate-recognition (lectin) domains that sense microbial cell wall sugars.
    *   **Ficolins (L-, H-, M-ficolin)**: Hexameric proteins containing N-terminal collagen-like domains and C-terminal fibrinogen-like domains that sense N-acetylglucosamine and lipoteichoic acid on Gram-positive bacterial cell walls.
    *   **C3**: The most abundant complement protein (185 kD dimer), containing an internal thioester bond buried within its thioester domain, which acts as a sensor of nucleophilic hydroxyl or amino groups on foreign surfaces upon spontaneous tickover or proteolytic cleavage.

#### Step-by-Step Cascade
*   **Phase 1 (Recognition/Initiation):**
    1.  **Classical Pathway Initiation**: Binding of two or more globular heads of C1q to the Fc domains of antigen-complexed IgM or clustered IgG induces a conformational change in C1q. This conformational change activates the auto-catalytic serine protease activity of the associated C1r dimer, which subsequently cleaves and activates the associated C1s dimer. Active C1s cleaves circulating C4 into a small soluble anaphylatoxin C4a and a larger C4b fragment. Cleavage of C4 exposes its internal thioester bond, allowing C4b to form covalent amide or ester linkages with adjacent cell membrane proteins or polysaccharides. Circulating C2 binds to cell-associated C4b and is cleaved by the adjacent active C1s protease, releasing a small soluble C2b fragment and retaining the larger, active serine protease fragment C2a on the C4b scaffold. This forms the classical pathway **C3 convertase (C4b2a)**.
    2.  **Lectin Pathway Initiation**: Soluble oligomers of MBL or ficolins bind to their carbohydrate ligands on microbial membranes. This binding activates the MBL-associated serine proteases **MASP1 and MASP2**, which are physically associated with the collagenous stalks of MBL/ficolins (analogous to C1r and C1s). MASP1 activates MASP2. Active MASP2 (and potentially MASP3) then cleaves circulating C4 and C2 in a manner identical to C1s, yielding the same cell surface-bound classical pathway **C3 convertase (C4b2a)**.
    3.  **Alternative Pathway Initiation (C3 Tickover & Amplification Loop)**: Normally, C3 in plasma undergoes spontaneous slow-rate hydrolysis of its thioester bond (C3 tickover) at 1% to 2% per hour to form a fluid-phase metastable species, C3(H2O). C3(H2O) binds plasma **Factor B (93 kD)**. Factor B is cleaved by the plasma serine protease **Factor D (25 kD)**, releasing a small Ba fragment and generating a larger active Bb serine protease fragment that remains associated with C3(H2O), forming the fluid-phase C3 convertase, C3(H2O)Bb. This fluid-phase convertase cleaves C3 to generate C3b. The exposed thioester bond in C3b is metastable; if it reacts with water, it becomes inactive, but if it undergoes nucleophilic attack by amino or hydroxyl groups on a nearby microbial cell wall, C3b becomes covalently attached. Membrane-bound C3b binds plasma Factor B. Factor D cleaves the bound Factor B to release Ba, assembling the membrane-bound alternative pathway **C3 convertase (C3bBb)**. This convertase is stabilized on the microbial surface by the binding of **Properdin** (composed of up to four 56 kD subunits, released by neutrophils, macrophages, and some T cells), which protects it from dissociation.
*   **Phase 2 (Amplification/Signaling):**
    1.  **The C3 Amplification Loop**: The assembled C3 convertases (C4b2a or C3bBb) proteolytically cleave massive numbers of circulating C3 molecules, releasing C3a and depositing millions of highly reactive C3b molecules on the target cell membrane within minutes. Newly deposited C3b can bind more Factor B and be cleaved by Factor D to form more C3bBb convertases. This constitutes a powerful positive feedback loop (the alternative pathway amplification loop) that amplifies complement deposition regardless of whether activation was initiated by the classical, alternative, or lectin pathways.
    2.  **C5 Convertase Assembly**: As C3b accumulation peaks, some of the generated C3b molecules bind covalently directly to the existing C3 convertases:
        *   In the classical/lectin pathways, C3b binds to C4b2a to form the **classical pathway C5 convertase (C4b2a3b)**.
        *   In the alternative pathway, C3b binds to C3bBb to form the **alternative pathway C5 convertase (C3bBbC3b)**.
    3.  **C5 Cleavage**: The C5 convertases bind circulating C5 (190 kD dimer) via its C3b component. The C2a (classical) or Bb (alternative) catalytic subunit then cleaves C5 into a highly potent soluble anaphylatoxin **C5a** and a larger **C5b** fragment, initiating the late steps of the cascade.
*   **Phase 3 (Effector Response):**
    1.  **Late Steps: Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) Assembly**: C5b is released from the convertase and binds to plasma **C6 (110 kD)**. The C5b-C6 complex binds to plasma **C7 (100 kD)**. This binding induces a major hydrophilic-to-amphiphilic conformational transition in C7, allowing the hydrophobic regions of the C5b-7 complex to insert directly into the lipid bilayer of the target cell membrane. Stably inserted C5b-7 recruits plasma **C8 (155 kD trimer of α, β, and γ chains)**. The C5b-8 complex penetrates the membrane deeply, creating a small, unstable pore (0.4 to 3 nm). The C5b-8 complex recruits and catalyzes the polymerization of multiple molecules of plasma **C9 (79 kD)** (up to 10 to 18 C9 monomers). The polymerized C9 forms a rigid, stable, symmetric tubular channel (the **Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) / C5b-C9 complex**) with an external diameter of 20 nm, an internal diameter of 1 to 11 nm, and a height of 15 nm. This pore allows the free bidirectional movement of water and ions, leading to rapid water influx, osmotic swelling, and cytolytic rupture of the target cell (especially thin-walled microbes like Neisseria).
    2.  **Opsonization and Phagocytosis**: C3b and C4b deposited on microbial surfaces act as powerful opsonins. Mononuclear phagocytes and neutrophils express **Type 1 Complement Receptor (CR1 / CD35)**, which binds C3b and C4b. Myeloid cells also express **Type 3 Complement Receptor (CR3 / MAC-1 / CD11bCD18)** and **Type 4 Complement Receptor (CR4 / CD11cCD18)**, which bind **iC3b** (the inactive degradation product of C3b generated by Factor I-mediated cleavage). Kupffer cells in the liver express **Complement Receptor of the Immunoglobulin family (CRIg)**, which binds C3b and iC3b. Ligation of these receptors on phagocytes triggers actin polymerization, pseudopod extension, and engulfment of the opsonized pathogen, followed by lysosomal fusion and respiratory burst-mediated destruction.
    3.  **Anaphylatoxin-Mediated Inflammation**: The small cleaved fragments **C5a** (most potent), **C3a**, and **C4a** act as powerful pro-inflammatory anaphylatoxins. They bind to their respective G-protein coupled receptors (such as **C5aR1 / CD88** and **C3aR**) expressed on mast cells, basophils, neutrophils, endothelial cells, and smooth muscle cells, inducing:
        *   Mast cell and basophil degranulation (releasing histamine, leukotrienes, and TNF).
        *   Smooth muscle contraction and increased vascular permeability (leading to plasma leakage).
        *   Direct chemotaxis of neutrophils, monocytes, and eosinophils toward the site of infection.
        *   Upregulation of endothelial adhesion molecules (P-selectin, E-selectin, ICAM-1) and production of inflammatory cytokines.
    4.  **Immune Complex Clearance**: Circulating immune complexes are coated with C3b and C4b. Erythrocytes express CR1 (CD35), which binds to these C3b/C4b-coated complexes. Erythrocytes transport the bound complexes through the circulation to the liver and spleen. Here, splenic and hepatic macrophages express high levels of CR1 and Fc Receptors, stripping the immune complexes from the erythrocyte membrane and phagocytosing them, leaving the erythrocyte undamaged to return to circulation.
    5.  **Humoral Response Enhancement**: Follicular B cells express **Type 2 Complement Receptor (CR2 / CD21)**, which forms a signaling coreceptor complex with CD19 and CD81. CR2 binds to **C3d** and **C3dg** (Factor I-degradation products of C3b) attached to antigen. Simultaneous binding of antigen to the BCR and C3d to CR2 cross-links the receptor complexes, activating the intracellular PI3K-Vav pathway. This amplifies proximal signaling, reducing the threshold for B-cell activation by 100- to 1000-fold. FDCs in germinal centers also express CR2 and CR1, which trap and display C3d/C3b-coated immune complexes to centrocytes, sustaining affinity maturation.
*   **Required Cofactors / Metal Ions:**
    *   **Calcium (Ca2+)**: Essential for the assembly and stability of the **C1 complex** (C1qr2s2). The interaction between C1q, C1r, and C1s requires Ca2+; chelation of calcium causes the C1r2s2 tetramer to dissociate from C1q. It is also required for the homodimerization and activity of MBL and ficolins.
    *   **Magnesium (Mg2+)**: Essential divalent cofactor for the assembly of the **classical and alternative pathway C3 convertases**. The binding of C2 to C4b (classical) and the binding of Factor B to C3b (alternative) are strictly magnesium-dependent; in the absence of Mg2+, Bb and C2a cannot stably associate with their scaffolds.

#### Regulation & Checkpoints
*   **Inhibitory Molecules & Checkpoints:**
    *   **C1 Inhibitor (C1-INH)**: A 104 kD circulating plasma serine protease inhibitor (serpin). It mimics the normal substrates of C1r/C1s and MASPs. Upon classical pathway initiation, C1-INH is cleaved by and becomes covalently bound to C1r2s2, causing the tetramer to dissociate from C1q and halting activation. It similarly binds to and inactivates MASP2, dampening the lectin pathway. C1-INH is also a major inhibitor of kallikrein and coagulation factor XII.
    *   **Decay-Accelerating Factor (DAF / CD55)**: A 70 kD GPI-linked membrane protein expressed widely on host erythrocytes, endothelial cells, and epithelial cells. It binds to C4b or C3b on host cell membranes, physically displacing C2a from C4b or Bb from C3b, leading to the rapid dissociation and inactivation of both the classical (C4b2a) and alternative (C3bBb) C3 convertases.
    *   **Membrane Cofactor Protein (MCP / CD46)**: A 52 to 58 kD transmembrane protein expressed on leukocytes, epithelial cells, and endothelial cells. It binds to membrane-deposited C3b and C4b, acting as an essential obligate cofactor for Factor I-mediated proteolytic cleavage.
    *   **Factor I**: An 88 kD circulating plasma serine protease. It proteolytically cleaves cell-associated C3b (into iC3b, then C3d and C3dg) and C4b, rendering them incapable of participating in convertase assembly. Factor I is active only in the presence of cofactors, utilizing MCP (CD46), CR1 (CD35), Factor H, or C4BP.
    *   **Factor H**: A 150 kD circulating plasma regulatory protein. It binds to cell-associated C3b, displacing Bb (dissociating C3bBb convertase) and serving as a cofactor for Factor I-mediated C3b degradation. Factor H binds preferentially to cell surfaces rich in **sialic acid** (highly expressed on host cells but absent on most microbes), selectively protecting host tissues from alternative pathway activation.
    *   **C4-Binding Protein (C4BP)**: A 570 kD plasma protein that binds C4b, displacing C2a (dissociating classical convertase) and serving as a cofactor for Factor I-mediated cleavage of C4b.
    *   **CD59 (Protectin)**: An 18 to 20 kD GPI-linked membrane protein expressed on all host blood cells, endothelial, and epithelial cells. It binds to C7 and C8 within the assembling C5b-8 complex on host membranes, physically blocking the recruitment and polymerization of C9, preventing MAC assembly and host cell lysis.
    *   **S Protein (Vitronectin)**: A circulating plasma protein that binds to soluble, uninserted C5b-7 complexes, preventing them from inserting into neighboring host cell lipid bilayers, protecting "innocent bystander" cells from collateral lysis.
*   **Feedback Loops:**
    *   **The Alternative Pathway Amplification Loop**: As C3b is generated by classical, alternative, or lectin pathways, it binds Factor B to assemble new alternative pathway convertases (C3bBb), driving a massive positive feedback loop that multiplies C3b deposition.
    *   **Anaphylatoxin Negative Feedback**: Circulating **plasma carboxypeptidases** rapidly cleave the C-terminal arginine residues of C3a and C5a, generating C3a des-Arg and C5a des-Arg, which have only 10% of the pro-inflammatory activity of the native anaphylatoxins, preventing runaway systemic inflammation.
*   **Mechanisms of Termination / Resolution:**
    *   **Decoy Receptor Buffering (FcγRIIIB / CD16b)**: GPI-linked decoy receptor on neutrophils that binds circulating IgG-complexes without signaling, preventing over-activation.
    *   **Spontaneous Dissociation**: Unregulated C3 convertases have an intrinsic half-life of only a few minutes in the absence of properdin, naturally decay-dissociating.
    *   **Endocytic Clearance**: Upon binding, receptor-ligand complexes (such as CR1-C3b-pathogen) are rapidly internalized by phagocytes, directing complement components to lysosomal degradation.

#### Physiologic & Pathologic Outcomes
*   **Primary Physiologic Purpose:**
    *   **Pathogen Clearance**: Osmotic lysis of thin-walled pathogens (MAC), robust opsonization-mediated phagocytosis (C3b/iC3b), and recruitment of leukocytes to the site of infection (anaphylatoxins C5a/C3a).
    *   **Humoral Immunity Enhancement**: CR2-mediated B cell costimulation and antigen presentation on FDCs.
    *   **Waste Disposal (Apoptotic Cell Clearance)**: C1q binds directly to apoptotic bodies and is recognized by C1q receptors on phagocytes, driving silent, non-inflammatory phagocytic clearance, preventing the release of intracellular nuclear antigens.
    *   **Systemic Clearance of Immune Complexes**: CR1-mediated transport of opsonized circulating immune complexes on erythrocytes to the liver and spleen for macrophage-mediated destruction.
*   **Consequence of Pathway Failure:**
    *   **C1q, C1r, C1s, C2, and C4 Deficiencies (Early Classical Pathway Defects)**:
        *   *C2 Deficiency*: The most common human complement deficiency.
        *   *Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)*: Over 50% of patients with homozygous C1q, C2, or C4 deficiencies develop SLE. This occurs due to a complete failure to clear circulating immune complexes and apoptotic bodies. Apoptotic fragments release self-nuclear antigens (like double-stranded DNA) that trigger autoantibody production, while circulating immune complexes deposit in blood vessel walls and renal glomeruli, activating leukocytes via Fc Receptors and causing chronic nephritis and vasculitis.
        *   *Increased Infections*: Some patients exhibit increased susceptibility to pyogenic infections, but others are asymptomatic because alternative and lectin pathways remain functional.
    *   **C3 Deficiency (Central Node Defect)**:
        *   Characterized by recurrent, severe, and frequently fatal pyogenic bacterial infections (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae) beginning in infancy, as well as immune complex glomerulonephritis. This illustrates the central, indispensable role of C3 in opsonization, leukocyte recruitment, and amplification.
    *   **C5, C6, C7, C8, and C9 Deficiencies (Terminal Pathway / MAC Defects)**:
        *   Patients are highly and selectively susceptible to recurrent, disseminated infections with **Neisseria bacteria** (including Neisseria meningitidis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae). This occurs because Neisseria has extremely thin peptidoglycan cell walls that make them uniquely dependent on MAC-mediated osmotic lysis for clearance.
    *   **Properdin, Factor B, and Factor D Deficiencies**:
        *   Rare (except properdin, which is X-linked recessive), causing increased susceptibility to pyogenic and meningococcal infections.
    *   **Factor I and Factor H Deficiencies**:
        *   *Factor I Deficiency*: Leads to unregulated, continuous fluid-phase C3 consumption (via spontaneous tickover). Plasma C3 becomes completely depleted, presenting with recurrent pyogenic bacterial infections.
        *   *Factor H Deficiency*: Leads to uncontrolled alternative pathway activation on endothelial cells and glomerular basement membranes. Presents with **Glomerulonephritis** (C3 glomerulopathy) and **Atypical Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (aHUS)**. In aHUS, mutant Factor H fails to bind host endothelial cells, leading to excessive alternative pathway hyperactivation, endothelial lysis, microthrombi formation, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute renal failure.
    *   **PIG-A Somatic Mutation (Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria (PNH))**:
        *   An acquired somatic mutation in hematopoietic stem cells in the PIG-A gene, which encodes an enzyme essential for synthesizing the GPI anchor.
        *   This leads to a complete absence of all GPI-linked membrane proteins on circulating blood cells, including the complement regulators **DAF (CD55)** and **CD59**.
        *   Erythrocytes lacking DAF and CD59 are highly vulnerable to spontaneous, unregulated alternative pathway complement activation and MAC assembly.
        *   Presents clinically as recurrent bouts of **intravascular hemolysis** (classically at night when mild respiratory acidosis enhances complement), leading to chronic hemolytic anemia, hemoglobinuria, iron deficiency, and a high risk of life-threatening venous thrombosis (due to platelet activation).
    *   **C1-INH Deficiency (Hereditary Angioedema (HAE))**:
        *   An autosomal dominant inherited deficiency of C1-INH.
        *   Reduced levels (<20% to 30%) or defective function of C1-INH leads to unregulated activation of C1 and MASPs (consuming C4 and C2), but the primary clinical driver is the loss of inhibition of **kallikrein** and **Factor XII**.
        *   This results in the overproduction of **bradykinin** (a highly potent vasoactive peptide).
        *   Presents as recurrent, intermittent episodes of localized non-pruritic, non-pitting edema of the skin and mucosa. This causes severe abdominal pain (GI wall edema), vomiting, diarrhea, and potentially fatal laryngeal edema causing airway obstruction.
    *   **C3 Nephritic Factor (C3NeF)**:
        *   An autoantibody specific for the alternative pathway C3 convertase (C3bBb). It binds and stabilizes the convertase, protecting it from Factor H-mediated dissociation. This leads to unregulated, continuous C3 consumption, presenting with membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis and partial lipodystrophy.
*   **Microbial / Tumor Evasion Strategies:**
    *   **Thick Peptidoglycan Cell Walls / Capsules**: Gram-positive bacteria (Streptococcus, Staphylococcus) and fungi possess thick cell walls or hydrophilic polysaccharide capsules that physically prevent the macromolecular MAC from reaching and inserting into their plasma membranes, rendering them resistant to complement-mediated lysis.
    *   **Factor H Recruitment**: Many pathogens express surface proteins that recruit host Factor H to their cell walls, or they express surface **sialic acid** (either scavenged from the host or synthesized de novo, as in E. coli K1 and Neisseria meningitidis). Factor H bound to the microbe displaces Bb, dissociating the C3bBb convertase and halting alternative pathway amplification. Examples include GP41 on HIV, and surface proteins on Streptococcus pyogenes, Borrelia burgdorferi, and Candida albicans.
    *   **Decoy Membrane Protein Expression**: Some parasites and viruses express or incorporate host regulatory proteins. HIV incorporates host GPI-anchored DAF (CD55) and CD59 into its envelope during budding, protecting the virion from complement-mediated destruction.
    *   **IgA1 Proteases**: Secreted by Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Haemophilus influenzae, these enzymes cleave the hinge region of IgA1, preventing classical pathway activation.

#### Clinical & Therapeutic Manipulation
*   **Pharmacologic / Biologic Targeting (e.g., Monoclonal Antibodies):**
    *   **Eculizumab / Ravulizumab (Anti-C5 monoclonal antibodies)**: Humanized monoclonal antibodies that bind specifically to C5, blocking its cleavage into C5a and C5b. This completely inhibits the late steps of complement and MAC assembly. Approved for the treatment of **PNH** (preventing intravascular hemolysis), **aHUS** (blocking endothelial damage), myasthenia gravis, and neuromyelitis optica.
    *   **Pegcetacoplan (C3 Inhibitor)**: A cyclic tridecapeptide covalently linked to polyethylene glycol (to extend half-life) that binds to C3 and blocks its activation. Approved for PNH to prevent both intravascular and extravascular (C3b-mediated) hemolysis.
    *   **Sutimlimab (Anti-C1s monoclonal antibody)**: Specifically blocks C1s protease activity, inhibiting the classical pathway. Approved for the treatment of **Cold Agglutinin Disease**, an autoimmune hemolytic anemia driven by IgM autoantibodies.
    *   **Avacopan (C5aR1 Antagonist)**: A small-molecule competitive inhibitor that binds the C5aR1 receptor on neutrophils and monocytes, preventing C5a-driven chemotaxis and activation. Approved for the treatment of **ANCA-associated vasculitis**.
    *   **Recombinant Human C1-INH**: Administered as enzyme replacement therapy for patients with **Hereditary Angioedema** to prevent and treat acute angioedema attacks.
*   **Use in Vaccines or Immunotherapy:**
    *   **Conjugate Vaccines**: Designed to generate high titers of opsonizing IgG antibodies against capsular polysaccharides of Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, and Haemophilus influenzae. These antibodies fix complement via the classical pathway, depositing C3b on the bacterial capsule to drive rapid phagocytic clearance.
    *   **Adjuvant Effects**: Some vaccine adjuvants are formulated to trigger local, low-level alternative or lectin complement activation. The resulting C3d deposits act on CR2 on B cells, enhancing vaccine immunogenicity and immunological memory.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    *   **"C3 Tickover"**: The continuous, spontaneous low-rate hydrolysis of the C3 thioester bond in plasma, maintaining a baseline state of complement surveillance.
    *   **"Anaphylatoxins"**: The small soluble proteolytic complement fragments (C5a, C3a, C4a) that induce mast cell degranulation, chemotaxis, and mimic anaphylaxis.
    *   **"Complement Fixation"**: The classic immunological term for the consumption of serum complement upon binding to antigen-antibody complexes.
    *   **"Bystander Lysis"**: The accidental insertion of assembling MACs into neighboring host cell membranes, which is prevented by regulatory S protein and CD59.
*   **Historical Discoveries or Assays:**
    *   **The CH50 Assay (Classical Pathway hemolytic assay)**: The gold-standard clinical screening test for classical and terminal complement pathway deficiencies. It measures the ability of a patient's serum to cause hemolysis of sheep erythrocytes precoated with complement-fixing rabbit antibodies. The dilution of the patient's serum that results in exactly **50% hemolysis** is defined as the CH50 value. A deficiency in any classical component (C1q, C1r, C1s, C4, C2) or terminal component (C5-C9) results in an extremely low or absent CH50.
    *   **The AH50 Assay (Alternative Pathway hemolytic assay)**: Screening test for alternative pathway function, measuring the hemolysis of unsensitized rabbit erythrocytes in the presence of EGTA (which chelates calcium, blocking classical/lectin pathways, but spares magnesium required for alternative pathway convertase).
    *   **Jules Bordet's Discovery (1896)**: Jules Bordet discovered complement (originally termed "alexine") by showing that immune serum contains two factors required to lyse bacteria: a heat-stable factor (antibody) and a heat-labile factor present in all normal serum (complement), for which he received the 1919 Nobel Prize.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   **The Preserved Viral Defense in CD8+ T TAP-Deficiency CTL Paradox**: Patients with TAP1/TAP2 deficiencies have extremely low MHC Class I expression and consequently very few CD8+ CTLs, yet they do not exhibit increased susceptibility to viral infections. Conversely, patients with genetic deficiencies in terminal complement components (C5-C9) are highly susceptible *only* to Neisseria infections, indicating that MAC-mediated cytolysis is biologically redundant for most bacterial and viral clearance except for thin-walled Neisseria.
    *   **The C2a / C2b Nomenclature Exception**: By international convention, the smaller cleavage fragment of a complement protein is designated "a" (e.g., C3a, C4a, C5a) and the larger fragment is "b" (e.g., C3b, C4b, C5b). However, for historical reasons, **C2 is the exception**: the larger membrane-binding active serine protease fragment is designated **C2a**, while the smaller released fragment is designated **C2b**.
    *   **Intracellular Complement Activation**: Classically, complement is described as an exclusively extracellular, plasma-restricted system. However, intracellular complement activation (such as intracellular C3 cleavage by cathepsin L) has been discovered to occur continuously inside T-cell lysosomal compartments, generating signals essential for maintaining homeostatic CD4+ T-cell survival.
