### Type I Hypersensitivity (Immediate/Allergy)

#### Pathogenesis & Molecular Defect
*   **Underlying Immune Defect / Mutation:**
    *   Immediate (type I) hypersensitivity is caused by a dysregulated, excessive adaptive immune response against nonmicrobial, otherwise harmless environmental proteins called allergens.
    *   In atopic individuals, exposure to allergens preferentially stimulates type 2 helper T cell (Th2) and T follicular helper (Tfh) cell responses, which produce the type 2 cytokines IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13.
    *   IL-4 and IL-13 act on B cells, signaling via the IL-4Rα chain and STAT6, to drive heavy-chain class-switch recombination to IgE. High-affinity IgE generation requires germinal center Tfh cells that secrete both IL-4 and IL-13.
    *   In contrast, in nonatopic individuals, exposure to these same antigens does not stimulate IgE or type 2 cytokine-driven responses, instead resulting in harmless T-cell and non-IgE antibody responses.
    *   Genetic polymorphisms in various candidate genes (such as the chromosome 5q cluster, IL-33, TSLP, MHC Class II, and the FcεRI β chain) alter the activation threshold, cytokine signaling, and smooth muscle reactivity, predisposing individuals to atopic diseases.
    *   Primary atopic disorders are caused by monogenic defects, such as dominant-negative mutations in STAT3, loss-of-function mutations in DOCK8, phosphoglucomutase 3 (PGM3) deficiency, or increased gene copy numbers of TPSAB1/TPSB2 encoding alpha-tryptase.
*   **Inheritance Pattern:**
    *   Most common atopic/allergic diseases (e.g., asthma, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, food allergies) are polygenic with multifactorial, complex inheritance, depending heavily on gene-environment interactions.
    *   Specific monogenic primary atopic disorders show Mendelian inheritance: autosomal dominant (e.g., STAT3 dominant-negative mutation in Hyper-IgE syndrome; copy number increase of alpha-tryptase in hereditary alpha-tryptasemia) or autosomal recessive (e.g., DOCK8 deficiency or PGM3 deficiency causing autosomal recessive Hyper-IgE syndrome).
*   **Cellular & Molecular Consequences:**
    *   **Sensitization Phase**: Soluble allergen-specific IgE produced by plasma cells enters the circulation and binds with extremely high affinity to FcεRI receptors on tissue-resident mast cells and circulating basophils, coating ("sensitizing") these cells. Mast cells express high levels of FcεRI, a tetrameric receptor consisting of one α chain (binds IgE Fc), one β chain (signaling, contains one ITAM), and a disulfide-linked homodimer of γ chains (signaling, each contains one ITAM).
    *   **Ligation and Signaling**: Upon repeat exposure to the multivalent allergen, the allergen binds and physically cross-links adjacent mast cell-bound IgE-FcεRI complexes. Cross-linking triggers the activation of the Src-family kinase LYN (constitutively associated with the β chain cytoplasmic tail), which phosphorylates the ITAMs of the β and γ chains.
    *   **Syk Activation**: Phosphorylated γ chain ITAMs recruit the tyrosine kinase SYK. Active SYK phosphorylates adaptor proteins (such as LAT) and enzymes, activating three distinct downstream cellular pathways:
        1.  **Granule Exocytosis (Degranulation)**: Syk-mediated activation of PLCγ (specifically PLCγ1/PLCγ2) generates IP3 and DAG. IP3 drives calcium release from the ER, and elevated Ca2+ and DAG activate PKC. Calcium-dependent phosphorylation of myosin light chains drives granule movement towards the cell surface. Calcium sensor proteins facilitate SNARE complex assembly and membrane fusion, causing rapid exocytosis of preformed granule contents (histamine, heparin/chondroitin sulfate, proteases like tryptase and chymase) within seconds.
        2.  **Lipid Mediator Synthesis**: Elevated intracellular Ca2+ and MAP-kinase (ERK) phosphorylation activate cytosolic phospholipase A2 (PLA2). PLA2 cleaves membrane phospholipids to release arachidonic acid, which is enzymatically converted via the cyclooxygenase pathway into Prostaglandin D2 (PGD2), and via the lipoxygenase pathway into the cysteinyl leukotrienes LTC4, LTD4, and LTE4, and Platelet-Activating Factor (PAF).
        3.  **Cytokine Production**: Activation of PKC and calcium/calcineurin pathways, alongside MAP kinase cascades, drives nuclear translocation of transcription factors (NFAT, NF-κB, and AP-1), inducing de novo transcription and secretion of cytokines (including TNF, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-13, and chemokine MIP-1α).
*   **Mechanism of Tissue Damage:**
    *   Tissue damage and physiological dysfunction are mediated by the effector molecules released during the immediate and late-phase allergic reactions.
    *   **Immediate Reaction (Vascular and Smooth Muscle Changes)**: Starts within minutes of allergen challenge.
        *   *Histamine*: Binds to histamine H1 receptors on endothelial cells, stimulating prostaglandin and nitric oxide synthesis, leading to rapid vasodilation and increased vascular permeability (leak), producing local edema (wheal). Histamine also stimulates bronchial and visceral smooth muscle contraction.
        *   *Leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4)*: Cause intense, prolonged bronchoconstriction, increased vascular permeability, and visceral smooth muscle contraction, being significantly more potent than histamine.
        *   *Prostaglandin D2 (PGD2)*: Drives vasodilation, bronchoconstriction, and leukocyte chemotaxis.
        *   *PAF*: Mediates vasodilation, bronchoconstriction, and leukocyte adhesion/chemotaxis.
    *   **Late-Phase Reaction (Inflammation)**: Starts 2 to 4 hours post-exposure, peaking at 24 hours.
        *   *Chemokines and Cytokines*: Mast cell-derived TNF and type 2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-13) upregulate endothelial cell adhesion molecules (E-selectin, ICAM-1, VCAM-1).
        *   *Leukocyte Recruitment*: Eosinophils, neutrophils, basophils, and CD4+ T cells (Th2, Th1, Th17) infiltrate the tissue, guided by CCR3 and CCR4-binding chemokines (such as eotaxin).
        *   *Eosinophil Damage*: Activated by IL-5, eosinophils degranulate, releasing cytotoxic cationic granule proteins (Major Basic Protein, Eosinophil Cationic Protein) and enzymes (Eosinophil Peroxidase), which are directly toxic to parasites and host epithelial tissues.
    *   **Chronic Tissue Remodeling**: Repeated, chronic bouts of immediate and late-phase reactions lead to tissue remodeling, characterized by goblet cell hyperplasia (mucus hypersecretion driven by IL-13), airway smooth muscle hypertrophy/hyperreactivity, and fibroblast activation causing subepithelial fibrosis (driven by TGF-β).
*   **Environmental, Infectious, or Commensal Triggers:**
    *   **Allergens**: Nonmicrobial environmental proteins (e.g., plant pollens, house dust mites, animal dander, insect venoms, food proteins like peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, wheat, soy) or chemicals acting as haptens on self-proteins (e.g., penicillin).
    *   **Innate "Alarmin" Activation**: Damaged epithelial tissues release the cytokines IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP in response to mechanical injury, stress, or pollutant exposure, raising the alarm and driving ILC2 and Th2 cell differentiation to amplify type 2 responses.
    *   **IgE-Independent Mast Cell Stimuli**: Physical factors (cold temperatures, intense exercise, pressure), neuropeptides (substance P), anaphylatoxins (C3a, C5a), and cationic drugs/antibiotics binding to MRGPRX2 (e.g., neuromuscular blockers, fluoroquinolones) can bypass IgE and directly trigger mast cell degranulation.

#### Clinical Presentation
*   **Age of Onset:**
    *   Typically presents in infancy or early childhood, particularly for atopic dermatitis (eczema) and food allergies, which can be the initial manifestations of the "atopic march".
    *   Allergic rhinitis and asthma frequently develop in early childhood to adolescence.
    *   However, immediate hypersensitivity reactions (including drug allergies and systemic anaphylaxis) can occur at any age upon appropriate exposure and sensitization.
*   **Classic Phenotype & Susceptibilities:**
    *   **Allergic Rhinitis (Hay Fever)**: Localized to the upper respiratory tract. Characterized by mucosal edema, coughing, sneezing, nasal and sinus congestion, inflamed conjunctiva (watery, red, itchy eyes), and nasal polyps in chronic cases.
    *   **Atopic Asthma**: Recurrent, reversible episodes of airway obstruction, chest tightness, wheezing, and dyspnea, driven by chronic bronchial inflammation, bronchial smooth muscle bronchoconstriction, and excess mucus production.
    *   **Atopic Dermatitis (Eczema)**: Pruritic, erythematous, dry skin lesions primarily on flexural surfaces, characterized by barrier breakdown and leukocyte infiltration.
    *   **IgE-Mediated Food Allergies**: Ingestion triggers vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, tissue edema (e.g., lips, tongue), and pruritus, with potential for systemic urticaria and life-threatening anaphylaxis.
    *   **Urticaria (Hives) and Angioedema**: Superficial dermal edema with severe itching (wheal-and-flare lesions) or deeper subcutaneous tissue swelling.
    *   **Systemic Anaphylaxis**: Severe, systemic reaction characterized by acute dyspnea (secondary to laryngeal edema, bronchoconstriction, and bronchial mucus hypersecretion), systemic vasodilation and vascular leak, abdominal cramps/diarrhea, and cardiovascular collapse (anaphylactic shock).
    *   **Congenital Primary Atopic Disorders**:
        *   *Hyper-IgE Syndrome (HIES)*: STAT3 or DOCK8 mutations present with severe eczema, extreme eosinophilia, cold staphylococcal skin abscesses, recurrent pulmonary infections (pneumonias with pneumatoceles), and skeletal/connective tissue defects.
        *   *Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome (WAS)*: WASp mutations present with eczema, microthrombocytopenia (low platelet count), and immunodeficiency.
        *   *Hereditary Alpha-Tryptasemia (HaT)*: Copy number increases in tryptase genes present with idiopathic anaphylaxis, angioedema, gastrointestinal pain, and connective tissue symptoms.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:**
    *   **Wheal-and-Flare Skin Reaction**: Intradermal antigen injection produces a central soft, pale swelling (wheal) within minutes, surrounded by a red rim (flare).
    *   **Atopic March / Atopic Triad**: The clinical progression from atopic dermatitis in infancy to allergic rhinitis and asthma in childhood.
    *   **Anaphylactic Shock**: Rapid-onset hypotension, tachycardia, and respiratory distress shortly after venom, drug, or food exposure.
    *   **Cold Abscesses**: Recurrent Staphylococcus aureus skin abscesses that lack the typical warm, inflammatory signs, characteristic of STAT3-deficient Hyper-IgE syndrome.
*   **Associated Syndromes or Triggers:**
    *   **Biphasic Anaphylaxis**: Recurrence of systemic symptoms 12 to 72 hours after the resolution of the initial anaphylactic episode without further allergen exposure.
    *   **Oral Allergy Syndrome**: IgE cross-reactivity between inhaled pollens (e.g., birch) and raw fruits/vegetables (e.g., apples), causing localized oral pruritus and edema.
    *   **Non-IgE Mediated Food Hypersensitivities**: Nonclassical food allergies such as Eosinophilic Esophagitis (EoE) and Food Protein-Induced Enterocolitis Syndrome (FPIES).

#### Laboratory & Diagnostics
*   **CBC / Lymphocyte Subsets:**
    *   **Eosinophilia**: Elevated absolute blood eosinophil count (often >500/μL), particularly during active late-phase reactions, asthma exacerbations, and primary atopic disorders.
    *   **Lymphocyte Subsets**: Usually normal in standard allergies. However, monogenic disorders may show alterations: TCRα constant region mutations cause a complete absence of αβ T cells (only γδ T cells are present); DOCK8 mutations cause progressive T-cell, B-cell, and NK-cell lymphopenia.
*   **Immunoglobulin Levels:**
    *   **Elevated Serum IgE**: Total serum IgE is frequently elevated in atopic individuals (often >150 ng/mL, or >100 IU/mL).
    *   **Extreme IgE Elevation**: Serum IgE levels are extraordinarily high (frequently >2,000 IU/mL, and often >20,000 IU/mL) in patients with Hyper-IgE Syndrome (HIES).
    *   **Allergen-Specific IgE**: Identified and quantified in serum using immunoassays (such as RAST or ELISA).
    *   Other immunoglobulins (IgG, IgM, IgA) are typically normal in classical allergies, though selective IgA deficiency or CVID may coexist with atopy.
*   **Flow Cytometry Findings:**
    *   Used to detect surface expression of FcεRI on basophils or mast cells.
    *   **Basophil Activation Test (BAT)**: Flow cytometric measurement of upregulated activation markers (such as CD63 or CD203c) on the surface of patient basophils following in vitro exposure to specific allergens.
    *   Can identify the absence of specific proteins in primary atopic immunodeficiencies (e.g., absence of WASp or DOCK8).
*   **Functional Assays & Genetic Testing:**
    *   **In Vivo Skin Prick Testing (SPT)**: Introducing tiny amounts of suspect allergens into the epidermis via needle pricks. A positive test is defined by the development of a wheal-and-flare reaction (wheal diameter ≥3 mm) within 15 to 20 minutes, demonstrating functional, mast cell-bound IgE sensitization.
    *   **Serum Tryptase**: Basal serum tryptase is measured to diagnose systemic mastocytosis or hereditary alpha-tryptasemia (HaT). Total tryptase >20 ng/mL is highly suggestive of mast cell disorders. Elevated serum tryptase levels within hours of an acute episode confirm systemic mast cell degranulation during anaphylaxis.
    *   **Genetic Testing**: Next-generation sequencing panels are utilized to identify monogenic primary atopic disorders (e.g., heterozygous mutations in STAT3, mutations in DOCK8, PGM3, WAS, or copy number variations of the TPSAB1 gene encoding alpha-tryptase).

#### Management & Therapeutics
*   **Acute Management:**
    *   **Epinephrine (First-Line for Anaphylaxis)**: Intramuscular epinephrine is the absolute lifesaving standard of care for systemic anaphylaxis. It acts rapidly to reverse bronchoconstriction (via β2-adrenergic receptors), induce vasoconstriction (via α1-adrenergic receptors) to reverse shock, and improve cardiac output, while also stabilizing mast cell membranes to limit further degranulation.
    *   **Antihistamines (H1 Receptor Antagonists)**: Used to treat acute urticaria, allergic rhinitis, and mild conjunctivitis by blocking histamine H1 receptors on endothelial and smooth muscle cells. They are only adjunctive in anaphylaxis.
    *   **Short-Acting β2-Adrenergic Agonists (e.g., Albuterol)**: Inhaled bronchodilators used to treat acute asthma bronchoconstriction.
    *   **Systemic Corticosteroids**: Administered acutely to suppress the late-phase inflammatory reaction.
*   **Curative / Definitive Treatment:**
    *   There is no absolute cure for polygenic allergies, but long-term disease modification is achieved via desensitization.
    *   **Specific Immunotherapy (Desensitization / Allergen Immunotherapy)**: Repeated subcutaneous (SCIT) or sublingual (SLIT) administration of gradually increasing doses of specific allergens. This treatment modifies the immune response, leading to a decrease in specific IgE, an increase in IgG (neutralizing antibodies like IgG4), a shift from Th2 to Th1 responses, and the induction of allergen-specific regulatory T cells (Tregs).
    *   **Oral Immunotherapy (OIT)**: Gradually introducing increasing doses of food allergens (e.g., peanuts) to raise the reaction threshold and prevent accidental fatal anaphylaxis.
    *   **Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation (HSCT)**: The definitive, curative treatment for severe monogenic primary atopic disorders with immunodeficiencies, such as Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome or DOCK8 deficiency.
*   **Contraindications (e.g., live vaccines):**
    *   Avoid live viral or bacterial vaccines (e.g., MMR, varicella, BCG) in patients with monogenic primary atopic disorders that involve cellular immunodeficiency (such as DOCK8-deficient Hyper-IgE syndrome or Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome).
    *   Avoid administration of allergens to which the patient has previously experienced a severe, uncontrolled systemic anaphylactic reaction unless performed under strict desensitization protocols with emergency resuscitation equipment available.
*   **Immunosuppression or Desensitization Protocols:**
    *   **Corticosteroids**: Block cytokine gene transcription (such as IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) and reduce the recruitment of inflammatory cells, being the mainstay for chronic asthma and atopic dermatitis.
    *   **Leukotriene Receptor Antagonists (e.g., Montelukast)**: Block cysteinyl leukotriene receptors to prevent airway bronchoconstriction.
    *   **Targeted Biologic Therapies (Monoclonal Antibodies)**:
        *   *Omalizumab (Anti-IgE)*: A monoclonal antibody that binds to circulating free IgE, preventing it from binding to FcεRI on mast cells and basophils, thereby downregulating FcεRI expression and preventing mast cell sensitization. Approved for severe asthma, chronic urticaria, and IgE-mediated food allergies.
        *   *Dupilumab (Anti-IL-4Rα)*: Blocks the shared subunit for IL-4 and IL-13 receptors, preventing STAT6 activation, Th2 tissue recruitment, and IgE class switching. Approved for moderate-to-severe asthma, atopic dermatitis, and eosinophilic esophagitis.
        *   *Mepolizumab / Reslizumab (Anti-IL-5)* and *Benralizumab (Anti-IL-5Rα)*: Neutralize IL-5 or its receptor, halting eosinophil production, survival, and tissue recruitment. Approved for eosinophilic asthma.
        *   *Tezepelumab (Anti-TSLP)*: Blocks the alarmin TSLP to prevent early activation of dendritic cells and the initiation of type 2 responses.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    *   **"Atopic March"**: The classic chronological progression of atopic disorders, starting with atopic dermatitis (eczema) in infancy, followed by food allergy, allergic rhinitis, and asthma in childhood.
    *   **"Atopic Triad"**: The clinical co-occurrence of atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, and asthma.
    *   **"Alarmins"**: Epithelial cell-derived cytokines (IL-25, IL-33, TSLP) released in response to barrier injury or stress that initiate type 2 inflammation.
    *   **"Anaphylatoxins"**: The complement cleavage fragments C3a and C5a that can directly trigger mast cell degranulation independent of IgE.
    *   **"Reagin"**: The historical term used to describe the skin-sensitizing antibody in serum before the physical discovery of IgE.
    *   **"Passive Cutaneous Anaphylaxis"**: The experimental transfer of hypersensitivity to a localized skin site of a naive animal by injecting IgE-rich serum.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   **Nonatopic Immediate Hypersensitivity**: Mast cell degranulation triggered by nonimmunologic physical or chemical stimuli (cold, exercise, neuropeptides, or direct cationic MRGPRX2 binding) without any allergen-specific IgE or T-cell involvement.
    *   **Eosinophil FcεRI Trimeric Form**: Unlike mast cells and basophils which express the signaling-efficient αβγ2 tetramer, eosinophils express low levels of FcεRI as an αγ2 trimer (lacking the β chain) and cannot be functionally activated by IgE-antigen binding.
    *   **Nonclassical / Non-IgE Food Allergies**: Pathologies like Eosinophilic Esophagitis (EoE) and Food Protein-Induced Enterocolitis Syndrome (FPIES) do not involve circulating allergen-specific IgE.
    *   **IgG-Mediated Anaphylaxis**: Ig ε chain knockout mice can still undergo antigen-induced anaphylaxis, demonstrating that IgG-mediated cross-linking of IgG Fc receptors can occasionally trigger systemic mast cell activation, though IgE is the dominant physiological mediator in humans.
    *   **T-Independent Immediate Hypersensitivity Limitation**: Pure polysaccharide antigens cannot elicit classic IgE-mediated immediate hypersensitivity because they cannot recruit helper CD4+ Tfh cells to drive IgE class-switch recombination. They must first be conjugated to proteins (forming hapten-carrier complexes) to elicit these reactions.
