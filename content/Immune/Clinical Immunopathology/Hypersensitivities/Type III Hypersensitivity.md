### Type III Hypersensitivity (Immune Complex-Mediated)

#### Pathogenesis & Molecular Defect
*   **Underlying Immune Defect / Mutation:**
    *   Type III hypersensitivity is caused by the formation of soluble antigen-antibody (immune) complexes in the circulation and their subsequent deposition in the walls of blood vessels and tissues. This is in contrast to Type II hypersensitivity, where antibodies bind to fixed cellular or extracellular matrix antigens.
    *   In the prototypic systemic immune complex disease, Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), genetic and environmental factors contribute to a breakdown of tolerance in self-reactive B and T lymphocytes, leading to the production of high-affinity antinuclear antibodies (ANAs), particularly antibodies against double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) and core snRNP proteins (anti-Smith).
    *   Genetic defects that impair the clearance of immune complexes and apoptotic bodies (which serve as the primary source of nuclear self-antigens) directly predispose to disease:
        *   **Classical Complement Deficiencies**: Homozygous genetic deficiencies in classical pathway components, including C1q (C1QA, C1QB, C1QG genes), C1r, C1s, C2, and C4 (C4A, C4B genes). C2 deficiency is the most common complement defect in humans. More than 50% of patients with C1q, C2, and C4 deficiencies develop SLE due to inadequate classical complement-mediated opsonization and clearance of immune complexes and apoptotic cells.
        *   **FcγRIIB Polymorphisms**: Polymorphisms in the FCGR2B gene encoding the B-cell and myeloid inhibitory FcγRIIB (CD32) receptor compromise negative feedback inhibition of antibody production, leading to uncontrolled B-cell activation and a failure to attenuate myeloid-mediated inflammatory responses.
        *   **Nuclease Defects**: Loss-of-function mutations in genes encoding nucleases, such as TREX1, prevent the degradation of endogenous DNA, leaving excessive amounts of nucleic acids available to stimulate cytosolic and endosomal pattern recognition receptors.
    *   Susceptibility is also linked to polymorphisms in non-HLA signaling genes, such as the tyrosine phosphatase PTPN22 (which regulates TCR/BCR signaling), BLK (B-cell activation), IRF5 (Type I Interferon production), and STAT4 (IFN-gamma response), alongside MHC Class II susceptibility haplotypes (such as HLA-DR2 and HLA-DR3).
*   **Inheritance Pattern:**
    *   In the vast majority of cases (e.g., SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, IgA nephropathy, polyarteritis nodosa), the inheritance pattern is complex, polygenic, and multifactorial, depending heavily on the interaction of multiple susceptibility alleles with environmental triggers.
    *   Congenital deficiencies of classical complement components (C1q, C2, C4) and nucleases (TREX1) display an autosomal recessive pattern of inheritance.
*   **Cellular & Molecular Consequences:**
    *   **Immune Complex Formation**: Soluble proteins (either self-antigens or foreign antigens) bind to circulating IgG or IgM antibodies. The physical characteristics and clearance of these complexes depend on the ratio of antigen to antibody. Under conditions of antigen excess, small, soluble immune complexes are formed. Unlike large complexes (which are rapidly bound, opsonized, and cleared by macrophages in the spleen and liver), these small complexes escape phagocytosis, remaining in the circulation for extended periods.
    *   **Immune Complex Deposition**: Soluble complexes are deposited in the walls of small blood vessels and capillaries. Deposition occurs preferentially at sites of high-pressure ultrafiltration where plasma is forced through specialized basement membranes, such as the renal glomerular capillaries (to form urine) and the synovia of joints (to form synovial fluid), as well as in the skin, lungs, and small arteries. Deposition is also promoted if the complexes contain cationic antigens, which bind with high affinity to negatively charged components (such as heparan sulfate) in the basement membranes of blood vessels and glomeruli.
    *   **Complement Activation**: Deposited immune complexes expose their antibody Fc regions, which bind to and activate C1q. This initiates the classical complement pathway, leading to the assembly of C3 convertases (C4b2a) and C5 convertases.
        *   **Opsonin Deposition**: Complement activation deposits C3b and iC3b on the complexes and surrounding tissue membranes.
        *   **Anaphylatoxin Release**: Cleavage of complement proteins releases the highly inflammatory soluble fragments C3a, C4a, and C5a.
    *   **Leukocyte Recruitment and Activation**:
        *   C3a and C5a act as potent anaphylatoxins, binding to their G-protein coupled receptors (such as C5aR1 / CD88) on local mast cells, triggering degranulation and the release of histamine. Histamine increases local vascular permeability and blood flow, driving more circulating immune complexes into the vessel wall.
        *   C5a acts as a major chemoattractant, recruiting neutrophils and monocytes to the site of deposition.
        *   Recruited neutrophils and monocytes bind to the deposited IgG antibodies via low-affinity activating FcγRIIA (CD32) and FcγRIIIA (CD16) receptors (which contain signaling ITAMs) and to C3b/iC3b via complement receptors CR1 (CD35), CR3 (CD11bCD18), and CR4 (CD11cCD18).
*   **Mechanism of Tissue Damage:**
    *   **Necrotizing Vasculitis**: Recruited neutrophils and monocytes attempt to phagocytose the deposited immune complexes on the vessel walls. Because the complexes are fixed to the tissue, phagocytosis is physically blocked. This induces "frustrated phagocytosis," where the activated leukocytes release their toxic lysosomal enzymes, granzymes, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) directly onto the vessel endothelium, causing localized necrosis of the vascular wall (fibrinoid necrosis).
    *   **Thrombosis and Ischemic Necrosis**: The severe inflammatory damage to the endothelial cells exposes the subendothelial basement membrane, stimulating platelet adhesion and activation. Platelets aggregate, and the local activation of the coagulation cascade deposits fibrin, forming thrombi within the vessel lumen. This obstructs local blood flow, leading to tissue ischemia, infarction, and necrosis.
    *   **Glomerulonephritis**: Deposition of complexes in the glomerular basement membrane activates complement and recruits neutrophils, causing severe glomerular inflammation, cellular proliferation, destruction of the filtration barrier, hematuria, proteinuria, and acute or chronic renal failure.
    *   **Synovitis**: Deposition in joint synovia causes intense local inflammation, leukocyte infiltration, synovial fluid accumulation, and joint damage (arthritis).
    *   **Type I Interferon Amplification Loop (in SLE)**: DNA- and RNA-containing antinuclear immune complexes are captured by plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) via FcγRIIA and internalized into endosomes. Within the endosomes, the nucleic acids engage Toll-like receptors TLR9 (which recognizes unmethylated CpG DNA) and TLR7 (which recognizes single-stranded RNA). This TLR signaling drives the high-level transcription and secretion of Interferon-alpha (IFN-α) by pDCs. IFN-α acts on surrounding T cells, B cells, and myeloid cells to enhance autoimmune activation and autoantibody production, while also sensitizing tissue cells to undergo UV-induced apoptosis, establishing a self-amplifying pathogenic loop.
*   **Environmental, Infectious, or Commensal Triggers:**
    *   **Ultraviolet (UV) Radiation**: In SLE, UV light triggers the apoptotic death of cutaneous keratinocytes. In patients with defective clearance mechanisms (such as complement or nuclease deficiencies), these apoptotic cell nuclei persist, releasing large quantities of nuclear antigens (dsDNA, histones, ribonucleoproteins) into the extracellular space, where they form pathogenic complexes with circulating antinuclear autoantibodies.
    *   **Foreign Protein Administration (Passive Immunization)**:
        *   **Serum Sickness**: Developed historically in diphtheria patients treated with horse serum containing antitoxin antibodies. Today, it is triggered by animal-derived antisera used to treat snakebites or rabies, or antithymocyte globulin (ATG) used as an immunosuppressant to prevent transplant rejection.
        *   **Local Vaccine Booster (Arthus-like Reaction)**: Subcutaneous or intradermal booster doses of vaccines (such as tetanus or diphtheria toxoids) in individuals with high pre-existing titers of circulating IgG antibodies against the vaccine.
    *   **Microbial Pathogens**:
        *   **Nephritogenic Group A β-hemolytic Streptococci**: Throat or skin infections (weeks or months prior) stimulate the production of antibodies that form soluble complexes with bacterial antigens. These complexes deposit in the glomerular basement membrane, causing acute Poststreptococcal Glomerulonephritis.
        *   **Hepatitis B Virus (HBV)**: Chronic infection results in high titers of circulating complexes of HBsAg and anti-HBs antibodies, which can deposit in medium-sized muscular arteries, causing Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN).
        *   **Hepatitis C Virus (HCV)**: Chronic infection is strongly linked to the generation of cryoglobulins (IgM rheumatoid factor binding to IgG complexes) that precipitate in the cold, causing cryoglobulinemic vasculitis.

#### Clinical Presentation
*   **Age of Onset:**
    *   Highly variable depending on the specific disease.
    *   **Poststreptococcal Glomerulonephritis**: Primarily affects children (ages 2 to 12 years) following a streptococcal pharyngitis or skin infection.
    *   **Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)**: Classically presents in women of childbearing age (ages 15 to 45 years), though pediatric and late-onset forms occur.
    *   **Acute Serum Sickness**: Typically begins 7 to 14 days after the first administration of a foreign protein, but can occur within 1 to 3 days (accelerated reaction) upon re-exposure in a previously sensitized individual.
    *   **Polyarteritis Nodosa**: Most commonly presents in middle-aged to older adults.
*   **Classic Phenotype & Susceptibilities:**
    *   **Acute Serum Sickness (Systemic)**: Characterized by the acute onset of a clinical triad:
        1.  Fever.
        2.  Pruritic cutaneous rash (typically urticarial, maculopapular, or purpuric).
        3.  Joint pain (arthralgias or active arthritis).
        *   Can be accompanied by generalized lymphadenopathy, proteinuria, and mild, transient glomerulonephritis. It is classically self-limiting, resolving once the foreign antigen is completely cleared from the circulation.
    *   **Chronic Serum Sickness**: Induced by repeated, prolonged administration of small amounts of antigen, resulting in the continuous formation of small complexes that cause progressive, irreversible damage to the kidneys, lungs, and systemic arteries.
    *   **Arthus Reaction (Localized)**: Characterized by localized, severe pain, swelling, erythema, and induration at the site of a subcutaneous or intradermal injection, appearing within 4 to 12 hours. In severe cases, the intense local cutaneous vasculitis and thrombosis can progress to tissue hemorrhage and ischemic necrosis.
    *   **Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)**: A systemic, chronic, relapsing-remitting multisystem disease with a 10:1 female-to-male ratio, showing a higher incidence and severity in Black females (about 1 in 250 in the United States). Clinical features include malar or discoid rashes, photosensitivity, symmetric inflammatory arthritis, glomerulonephritis (lupus nephritis), pleuritis, pericarditis, cytopenias, and neuropsychiatric symptoms.
    *   **Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN)**: A necrotizing vasculitis involving medium-sized muscular arteries. It leads to the formation of microaneurysms, vascular thrombosis, and ischemic organ damage, presenting with fever, abdominal pain (secondary to mesenteric ischemia), accelerated hypertension (due to renal artery involvement), peripheral neuropathy (mononeuritis multiplex), and nodules or ulcers in the skin.
    *   **Poststreptococcal Glomerulonephritis (PSGN)**: Manifests as acute nephritic syndrome, presenting with hematuria (cola-colored or smoky urine), oliguria, hypertension, and periorbital edema.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:**
    *   **Malar (Butterfly) Rash**: Fixed, flat or raised erythema over the malar eminences and nasal bridge, characteristically sparing the nasolabial folds, in patients with SLE.
    *   **Coarse (Granular) Immunofluorescence**: A "lumpy-bumpy," granular deposition of immunoglobulins (IgG, IgM) and complement components (C3, C4, C1q) along the glomerular basement membrane or in the mesangium on biopsy, representing discrete aggregates of deposited complexes (contrasting with the smooth, linear immunofluorescence of anti-GBM Type II hypersensitivity).
    *   **Fibrinoid Necrosis**: A histopathological finding in vasculitic lesions showing a smudgy, eosinophilic, bright-pink appearance of the vessel wall caused by the deposition of fibrin, immunoglobulins, and complement proteins, accompanied by a neutrophilic infiltrate.
    *   **"Wire-loop" Lesions**: Thickening of the glomerular capillary wall under light microscopy in diffuse proliferative lupus nephritis (Class IV), caused by subendothelial immune complex deposits.
*   **Associated Syndromes or Triggers:**
    *   **Cryoglobulinemia**: A syndrome characterized by abnormal immunoglobulins that precipitate at cold temperatures, forming complexes that deposit in small blood vessels. It presents with palpable purpura, arthralgias, and peripheral neuropathy (Meltzer's triad), and is strongly associated with chronic Hepatitis C Virus (HCV) infection.
    *   **Henoch-Schönlein Purpura (IgA Vasculitis)**: An IgA-dominant immune complex small-vessel vasculitis, presenting as a tetrad of palpable purpura (typically on the lower extremities), arthralgias, abdominal pain, and glomerulonephritis (IgA nephropathy).
    *   **Drug-Induced Lupus**: Triggered by medications such as hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid, and anti-TNF agents, characterized by anti-histone antibodies, milder systemic symptoms, and the absence of severe renal or central nervous system involvement, resolving completely upon drug discontinuation.

#### Laboratory & Diagnostics
*   **CBC / Lymphocyte Subsets:**
    *   **Lupus Cytopenias**: Leukopenia (white blood cell count <4,000/μL), lymphopenia (lymphocytes <1,500/μL), thrombocytopenia (platelets <100,000/μL), and hemolytic anemia can be observed in patients with active SLE.
    *   **Neutrophilic Leukocytosis**: Elevated absolute neutrophil counts are common during acute vasculitic flares or active systemic serum sickness.
*   **Immunoglobulin Levels:**
    *   **Hypergammaglobulinemia**: Marked polyclonal elevation of serum IgG and IgM is common in patients with active systemic autoimmune diseases like SLE.
    *   **Circulating Autoantibodies (in SLE)**:
        *   **Antinuclear Antibodies (ANAs)**: Highly sensitive screening test (positive in >95% of active cases) but has low specificity.
        *   **Anti-Double-Stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA)**: Highly specific for SLE. Titers fluctuate with disease activity and correlate strongly with the development of active lupus nephritis.
        *   **Anti-Smith (anti-Sm)**: Antibodies against core snRNP proteins, highly specific for SLE.
        *   **Anti-Ro (SSA) and Anti-La (SSB)**: Associated with cutaneous photosensitivity, Sjögren's syndrome, and a high risk of neonatal lupus with congenital heart block in infants born to positive mothers.
        *   **Anti-Histone**: Positive in >95% of patients with drug-induced lupus.
*   **Flow Cytometry Findings:**
    *   Used to monitor the absolute depletion of CD19+ and CD20+ B lymphocytes in patients undergoing anti-CD20 (Rituximab) therapy.
    *   Used to detect specific cell surface markers on plasmacytoid dendritic cells (such as CD303/BDCA2 and CD304/neuropilin) or to identify alterations in Fc receptor expression profiles on leukocytes.
*   **Functional Assays & Genetic Testing:**
    *   **Serum Complement Levels (C3 and C4)**: Measured quantitatively by automated nephelometry. Serum C3 and C4 levels are markedly **decreased** (consumed) during active immune complex diseases (e.g., active lupus nephritis flare, acute PSGN, active serum sickness) due to classical complement pathway activation by deposited complexes. Monitoring C3 and C4 is a primary clinical method to assess ongoing immune complex formation and disease activity.
    *   **CH50 Hemolytic Complement Assay**: Measures the functional integrity of the classical and terminal complement pathways. The CH50 is significantly reduced or undetectable in patients with active immune complex diseases (complement consumption) or those with congenital classical pathway deficiencies.
    *   **Renal or Skin Biopsy (with Immunofluorescence and Electron Microscopy)**:
        *   **Immunofluorescence Microscopy**: Demonstrates coarse, granular ("lumpy-bumpy") deposits of IgG, IgM, IgA, C1q, C3, and C4 along the glomerular capillaries or mesangium, or in a continuous band at the dermal-epidermal junction of sun-exposed skin (lupus band test).
        *   **Electron Microscopy**: Reveals electron-dense deposits in subendothelial, subepithelial, or mesangial spaces. Subepithelial "humps" are characteristic of acute PSGN, whereas subendothelial deposits forming "wire loops" are classic for Class IV lupus nephritis.
    *   **Genetic Testing**: Next-generation sequencing is used to identify homozygous mutations in C1QA, C1QB, C1QG, C2, and C4 genes in patients presenting with atypical, early-onset, or familial systemic lupus erythematosus.

#### Management & Therapeutics
*   **Acute Management:**
    *   **High-Dose Corticosteroids (e.g., Methylprednisolone pulses)**: First-line emergency treatment for severe, organ-threatening Type III reactions (such as active lupus nephritis, systemic vasculitis, or severe systemic serum sickness). Corticosteroids rapidly suppress cytokine transcription, inhibit leukocyte chemotaxis, downregulate activating Fcγ receptors on macrophages, and decrease vascular permeability.
    *   **Plasmapheresis (Plasma Exchange)**: Utilized as a rapid rescue therapy in severe, life-threatening active immune complex diseases (such as severe lupus nephritis, active cryoglobulinemia with renal failure, or hyperacute vasculitis) to physically extract pathogenic circulating autoantibodies, soluble antigens, and circulating immune complexes.
*   **Curative / Definitive Treatment:**
    *   There is no absolute cure for polygenic systemic autoimmune Type III hypersensitivities. Complete, curative resolution is only achievable in acute, self-limiting cases (such as acute serum sickness or PSGN) once the causative foreign protein or microbial antigen is cleared from the host circulation.
*   **Contraindications (e.g., live vaccines):**
    *   Avoid live attenuated viral or bacterial vaccines (such as MMR, varicella, oral polio, or BCG) in patients who are actively undergoing systemic immunosuppressive therapy.
    *   Avoid re-exposure to foreign therapeutic proteins, drugs, or vaccines that have previously triggered severe, documented systemic serum sickness or localized Arthus reactions.
*   **Immunosuppression or Desensitization Protocols:**
    *   **Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF)**: An immunosuppressive agent that blocks de novo purine synthesis in lymphocytes. It is highly effective and represents the standard first-line maintenance therapy for lupus nephritis.
    *   **Cyclophosphamide**: An alkylating agent used as intensive induction therapy for severe, life-threatening active lupus nephritis or systemic vasculitis, destroying rapidly dividing lymphocytes.
    *   **Belimumab (Anti-BAFF)**: A human monoclonal antibody that binds to and neutralizes soluble BAFF (BLyS), preventing its interaction with BAFF-R, TACI, and BCMA. This blocks survival and differentiation signals in self-reactive B cells, driving their apoptosis. Approved for active SLE and lupus nephritis.
    *   **Anifrolumab (Anti-IFNAR1)**: A monoclonal antibody that binds to subunit 1 of the Type I Interferon receptor (IFNAR1), blocking the signaling of all Type I interferons (IFN-α, IFN-β, IFN-ω). This suppresses the interferon-stimulated gene signature that drives plasmacytoid DC and B-cell activation in SLE, approved for moderate-to-severe SLE.
    *   **Avacopan (C5aR1 Antagonist)**: A small-molecule, competitive inhibitor of the C5a receptor (C5aR1 / CD88). It blocks C5a-mediated neutrophil activation and recruitment, preventing complement-driven systemic vasculitis. Approved for the treatment of active ANCA-associated vasculitis.
    *   **Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG)**: High-dose pooled IgG (1 to 2 g/kg) delivers immunomodulatory effects by: (1) binding to the inhibitory receptor FcγRIIB on B lymphocytes and DCs to attenuate autoantibody production and myeloid inflammation, and (2) competing with host pathogenic IgG for neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) binding, accelerating the catabolism and clearance of the disease-causing antibodies.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    *   **"Lumpy-Bumpy"**: The granular, discontinuous immunofluorescence staining of IgG and C3 deposits along basement membranes, distinguishing Type III immune complex deposition from the smooth, ribbon-like linear pattern of Type II anti-basement membrane diseases.
    *   **"Frustrated Phagocytosis"**: The destructive release of lysosomal enzymes and reactive oxygen species by neutrophils unable to internalize large, tissue-deposited immune complexes, causing localized tissue necrosis.
    *   **"Serum Sickness"**: The systemic vasculitic syndrome triggered by host antibody responses to foreign therapeutic proteins, historically horse serum and modern animal-derived antivenoms or monoclonal antibodies containing nonhuman sequences.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   **IgA Immune Complexes**: While IgG and IgM are the classic complement-fixing antibodies, many Type III diseases are linked to IgA-dominant immune complexes (e.g., IgA nephropathy, Henoch-Schönlein purpura). IgA does not bind C1q and cannot activate the classical pathway. Instead, altered IgA1 glycosylation recruits mannose-binding lectin (MBL) to activate the lectin pathway. Once some C3b is generated, the alternative complement pathway is recruited to amplify tissue damage.
    *   **Planted Antigens (In Situ Formation)**: In some forms of glomerulonephritis, the antigen is not part of a circulating complex that deposits in the kidney. Instead, a cationic foreign or self-protein first physically binds or "plants" in the glomerular basement membrane due to charge interactions. Host antibodies subsequently bind to these planted antigens locally, forming immune complexes in situ, which present with the identical "granular" pathologic appearance of deposited circulating complexes.
    *   **The Complement Clearance Paradox**: Early complement proteins (C1q, C2, C4) are essential components of the classical pathway that mediates the inflammatory tissue damage in Type III hypersensitivity. However, congenital deficiencies in these exact same proteins are the strongest known genetic risk factors for developing SLE (with >50% of C1q-deficient individuals developing severe lupus), because complement is also absolutely required for the physiological opsonization, solubilization, and macrophage clearance of circulating immune complexes and apoptotic debris. In their absence, complexes accumulate, deposit in vessels, and drive systemic disease.
