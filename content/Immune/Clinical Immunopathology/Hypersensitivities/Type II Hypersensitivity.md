### Type II Hypersensitivity (Antibody-Mediated)

#### Pathogenesis & Molecular Defect
*   **Underlying Immune Defect / Mutation:**
    *   Type II hypersensitivity is caused by a breakdown in immunological self-tolerance, leading to the production of tissue-specific or cell-surface-specific autoantibodies (typically IgG or IgM). It can also be initiated by antibodies produced against foreign antigens (such as microbial cell wall proteins or therapeutic drugs acting as haptens) that cross-react with self-tissues.
    *   Unlike Type III hypersensitivity, which is mediated by circulating immune complexes, the antibodies in Type II hypersensitivity specifically target fixed, non-soluble antigens present on particular cell membranes (e.g., erythrocytes, platelets, epidermal cells) or within extracellular matrix structures (e.g., basement membranes).
    *   Specific genetic defects can also lead to Type II hypersensitivity-like presentations, such as acquired somatic mutations in the PIG-A (phosphatidylinositol glycan class A) gene in hematopoietic stem cells. This mutation disrupts the synthesis of GPI (glycophosphatidylinositol) anchors, preventing the expression of GPI-linked complement regulatory proteins (DAF/CD55 and CD59) on erythrocytes, leading to intravascular hemolysis.
    *   Specific polymorphisms in the FCGR2B (CD32) gene, which encodes the B-cell and myeloid inhibitory FcγRIIB receptor, can compromise feedback inhibition of antibody production, increasing susceptibility to autoimmune cytopenias and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).
*   **Inheritance Pattern:**
    *   In the majority of organ-specific autoimmune Type II hypersensitivities (such as Graves' disease, Myasthenia Gravis, Pemphigus vulgaris, and Goodpasture syndrome), inheritance is complex, polygenic, and multifactorial rather than Mendelian, involving susceptibility alleles (such as specific HLA-DR and HLA-DQ alleles) and environmental triggers.
    *   For Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria (PNH), it is an acquired, non-inherited clonal disorder caused by a somatic mutation in the PIG-A gene on the X chromosome of hematopoietic stem cells.
    *   Rarely, mutations in genes encoding complement regulatory proteins can display autosomal recessive or codominant patterns (e.g., Factor H and MCP gene mutations in atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS)).
*   **Cellular & Molecular Consequences:**
    *   Autoantibodies of the IgG or IgM isotype bind to their specific target antigens on cell surfaces or basement membranes. This local antigen-antibody binding triggers three main effector pathways:
        1.  **Opsonization and Phagocytosis**:
            *   Antibodies of IgG subclasses (mainly IgG1 and IgG3) coat (opsonize) target cells (such as erythrocytes or platelets).
            *   These opsonized cells are recognized with high affinity by the leukocyte activating receptor FcγRI (CD64) expressed on macrophages and neutrophils, which contains a signaling homodimer of the FcR γ chain containing an ITAM (Immunoreceptor Tyrosine-based Activation Motif).
            *   Alternatively, IgG or IgM antibody binding initiates the classical pathway of complement. Complement activation deposits the opsonin C3b (and iC3b) on the cell membrane, which is recognized by the complement receptor CR1 (CD35) and the integrin receptors CR3 (CD11bCD18 / Mac-1) and CR4 (CD11cCD18) on phagocytes.
            *   Dual ligation of Fc receptors and complement receptors triggers the remodeling of the actin cytoskeleton, leading to the engulfment (phagocytosis) and clearance of the opsonized cells, primarily within the spleen and liver.
        2.  **Complement- and Fc Receptor-Mediated Inflammation**:
            *   When antibodies deposit on fixed, non-phagocytosable tissue structures (such as basement membranes or extracellular matrix), they initiate the classical complement cascade, leading to the assembly of C1q onto antigen-complexed Fc portions.
            *   Complement activation releases the highly inflammatory, chemotactic cleavage fragments (anaphylatoxins) C3a and C5a, which bind to their G-protein coupled receptors (such as CD88) on granulocytes.
            *   Neutrophils and monocytes are recruited to the tissue site. They bind to the deposited IgG antibodies via low-affinity activating FcγRIIA (CD32) and FcγRIIIA (CD16) receptors, which contain or associate with ITAMs.
            *   Instead of phagocytosing the fixed tissue, these activated leukocytes release microbicidal substances, including reactive oxygen species (ROS), nitric oxide (NO), and lysosomal proteolytic enzymes directly onto the host tissue, causing localized tissue injury (frustrated phagocytosis).
        3.  **Abnormalities in Cellular Function**:
            *   Antibodies bind to cell-surface receptors or key physiological proteins, altering their signaling or neutralizing their functions without directly causing structural cell lysis or inflammatory cell infiltration.
            *   These functional autoantibodies can act as agonists (e.g., stimulating hormone receptors without their natural ligand) or antagonists (e.g., blocking neurotransmitter binding, neutralizing essential secreted transport proteins, or inducing internalization and downmodulation of critical cell receptors).
*   **Mechanism of Tissue Damage:**
    *   **Erythrocyte and Platelet Destruction**: In autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) and autoimmune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP), opsonized red cells or platelets are destroyed by splenic and hepatic macrophages via FcγRI- and CR1-mediated phagocytosis. Red cells may also undergo direct complement-mediated cytolysis through the assembly of the Membrane Attack Complex (MAC, C5b-9) on their membranes.
    *   **Basement Membrane Damage**: In Goodpasture syndrome, IgG autoantibodies specific for the noncollagenous NC1 domain of the α3 chain of Type IV collagen bind to basement membranes in the renal glomeruli and pulmonary alveoli. This fixed antibody deposition drives classical complement pathway activation, C3a/C5a release, and neutrophil-rich inflammatory infiltration, causing severe, crescentic glomerulonephritis and alveolar hemorrhage.
    *   **Epidermal Acantholysis**: In Pemphigus vulgaris, IgG autoantibodies target Desmoglein-1 and Desmoglein-3, which are critical desmosomal cell-adhesion molecules in the intercellular junctions of epidermal cells. Antibody binding triggers the activation of extracellular proteases, leading to the dissolution of intercellular junctions (acantholysis) and the formation of severe skin blisters (bullae).
    *   **Neuromuscular Blockade**: In Myasthenia Gravis, IgG autoantibodies bind to the alpha subunit of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (AChR) at the neuromuscular junction. These antibodies block acetylcholine binding, induce the endocytosis and lysosomal degradation of the receptors (receptor downmodulation), and activate the classical complement pathway, causing inflammatory destruction of the motor endplate membrane, resulting in severe muscle weakness and flaccid paralysis.
    *   **Receptor Hyperactivation**: In Graves' disease, IgG autoantibodies (thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins (TSIs)) bind to the thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) receptor on thyroid epithelial cells. This binding mimics TSH, activating the adenylate cyclase pathway and stimulating continuous, uncontrolled thyroid hormone (T3 and T4) synthesis and release, leading to hyperthyroidism without causing cell injury or inflammation.
    *   **Vitamin B12 Malabsorption**: In autoimmune pernicious anemia, IgG antibodies are directed against Intrinsic Factor (IF) secreted by gastric parietal cells. These antibodies neutralize IF, preventing its binding to vitamin B12 or blocking the IF-B12 complex from binding to its receptor on ileal enterocytes. This impairs vitamin B12 absorption, compromising DNA synthesis during erythropoiesis. There may also be antibody- or T-cell-mediated destruction of gastric parietal cells, permanently reducing IF secretion.
    *   **Myocarditis and Endocarditis**: In acute rheumatic fever, cross-reactive IgG antibodies deposit in cardiac tissue, triggering complement activation, macrophage recruitment, and chronic inflammatory damage to the heart muscle (myocarditis) and heart valves (endocarditis).
    *   **Vascular Endothelial Damage and Occlusion**: In hyperacute transplant rejection, preformed IgM (e.g., anti-ABO) or IgG (anti-HLA) antibodies bind to donor endothelial cell-surface antigens. This binding triggers rapid classical complement activation, causing endothelial cell injury, exposure of the subendothelial basement membrane, secretion of high-molecular-weight von Willebrand factor, and platelet adhesion/aggregation. It also leads to the loss of cell-surface heparan sulfate proteoglycans, resulting in intravascular thrombosis, fibrin deposition, and irreversible ischemic necrosis of the graft.
*   **Environmental, Infectious, or Commensal Triggers:**
    *   **Microbial Infections (Molecular Mimicry)**:
        *   **Group A β-hemolytic Streptococci**: Pharyngeal infection with streptogenic strains induces antibodies against bacterial cell wall proteins (such as M protein). These antibodies cross-react with host myocardial sarcolemmal proteins, cardiac myosin, and endocardial valvular glycoproteins, triggering Acute Rheumatic Fever.
        *   **Nephritogenic Streptococcal Strains**: Skin or throat infections can precede antibody-mediated poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis.
    *   **Therapeutic Drug Haptens**: Drugs (such as penicillin, cephalosporins, quinidine, or methyldopa) can bind chemically to the surface membranes of red blood cells or platelets, acting as haptens. This hapten-carrier complex stimulates B cells to produce IgG antibodies that target the modified membrane, leading to drug-induced hemolytic anemia or drug-induced thrombocytopenia.
    *   **Alloantigenic Exposures**: Prior exposure to non-self blood group (ABO) carbohydrates or polymorphic human leukocyte antigens (HLA) via blood transfusions, previous organ transplantations, or multiple pregnancies sensitizes individuals, generating preformed antibodies that can trigger hemolytic transfusion reactions or hyperacute graft rejection.

#### Clinical Presentation
*   **Age of Onset:**
    *   Highly variable depending on the specific disease entity.
    *   **Hemolytic Disease of the Fetus and Newborn (Erythroblastosis fetalis)**: Occurs in utero or immediately at birth, specifically in the second or subsequent pregnancies of Rh-negative mothers carrying an Rh-positive fetus.
    *   **Hyperacute Transplant Rejection**: Begins within minutes to hours after host blood vessels are anastomosed to graft vessels.
    *   **Acute Rheumatic Fever**: Typically presents in children and adolescents (ages 5 to 15 years), weeks to months after an untreated streptococcal pharyngitis.
    *   **Autoimmune Cytopenias (AIHA, ITP)**: Can occur at any age, from childhood to late adulthood.
    *   **Myasthenia Gravis, Graves' Disease, Goodpasture Syndrome, and Pemphigus Vulgaris**: Most commonly present in young to middle-aged adults, with a pronounced female predominance in Myasthenia Gravis and Graves' disease.
*   **Classic Phenotype & Susceptibilities:**
    *   **Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)**: Characterized by severe fatigue, pallor, dyspnea, splenomegaly (due to extravascular splenic macrophage phagocytosis of red cells), and jaundice (due to bilirubin accumulation from hemoglobin degradation).
    *   **Autoimmune Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP)**: Presents with mucosal bleeding, petechiae, purpura, epistaxis, and a high susceptibility to intracranial or visceral hemorrhage in severe cases.
    *   **Goodpasture Syndrome**: Presents with the classic clinical dyad of nephritis (characterized by hematuria, proteinuria, rapidly progressive renal failure, and uremia) and pulmonary alveolar hemorrhage (characterized by hemoptysis, dyspnea, cough, and iron deficiency anemia).
    *   **Pemphigus Vulgaris**: Characterized by flaccid, fragile skin blisters (bullae) and painful mucosal erosions (e.g., in the mouth) that easily rupture, leaving raw, denuded areas susceptible to secondary bacterial infections and severe fluid loss.
    *   **Myasthenia Gravis**: Manifests as fluctuating skeletal muscle weakness that worsens with repetitive activity and improves with rest. Initial symptoms often involve ocular muscles, causing ptosis and diplopia, and can progress to generalized weakness, bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysarthria), and life-threatening respiratory failure (myasthenic crisis).
    *   **Graves' Disease**: Presents with hyperthyroidism (characterized by weight loss, heat intolerance, anxiety, tremors, sinus tachycardia, palpitations, goiter, pretibial myxedema) and Graves' ophthalmopathy (characterized by exophthalmos and retro-orbital tissue inflammation).
    *   **Autoimmune Pernicious Anemia**: Characterized by macrocytic (megaloblastic) anemia (causing fatigue and pallor) and neurological symptoms (such as paresthesias, loss of vibratory and position sensation, and spastic ataxia due to subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord resulting from vitamin B12 deficiency).
    *   **Hyperacute Rejection**: Characterized by rapid cyanosis, mottling, and complete failure of the newly transplanted organ (such as a kidney) on the operating table, with absent urine output and severe ischemia.
    *   **Acute Rheumatic Fever**: Characterized by migratory polyarthritis of large joints, carditis (myocarditis, endocarditis with chronic valve dysfunction), subcutaneous nodules, erythema marginatum, and Sydenham's chorea.
*   **Pathognomonic Signs:**
    *   **Nikolsky's Sign**: Gentle lateral pressure on normal-appearing skin induces peeling or blister extension, characteristic of Pemphigus Vulgaris (distinguishing it from bullous pemphigoid).
    *   **Linear Immunofluorescence**: Continuous, smooth, linear ribbon-like staining of IgG and C3 along the glomerular and alveolar basement membranes under immunofluorescence microscopy, pathognomonic for Goodpasture Syndrome.
    *   **C4d Capillary Staining**: Brown staining of peritubular capillaries under immunohistochemical evaluation of a renal biopsy, a pathognomonic marker of active complement classical pathway activation in acute antibody-mediated allograft rejection.
    *   **Coombs Positive Hemolysis**: Agglutination of red blood cells in the Direct Coombs test, confirming antibody-mediated red cell destruction.
*   **Associated Syndromes or Triggers:**
    *   **Transient Neonatal Myasthenia Gravis / Graves' Disease**: An experiment of nature where infants born to mothers with active Myasthenia Gravis or Graves' disease are born with transient muscle weakness or hyperthyroidism, respectively, due to the transplacental transfer of maternal IgG autoantibodies via the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn). Symptoms resolve within weeks as maternal IgG is naturally catabolized.
    *   **Evans Syndrome**: The co-occurrence of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) and autoimmune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) in the same individual.
    *   **Cold Agglutinin Disease**: A subtype of autoimmune hemolytic anemia mediated by IgM autoantibodies that bind to red blood cells at lower temperatures (such as in the peripheral circulation), causing complement activation, intravascular agglutination, and extravascular hemolysis. Often triggered by Mycoplasma pneumoniae infections or Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV).
    *   **Atypical Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (aHUS)**: Endothelial injury and microvascular thrombosis in the kidneys, triggered by alternative pathway complement hyperactivation due to mutations in Factor H, Factor I, or MCP.

#### Laboratory & Diagnostics
*   **CBC / Lymphocyte Subsets:**
    *   **Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)**: Shows normocytic or macrocytic anemia, low hemoglobin, elevated reticulocyte count (compensatory bone marrow response), and spherocytes on peripheral blood smear.
    *   **Autoimmune Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP)**: Reveals severe, isolated thrombocytopenia (low platelet count, often <50,000/μL, and sometimes <10,000/μL) with normal or elevated megakaryocytes in the bone marrow and giant platelets on peripheral smear.
    *   **TCRα Constant Region Mutation**: Rare congenital immunodeficiency with autoimmune cytopenias, showing a complete absence of circulating αβ T cells, with only γδ T cells present.
*   **Immunoglobulin Levels:**
    *   Standard immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgM, IgA) are typically normal or elevated in organ-specific autoimmune Type II hypersensitivities, though selective IgA deficiency or Common Variable Immunodeficiency (CVID) may coexist.
    *   Quantifiable circulating autoantibodies are identified using automated nephelometry, ELISA, or radioimmunoassay:
        *   Anti-AChR IgG in Myasthenia Gravis.
        *   Anti-TSH Receptor TSIs in Graves' disease.
        *   Anti-Desmoglein-1 and Anti-Desmoglein-3 IgG in Pemphigus vulgaris.
        *   Anti-GBM (NC1 domain of α3 collagen IV) IgG in Goodpasture syndrome.
        *   Anti-Intrinsic Factor IgG in Pernicious anemia.
        *   Cold agglutinin IgM titers in Cold Agglutinin Disease.
*   **Flow Cytometry Findings:**
    *   Used to confirm CD20 expression on B cells prior to Rituximab therapy.
    *   In PNH, flow cytometry is used to demonstrate the complete absence of GPI-anchored proteins (specifically CD55/DAF and CD59) on the surface of erythrocytes and granulocytes.
    *   Used to monitor B-cell depletion (CD19+ and CD20+ B cells) following Rituximab administration.
    *   Used in flow cytometric cross-matching prior to transplantation to detect if the recipient's serum contains preformed IgG antibodies that bind specifically to donor lymphocytes.
*   **Functional Assays & Genetic Testing:**
    *   **Direct Antiglobulin (Coombs) Test (DAT)**: The gold standard for diagnosing autoimmune hemolytic anemia. The patient's washed red blood cells (coated in vivo with autoantibodies) are mixed with antihuman globulin (Coombs reagent). Agglutination (clumping) confirms the presence of host IgG and/or C3b bound to the red cell membrane.
    *   **Indirect Antiglobulin (Coombs) Test**: Used to detect free, circulating anti-Rh or other red cell alloantibodies in maternal or recipient serum. The patient's serum is incubated with standardized donor red blood cells, followed by the addition of antihuman globulin. Agglutination confirms the presence of circulating free antibodies.
    *   **CH50 Hemolytic Complement Assay**: Measures classical and terminal complement pathway function. Determined by the dilution of patient serum required to cause 50% hemolysis of sheep erythrocytes precoated with complement-fixing antibodies. Low CH50 indicates complement consumption.
    *   **Renal or Skin Biopsy**: Under light microscopy and immunofluorescence:
        *   Goodpasture syndrome shows crescentic glomerulonephritis with smooth, linear IgG and C3 deposits along the basement membrane.
        *   Pemphigus vulgaris shows epidermal acantholysis with a characteristic "fishnet-like" or "chicken-wire" pattern of IgG deposition in the intercellular spaces.
    *   **Genetic Testing**: Next-generation sequencing is used to identify mutations in PIG-A (for PNH), TCRα, or genes encoding complement regulators (Factor H, MCP, Factor I) in atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome.

#### Management & Therapeutics
*   **Acute Management:**
    *   **Plasmapheresis (Plasma Exchange)**: Used as an emergent acute rescue therapy in severe, life-threatening Type II hypersensitivities (such as Goodpasture's pulmonary hemorrhage, acute antibody-mediated transplant rejection, or severe Myasthenia Gravis crisis). The patient's blood is pumped through a machine that removes pathogenic autoantibodies or alloantibodies from the plasma before returning the cellular components to the circulation.
    *   **Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG)**: Administered in high doses (1 to 2 g/kg) as acute immunomodulatory therapy in ITP, AIHA, and acute humoral transplant rejection.
    *   **Corticosteroids (High-Dose Methylprednisolone)**: Administered intravenously to rapidly suppress inflammation, block complement-mediated leukocyte recruitment, and downregulate macrophage Fc receptor expression.
    *   **Epinephrine**: Required immediately if an acute hemolytic transfusion reaction triggers severe shock and cardiorespiratory distress.
*   **Curative / Definitive Treatment:**
    *   **Splenectomy**: Highly beneficial and often curative in severe autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) and autoimmune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP). Removing the spleen eliminates: (1) the primary anatomical site where opsonized red cells and platelets are cleared by macrophage phagocytosis, and (2) a major lymphoid organ where autoantibody-secreting plasma cells reside.
    *   **Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation (HSCT)**: Curative for rare primary immunodeficiencies presenting with Type II autoimmune cytopenias, such as congenital TCRα deficiency.
    *   **Thymectomy**: Clinically beneficial and often leads to long-term remission in Myasthenia Gravis, particularly in patients with thymoma or thymic hyperplasia, as the thymus is the site of active autoantigen presentation and sensitization.
*   **Contraindications (e.g., live vaccines):**
    *   Avoid live viral or bacterial vaccines (such as MMR, varicella, oral polio, or BCG) in patients who are actively undergoing high-dose systemic immunosuppressive therapy (e.g., high-dose corticosteroids, Rituximab, or cyclophosphamide) or those with underlying cellular immunodeficiencies.
    *   Avoid further transfusions of ABO- or Rh-incompatible blood components.
*   **Immunosuppression or Desensitization Protocols:**
    *   **B-Cell Depletion (Rituximab)**: A chimeric monoclonal antibody targeting CD20 expressed on B cells (but not plasma cells). Rituximab depletes B cells via ADCC, complement-mediated lysis, and apoptosis, halting the generation of new autoantibody-producing B cells. Clinically approved for severe Myasthenia Gravis, Pemphigus vulgaris, Cold agglutinin disease, and acute transplant rejection.
    *   **Complement Inhibition (Eculizumab / Ravulizumab)**: Humanized monoclonal antibodies that bind to C5, preventing its cleavage to C5a and C5b, thereby completely blocking the assembly of the Membrane Attack Complex (C5b-9) and the release of the potent anaphylatoxin C5a. Curative for the intravascular hemolysis of PNH and approved for Myasthenia Gravis to prevent complement-mediated destruction of the motor endplate.
    *   **FcRn Blockers (Efgartigimod)**: An engineered IgG1 Fc fragment designed to bind with extremely high affinity to the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), blocking the recycling of host IgG. This accelerates the catabolism and rapid clearance of all circulating IgG antibodies, including the pathogenic autoantibodies, without altering albumin levels. Approved for the treatment of generalized Myasthenia Gravis.
    *   **Rituximab + Plasmapheresis + IVIG Protocol**: Used as a desensitization protocol to reduce preformed donor-specific anti-HLA antibodies in highly sensitized patients prior to undergoing an ABO-incompatible or HLA-mismatched organ transplantation.
    *   **Anti-RhD Prophylaxis (RhoGAM)**: Administering human anti-RhD IgG antibodies to Rh-negative mothers within 72 hours of childbirth (or during invasive prenatal procedures). The passive antibodies bind and clear any fetal Rh-positive red cells that entered the maternal circulation, preventing maternal sensitization and protecting subsequent pregnancies from erythroblastosis fetalis.

#### Trivia & Edge Cases
*   **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
    *   **"Linear Staining"**: The smooth, continuous ribbon-like immunofluorescence pattern pathognomonic of anti-basement membrane antibodies (Goodpasture's), distinguishing it from the coarse, "granular" lumpy-bumpy pattern of Type III circulating immune complex deposition.
    *   **"Frustrated Phagocytosis"**: The release of microbicidal reactive oxygen species and lysosomal enzymes by neutrophils unable to engulf a large, fixed antibody-coated tissue surface, causing localized tissue injury.
    *   **"Accommodation"**: The beneficial, protective adaptation of a transplanted organ (e.g., an ABO-incompatible graft) wherein endothelial cells survive and resist hyperacute rejection by upregulating complement regulatory proteins (DAF, CD59) even in the presence of circulating antigraft antibodies.
    *   **"Acantholysis"**: The loss of intercellular adhesion between keratinocytes, producing flaccid intraepidermal blisters in Pemphigus vulgaris.
    *   **"Warm vs. Cold Agglutinins"**: Warm AIHA is mediated by IgG antibodies that bind at 37°C and clear red cells in the spleen; Cold AIHA is mediated by IgM antibodies that bind at lower temperatures (<37°C) in the extremities, fixing complement and causing intravascular hemolysis or hepatic clearance.
*   **Exceptions to the Rule:**
    *   **Myasthenia Gravis Lack of Inflammation**: Unlike Goodpasture's or Pemphigus, some forms of Myasthenia Gravis cause muscle weakness solely via steric receptor blockade and downmodulation without triggering localized cell death or classical inflammatory cell infiltration, though complement activation plays a role in endplate damage.
    *   **Graves' Disease Agonistic Activation**: A unique exception where autoantibodies function as continuous agonists, stimulating the TSH receptor to drive hyperthyroidism, completely bypassing the normal feedback loop without causing cell death or tissue damage.
    *   **PNH Acquired Somatic Nature**: Unlike most genetic hematological diseases which are germline-encoded, PNH is caused by an acquired somatic mutation in the PIG-A gene within a hematopoietic stem cell clone, making it a non-hereditary genetic disease.
    *   **Direct Coombs Negative AIHA**: A rare clinical exception where a patient exhibits clinical hemolytic anemia that is antibody-mediated, but the Direct Coombs test is negative because the density of bound IgG is below the detection threshold of the standard DAT assay.
