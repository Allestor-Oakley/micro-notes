# Osteomyelitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Osteomyelitis is an inflammation and infection of the bone, bone marrow, and surrounding periosteal tissue characterized by progressive inflammatory bone destruction, localized osteonecrosis, vascular compromise, and the formation of devitalized bone fragments termed sequestra. It can be caused by bacteria, mycobacteria, or fungi and remains one of the most difficult-to-treat infectious diseases despite modern surgical and antimicrobial advancements.
- **Major Classifications (e.g., Acute vs. Chronic, Community vs. Nosocomial):**
  - Lew and Waldvogel Etiologic Classification: Categorizes osteomyelitis based on: (1) Duration of illness—Acute (symptoms <2 weeks, characterized by acute inflammatory edema and bone congestion without macroscopically dead bone) vs. Chronic (symptoms >4 weeks or persistent infection with osteonecrosis, sinus tracts, and sequestrum formation); (2) Mechanism of infection—Hematogenous (seeding via bloodstream, usually monomicrobial) vs. Contiguous (spread from adjacent soft tissue/joint infection or direct inoculation via trauma/surgery, usually polymicrobial); and (3) Presence of Vascular Insufficiency (e.g., in diabetic foot ulcers or peripheral artery disease).
  - Cierny-Mader Staging System: Staging system based on anatomic bone involvement and host physiological status to guide surgical management and prognosis:
    - Anatomic Types: Stage 1 (Medullary osteomyelitis; hematogenous origin within marrow cavity); Stage 2 (Superficial osteomyelitis; secondary to contiguous soft tissue defect/ulcer); Stage 3 (Localized osteomyelitis; well-demarcated cortical/medullary sequestrum where stability is maintained); Stage 4 (Diffuse osteomyelitis; through-and-through mechanical instability before or after debridement).
    - Physiologic Host Classes: Class A (Normal healthy host); Class B (Compromised host—systemic compromise (Bs), local vascular/tissue compromise (Bi), or both (Bis)); Class C (Prohibitive host—treatment morbidity is worse than the disease itself).
  - Anatomic / Clinical Subtypes:
    - Vertebral Osteomyelitis / Spondylodiskitis: Infection of the intervertebral disk space and adjacent vertebral bodies, predominantly hematogenous in origin or postsurgical/procedural.
    - Acute Hematogenous Long Bone Osteomyelitis: Occurs predominantly in prepubertal children, IV drug abusers, hemodialysis patients, and central venous catheter users; mainly involves long bone metaphyses (tibia, femur).
    - Osteomyelitis After Contaminated Open Fracture: Exogenous posttraumatic infection at the fracture site (occurs in 3% to 25% of open fractures, classified by Gustilo-Anderson types I to IIIC).
    - Osteomyelitis in Diabetes Mellitus / Vascular Insufficiency: Contiguous infection arising from chronic neuropathic/ischemic foot ulcers or pressure injuries.
    - Internal Fixation Device-Associated Osteomyelitis: Infection associated with fracture stabilization hardware (pins, plates, intramedullary nails, external fixators); classified as early (<=2 weeks post-op), delayed (3 to 10 weeks post-op), or late (>10 weeks post-op).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Systemic Host Risk Factors (Bs): Diabetes mellitus, malnutrition, advanced age, chronic renal failure / hemodialysis, chronic hypoxia, liver failure, systemic malignancy, sickle cell disease, rheumatoid arthritis, IV drug abuse, central venous catheterization, immunosuppressive therapy, and tobacco abuse.
  - Local Host Risk Factors (Bi): Peripheral vascular disease, chronic lymphedema, venous stasis, major vessel compromise, small vessel arteriolosclerosis, neuropathy, extensive scarring, radiation fibrosis, open fractures, and presence of orthopedic hardware / foreign bodies.
  - Epidemiology & Specific Populations:
    - Prepubertal Children: Highest incidence of acute hematogenous long bone osteomyelitis (metaphyseal involvement of tibia/femur).
    - Open Fractures: Infection rate ranges from 1.8% in Gustilo Type I to 16.1% in Gustilo Type IIIC open tibial shaft fractures.
    - Diabetes Mellitus: Peripheral neuropathy and micro/macrovascular disease lead to chronic foot ulcers; >20% of deep diabetic foot ulcers involve underlying contiguous osteomyelitis.
    - Hemodialysis Patients: Tunneled catheter bloodstream infections lead to metastatic skeletal seeding in 7% of cases (vertebral osteomyelitis, diskitis, septic arthritis).
    - People Who Inject Drugs (PWID): Increased risk for acute hematogenous long bone, vertebral, sternoclavicular, pubic, and clavicular osteomyelitis.
    - Sickle Cell Disease: Prevalence of osteomyelitis is ~12% in homozygous sickle cell anemia; vaso-occlusive bone infarction creates necrotic marrow beds susceptible to hematogenous seeding.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Staphylococcus aureus: The single most common microorganism isolated across all forms of acute and chronic osteomyelitis (>50% of overall cases). Includes both MSSA and MRSA (HA-MRSA and CA-MRSA, the latter often expressing Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL)).
  - Coagulase-Negative Staphylococci: Staphylococcus epidermidis and other CoNS account for a major fraction of chronic, hardware-associated, and contiguous osteomyelitis (>25% of cases).
  - Gram-Negative Aerobic Bacilli: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter species, Proteus mirabilis, Serratia marcescens, and Acinetobacter species (frequent in open fractures, diabetic foot osteomyelitis, PWID, and healthcare-associated infections).
  - Streptococci & Enterococci: Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A streptococci), Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B streptococci, especially in diabetics and neonates), Streptococcus pneumoniae, viridans streptococci, Streptococcus gallolyticus (S. bovis group), and Enterococcus faecalis / Enterococcus faecium.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups (e.g., Neonates, Elderly):**
  - Neonates: Group B Streptococcus (Streptococcus agalactiae), Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Candida species (especially with central venous lines).
  - Prepubertal Children: Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA and CA-MRSA), Kingella kingae (especially in children under 4 years of age), Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus pneumoniae (declining due to conjugate vaccines), and Haemophilus influenzae type b (historically common, now rare due to Hib vaccine).
  - Adults & Elderly: Staphylococcus aureus (high MRSA rate), coagulase-negative staphylococci, Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B streptococci, strongly linked to diabetes), E. coli, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterococcus species.
  - Diabetic / Vascular Foot Osteomyelitis: Frequently polymicrobial; combination of S. aureus, Group B streptococci, Enterobacteriaceae (E. coli, Proteus, Klebsiella), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and anaerobes (Finegoldia magna / Peptostreptococcus, Bacteroides fragilis, Clostridium species).
  - Sickle Cell Disease: Salmonella species (S. enterica serovars) and Staphylococcus aureus are the two predominant pathogens.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Fastidious & Zoonotic Bacteria: Kingella kingae, Brucella species (Brucella abortus, B. melitensis causing brucellar spondylodiskitis and sacroiliitis), Bartonella henselae, Eikenella corrodens, Pasteurella multocida (animal bites), Streptobacillus moniliformis, Actinomyces species (Actinomyces israelii causing cervicofacial and thoracic lumpy jaw osteomyelitis), Cutibacterium acnes (formerly Propionibacterium acnes, causing shoulder, spine, and post-arthroscopic hardware osteomyelitis), Tropheryma whipplei, Mycoplasma species, and Ureaplasma.
  - Mycobacteria: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Pott disease of the spine and tuberculous osteomyelitis of long bones/clavicle), Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (NTM) including Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) and M. xenopi (common NTMs in HIV/immunosuppressed vertebral osteomyelitis), Mycobacterium marinum (post-water exposure), Mycobacterium fortuitum, Mycobacterium chelonae, Mycobacterium abscessus, Mycobacterium kansasii, Mycobacterium terrae / M. arupense, and Mycobacterium bovis (complication of intravesical BCG).
  - Fungi: Candida species (Candida albicans, Candida parapsilosis in central lines/PWID), Aspergillus species (A. fumigatus, A. flavus, A. nidulans), Dimorphic fungi (Blastomyces dermatitidis (associated with juxta-articular osteomyelitis), Coccidioides immitis/posadasii (spinal and long bone coccidioidomycosis), Histoplasma capsulatum, Sporothrix schenckii, Paracoccidioides), Mucorales (Rhizopus), Scedosporium apiospermum, Lomentospora prolificans, Cladophialophora arxii, and Cryptococcus neoformans.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Routes of Entry:
    1. Hematogenous Route: Bloodstream seeding from distant foci (e.g., endocarditis, intravascular catheters, urinary tract infection, cutaneous abscess, IV drug abuse). Predominates in prepubertal long bone metaphyses and adult vertebral bodies.
    2. Contiguous Route: Direct extension from adjacent soft tissue infections, deep ulcers (e.g., diabetic foot ulcers, decubitus pressure ulcers), or infected joints.
    3. Direct Inoculation: Direct entry into bone matrix following open compound fractures, penetrating trauma, orthopedic surgery, or internal fixation hardware placement.
  - Metaphyseal Susceptibility in Children: Metaphyseal nutrient capillaries form sharp, non-anastomosing hair-pin loops beneath the epiphyseal growth plate, dumping into large, low-pressure venous sinusoids with slow, turbulent blood flow. Capillaries lack phagocytic lining cells. Minor trauma creates small metaphyseal hematomas and vascular occlusion, allowing transient bacteremia to seed the avascular necrotic bone bed.
  - Vertebral / Disk Space Susceptibility in Adults: Two adjacent vertebral endplates receive arterial supply from common segmental arteries. Hematogenous bacteria seed the rich vascular subchondral bone of the vertebral endplates, extending secondarily into the avascular intervertebral disk space (spondylodiskitis).
  - Foreign Body & Biofilm Dynamics: Biomaterials (implants, pins, plates) lower the minimal infecting bacterial inoculum by >10,000-fold. S. aureus expresses high-affinity MSCRAMMs (microbial surface components recognizing adhesive matrix molecules) binding fibronectin, laminin, collagen, and sialoglycoproteins. Adherent bacteria switch to a sessile, biofilm-encased phenotype (including phenotypic small colony variants (SCVs)) with reduced metabolic activity, impaired innate immune clearance, and resistance to standard antimicrobials that target active cell wall synthesis.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Inflammatory Progression & Bone Necrosis: Bacterial proliferation triggers acute neutrophilic infiltration, localized edema, increased intramedullary pressure, tissue acidosis, and local vascular thrombosis.
  - Sequestrum & Involucrum Formation: Compromised medullary and periosteal blood supply leads to avascular bone necrosis. Detached, devitalized segments of dead bone isolated from blood supply and host defenses form a sequestrum. In response, periosteal osteoblasts synthesize a surrounding sheath of new reactive cortical bone termed an involucrum.
  - Sinus Tracts & Brodie Abscess: Inflammatory pus breaches cortical bone through Volkmann and Haversian canals, elevating the periosteum to form subperiosteal abscesses and draining sinus tracts (fistulae) to the skin surface. Chronic localized bone abscesses contained within fibrous sclerotic borders are known as Brodie abscesses.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Acute Hematogenous Osteomyelitis (Children): Rapid onset of localized bone pain, high fever, chills, irritability, lethargy, pseudoparalysis, and refusal to bear weight or move the affected limb.
  - Chronic Osteomyelitis: Insidious presentation with long-standing, dull, aching bone pain, localized swelling, low-grade fever or absence of fever, induration, non-healing wounds, persistent purulent discharge from draining sinus tracts, or recurrent flare-ups over months to years.
  - Vertebral Osteomyelitis / Spondylodiskitis: Localized, insidious back or neck pain and spinal tenderness present in ~90% of cases. Fever is absent in >50% of patients. Pain is unremitting, exacerbated by movement/weight-bearing, worsens at night, and may radiate radicularly to the abdomen, groin, or legs. Motor or sensory neurological deficits occur in 15% of patients due to spinal cord or nerve root compression.
  - Diabetic Foot Osteomyelitis: Chronic neuropathic foot ulcer (often present >2 weeks, surface area >2 cm2), deep tissue exposure, mild localized pain (blunted by sensory neuropathy), and absence of prominent fever or systemic toxicity.
  - Posttraumatic / Hardware Osteomyelitis: Persistent wound drainage, local erythema, wound dehiscence, nonunion of fracture site, early mechanical implant loosening, or chronic pain.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Focal Physical Findings: Point tenderness on palpation over involved bone or vertebral spinous process, local warmth, erythema, edema, induration, fluctuating soft tissue masses, and draining sinus tracts.
  - Neurological Assessment (Spine): Motor weakness, sensory loss, hyperreflexia, clonus, bowel/bladder dysfunction, or radiculopathy in vertebral osteomyelitis with epidural abscess or cord compression.
  - Pediatric Findings: Antalgic gait or complete refusal to bear weight; limping; holding affected limb motionless (pseudoparalysis).
  - Probe-to-Bone Test (Diabetic Foot): Inserting a sterile metallic probe into a chronic foot ulcer; feeling a hard, metallic, gritty surface at the base of the ulcer indicates exposed bone (high positive predictive value).
  - Psoas Sign: Pain on passive extension of the hip or relief of back/abdominal pain with hip flexion, indicating psoas muscle abscess secondary to vertebral osteomyelitis.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Positive Probe-to-Bone Test: Palpable hard, gritty bone with a sterile metallic probe through a chronic diabetic foot ulcer.
  - Draining Sinus Tract with Visible Bone: A chronic cutaneous sinus tract communicating directly with underlying bone or sequestrum.
  - Brodie Abscess: Radiographic finding of a well-circumscribed, lytic metaphyseal lesion surrounded by a thick, dense wall of sclerotic bone (classic in distal tibia).
  - Vertebral Endplate Erosions & Disk Space Collapse: Paired vertebral endplate destruction with narrowing/destruction of the intervening intervertebral disk on spine imaging.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis with a neutrophilic left shift is present in acute hematogenous osteomyelitis and sepsis, but WBC count is normal in <50% of vertebral cases and most chronic/diabetic foot osteomyelitis cases.
  - Inflammatory Markers (ESR & CRP): Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) is elevated in >90% of vertebral and acute osteomyelitis cases. C-reactive Protein (CRP) rises rapidly within 24 hours of infection and falls rapidly with effective therapy. A >=25% decrease in ESR and CRP at 4 to 8 weeks correlates with successful response to treatment.
  - Blood Cultures: Two sets of blood cultures should be drawn in all suspected cases prior to antimicrobial therapy; positive in 50% to 70% of acute hematogenous cases in children, ~50% of vertebral osteomyelitis cases, and up to 90% in hemodialysis/PWID cases, providing a non-invasive microbiologic diagnosis.
- **Imaging Modalities (e.g., X-ray, CT, Ultrasound):**
  - Plain Film Radiography: Initial screening test. Insensitive in acute infection; osseous changes (periosteal reaction, cortical erosion, lytic lesions, osteopenia) appear 10 to 14 days after symptom onset. Highly useful in chronic osteomyelitis to visualize sequestra, involucrum, fracture nonunion, and hardware loosening.
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): Modality of choice and standard of care for diagnosing osteomyelitis. High sensitivity (90%) and specificity (80-90%). Demonstrates early bone marrow edema (decreased signal on T1, increased signal on T2/STIR), soft tissue abscesses, intervertebral disk space involvement, epidural abscess, and spinal cord compression. Metal Artifact Suppression sequences (MAVRIC) allow MRI use near metallic hardware.
  - Computed Tomography (CT): Standard of care for delineating cortical bone destruction, sequestra, involucrum, periosteal gas, nonunion, and guiding percutaneous needle biopsy. Superior to MRI for fine cortical bone detail.
  - Ultrasonography: Useful in pediatric patients and superficial bones to detect subperiosteal fluid collections, soft tissue abscesses, joint effusions, and to guide needle aspiration.
  - Nuclear Medicine & PET Imaging: Three-phase bone scintigraphy (99mTc-MDP), Gallium-67 citrate scan (100% sensitive for spondylodiskitis), Indium-111 labeled WBCs, and 18F-FDG PET/CT. FDG PET/CT offers superior spatial resolution and accuracy, particularly in chronic posttraumatic osteomyelitis and when MRI is contraindicated.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Deep Tissue / Bone Biopsy Culture: Definitive microbiologic diagnosis requires deep surgical tissue or bone biopsy specimens submitted for aerobic, anaerobic, mycobacterial, and fungal cultures. Superficial swab cultures from draining wounds or sinus tracts are unreliable and misleading (except for S. aureus and MRSA, which have higher correlation). Two consecutive deep sinus tract cultures yielding the identical pathogen predict the true bone pathogen.
  - Image-Guided Percutaneous Biopsy: CT- or fluoroscopy-guided needle aspiration/biopsy of infected disk space, vertebral body, or long bone metaphysis. Specificity is 90-100%, though sensitivity is variable (30-70%); a second percutaneous biopsy should be performed if the first biopsy is culture-negative before embarking on empirical therapy.
  - Histopathology: Bone biopsy demonstrates acute neutrophilic exudate, chronic plasma cell/lymphocytic infiltration, osteonecrosis, lack of osteocytes in lacunae, or caseating/noncaseating granulomas (in TB/fungal osteomyelitis).
  - Molecular Diagnostics (PCR): Broad-range 16S rRNA gene PCR and multiplex real-time PCR assays on bone tissue, purulent aspirates, or sonication fluid from removed implants detect fastidious organisms (K. kingae, T. whipplei, Mycobacteria, anaerobes) and identify pathogens in culture-negative cases resulting from prior antibiotic exposure.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Histopathologic confirmation of osteonecrosis and inflammation combined with positive isolation of the microorganism from a sterilely obtained deep bone biopsy or surgical tissue culture remains the definitive gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Hemodynamic Stabilization: Resuscitation with IV fluids and vasopressors if osteomyelitis is complicated by severe sepsis, septic shock, or toxic shock syndrome.
  - Immobilization & Spine Protection: Rigid external bracing (corset or body brace) and spinal precautions for unstable vertebral osteomyelitis; limb splinting/immobilization in functional position.
  - Antimicrobial Hold Protocol: All antimicrobials MUST be held prior to obtaining bone biopsy, CT-guided aspirates, or intraoperative deep surgical cultures, UNLESS the patient has concomitant sepsis, hemodynamic instability, or rapidly progressive neurological deficits.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Acute Long Bone / Vertebral / Posttraumatic Osteomyelitis (Empiric Adult): Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h (targeting serum trough 15-20 mcg/mL or AUC/MIC 400-600) PLUS Cefepime 2 g IV q8h OR Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h OR Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h.
  - Pediatric Acute Hematogenous Osteomyelitis: Cefazolin 25-37.5 mg/kg IV q6h (100-150 mg/kg/day) or Nafcillin/Oxacillin 50 mg/kg IV q6h. If CA-MRSA prevalence is high (>10-20%): Vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV q6h or Clindamycin 10-13.3 mg/kg IV q8h (40 mg/kg/day).
  - Diabetic Foot / Vascular Osteomyelitis (Empiric Broad-Spectrum): Vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg IV q12h PLUS Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h OR Cefepime 2 g IV q8h + Metronidazole 500 mg PO/IV q8h.
  - Contaminated Open Fractures (Prophylaxis/Early Prevention):
    - Gustilo Type I & II: Cefazolin 1-2 g IV q8h (or Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h) for 24-72 hours.
    - Gustilo Type III: Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h or Amoxicillin-clavulanate for 24-72 hours.
- **Alternative Regimens (e.g., for Penicillin Allergy):**
  - Gram-Positive / MRSA Coverage (Vancomycin Allergy / Intolerance / Renal Impairment / VRE): Daptomycin 6 to 10 mg/kg IV q24h OR Linezolid 600 mg IV/PO q12h OR Tedizolid 200 mg IV/PO q24h OR Ceftaroline 600 mg IV q12h OR Telavancin 10 mg/kg IV q24h. Long-acting lipoglycopeptides: Dalbavancin 1500 mg IV single dose or 1000 mg IV on Day 1 + 500 mg IV on Day 8.
  - Gram-Negative Coverage (Severe Beta-Lactam / Penicillin Allergy): Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Imipenem 500 mg IV q6h OR Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q8h / 750 mg PO q12h OR Levofloxacin 750 mg IV/PO q24h OR Aztreonam 2 g IV q8h.
  - Targeted Directed Therapies:
    - Methicillin-Susceptible S. aureus (MSSA): Cefazolin 2 g IV q8h or Nafcillin/Oxacillin 2 g IV q4h. (Oral step-down: Cephalexin 1000 mg PO q6h or Dicloxacillin 500 mg PO q6h).
    - MRSA Oral Step-Down (after initial IV response): TMP-SMX 1-2 DS tabs PO q8-12h, Doxycycline 100 mg PO q12h, Minocycline 100 mg PO q12h, or Linezolid 600 mg PO q12h.
    - Pseudomonas aeruginosa: Cefepime 2 g IV q8h, Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h, or Meropenem 1 g IV q8h; oral step-down to Ciprofloxacin 750 mg PO q12h.
    - Biofilm-Active Staphylococcal Hardware/Implant Infection: Addition of Rifampin 300 mg PO q12h or 600 mg PO q24h in combination with a primary antistaphylococcal partner drug (initiated once surgical wound is completely dry).
  - Treatment Duration:
    - Standard Adult Native Osteomyelitis: Total of 4 to 6 weeks of targeted parenteral/oral antimicrobial therapy.
    - Pediatric Acute Hematogenous Long Bone Osteomyelitis: Short-course 2 to 3 weeks total therapy (IV converted to oral early once CRP declines and clinical improvement occurs).
    - Vertebral Osteomyelitis: 6 weeks total therapy (6 weeks non-inferior to 12 weeks; extend to >8 weeks if MRSA, ESRD, or undrained psoas abscess).
    - Diabetic Foot Osteomyelitis (Post-Amputation): 2 to 5 days if clean non-infected margin remains; 2 to 4 weeks if residual soft tissue infection; 6 weeks if residual necrotic infected bone remains without resection.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions (e.g., Drainage, Debridement):**
  - Surgical Debridement: Radical excision of all macroscopic sequestra, necrotic bone, and devitalized soft tissue back to bleeding, viable host bone ("paprika sign"). Essential for curing chronic, stage 3/4, or contiguous osteomyelitis.
  - Abscess Drainage: CT-guided catheter aspiration or open surgical drainage of paravertebral, psoas, epidural, subperiosteal, or soft tissue abscesses.
  - Dead Space Management & Reconstruction: Filling osseous defects using muscle/skin flaps, local antibiotic-impregnated polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) beads, or biodegradable calcium sulfate carriers containing tobramycin/vancomycin. Delayed orthopedic reconstruction (Ilizarov distraction osteogenesis, bone grafting).
  - Hardware Management (Fixation / Implants):
    - Early / Acute Posttraumatic Infection (<2 weeks) with Stable Hardware: Debridement, irrigation, hardware retention, and 3 months of biofilm-active antimicrobial suppression until bone fracture unites.
    - Chronic / Delayed Hardware Infection (>3-10 weeks): Removal of all hardware after fracture consolidation; or two-stage hardware exchange.
  - Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy (HBOT): Adjunctive measure for chronic refractory osteomyelitis; enhances oxygen-dependent PMN killing, fibroblast activity, and angiogenesis in hypovascular bone beds.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Local Spread: Subperiosteal abscess formation, contiguous pyomyositis, deep fascial space abscesses, psoas abscess, and contiguous septic arthritis (especially in neonates where metaphyseal vessels cross the growth plate, and in intra-articular metaphyses like the proximal femur/hip, proximal humerus/shoulder, radial neck/elbow, and distal fibula/ankle).
  - Spinal Complications: Epidural abscess, subdural empyema, spinal cord compression, acute paraplegia, quadriplegia, or radiculopathy.
  - Systemic: Bacteremia, severe sepsis, septic shock, metastatic embolic seeding (infective endocarditis, brain abscess, septic pulmonary emboli), and acute kidney injury from nephrotoxic antimicrobial regimens (e.g., high-trough vancomycin).
- **Long-Term Sequelae:**
  - Bone Sequelae: Chronic refractory osteomyelitis with recurrent sinus tract drainage, pathological fractures through weakened necrotic bone, fracture nonunion, pseudoarthrosis, and permanent bony defects.
  - Pediatric Skeletal Deformities: Damage to epiphyseal growth plate leading to premature physeal closure, limb-length discrepancy, and angular bone deformities.
  - Neurological & Structural Sequelae: Permanent neurological deficits (paraplegia, neurogenic bladder), chronic spinal instability, kyphotic deformity (gibbus deformity), and spontaneous intervertebral body fusion.
  - Malignant Transformation: Marjolin ulcer (squamous cell carcinoma developing within chronic, long-standing epithelialized sinus tracts of chronic osteomyelitis, typically >20-30 years duration); rare secondary amyloidosis (AA amyloidosis).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Sequestrum: A piece of dead, avascular bone that has become separated during the process of necrosis from normal sound bone.
  - Involucrum: A sheath of new, reactive cortical bone formed by periosteum around a sequestrum.
  - Cloaca: An opening or defect in the involucrum through which purulent exudate and sequestra drain from the medullary cavity to soft tissues.
  - Brodie Abscess: Chronic localized bone abscess, classically presenting as a single, subacute/chronic lytic metaphyseal lesion in the distal tibia of young patients (<25 years).
  - Probe-to-Bone Test: Palpated hard, metallic, gritty surface at the base of a deep diabetic ulcer using a sterile metallic probe.
  - Paprika Sign: Microscopic pinpoint bleeding from healthy cortical/cancellous bone during surgical debridement, signaling viable tissue margins.
  - Marjolin Ulcer: Malignant transformation into aggressive squamous cell carcinoma arising within a chronic, long-standing osteomyelitis sinus tract.
  - SAPHO Syndrome: Acronym for Synovitis, Acne, Plantar pustulosis, Hyperostosis, and Osteitis (autoinflammatory non-bacterial bone disease).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Sinus Tract Cultures vs. Deep Bone Cultures: Sinus tract swab cultures do NOT correlate with deep bone pathogens (and should be avoided), EXCEPT for Staphylococcus aureus and MRSA, where two consecutive deep sinus tract cultures showing the identical organism predict the true pathogen with high reliability.
  - Ciprofloxacin & Fracture Healing: Ciprofloxacin and other fluoroquinolones inhibit experimental osteoblast activity and fracture healing in animal models, whereas vancomycin and cefazolin do not; quinolones must be used with caution when bone healing needs to occur concomitantly with antimicrobial administration.
  - Pediatric Short-Course Antibiotic Efficacy: In acute hematogenous long bone osteomyelitis in children, a short course of 2 to 3 weeks of antimicrobial therapy (converted early to oral) is as effective as 6 weeks of intravenous therapy.
  - Vertebral Osteomyelitis Treatment Duration: A 6-week course of antimicrobial therapy is non-inferior to a 12-week course for native pyogenic vertebral osteomyelitis (unless complicated by MRSA, ESRD, or undrained psoas abscess).
  - Normal WBC Count in Spine & Chronic Osteomyelitis: White blood cell count is normal in >50% of patients with pyogenic vertebral osteomyelitis and chronic osteomyelitis; a normal WBC count CANNOT be used to exclude bone infection.
  - Post-Treatment MRI Fallacy in Vertebral Osteomyelitis: Follow-up MRI at the end of successful therapy often demonstrates persistent or worsening bone marrow edema and contrast enhancement despite complete clinical resolution; routine follow-up MRI should NOT be used to judge treatment response unless clinical or inflammatory markers deteriorate.
