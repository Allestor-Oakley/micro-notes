# Infectious arthritis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Infectious arthritis (also known as septic, suppurative, or pyogenic arthritis) is an infection of one or more joint spaces caused by bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites. It is characterized by acute or chronic inflammation of the synovial membrane, purulent or inflammatory joint effusion, and potential for rapid destruction of articular cartilage and subchondral bone. Acute bacterial arthritis is considered a medical and rheumatologic emergency due to its potential for permanent loss of joint function, severe physical disability, and mortality.
- **Major Classifications (e.g., Acute vs. Chronic, Community vs. Nosocomial):**
  - Acute Bacterial Arthritis: Abrupt onset over hours to days, usually monoarticular (80% to 90% of native joint cases), most commonly affecting large weight-bearing joints (knee > hip > shoulder > wrist > ankle). Characterized by purulent synovial fluid, high neutrophil counts, and rapid cartilage degradation.
  - Gonococcal Arthritis / Disseminated Gonococcal Infection (DGI): Categorized into two distinct clinical syndromes: (1) Bacteremic / Dermatitis-Tenosynovitis Syndrome, characterized by the triad of migratory polyarthralgias, tenosynovitis, and dermatitis without frank suppurative joint effusions; and (2) Suppurative Gonococcal Arthritis, presenting as purulent monoarthritis or oligoarthritis without skin lesions or tenosynovitis.
  - Viral Arthritis: Acute, symmetrical, usually self-limited polyarticular arthritis occurring concurrently with systemic febrile illness, viral exanthems, or myalgias (e.g., Parvovirus B19, Chikungunya virus, Rubella, Hepatitis B and C, Ross River virus, O'nyong-nyong virus). Parvovirus B19 and Chikungunya may lead to prolonged or chronic persistent joint symptoms.
  - Chronic Infectious Arthritis: Insidious onset over weeks to months, typically monoarticular or oligoarticular involving large peripheral weight-bearing joints, caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), dimorphic fungi (Blastomyces, Coccidioides, Sporothrix, Histoplasma), opportunistic yeasts (Candida, Cryptococcus), or molds (Aspergillus, Scedosporium).
  - Septic Bursitis: Infection of superficial subcutaneous bursae (olecranon > prepatellar > infrapatellar/trochanteric), distinguished from intra-articular septic arthritis by localized extra-articular fluid accumulation and pain exacerbated by joint flexion rather than extension.
  - Community-Acquired vs. Healthcare-Associated / Nosocomial: Community-acquired cases feature MSSA, CA-MRSA, Streptococcus pyogenes, or S. pneumoniae. Healthcare-associated or postsurgical cases follow joint procedures (arthroscopy, arthroplasty, intra-articular steroid injections) or nosocomial bacteremic seeding, featuring HA-MRSA, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae, or Cutibacterium acnes.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Epidemiology: Incidence ranges from 2 to 12 cases per 100,000 person-years in western Europe and North America, escalating to 28 to 70 cases per 100,000 per year in elderly individuals and patients with rheumatoid arthritis. Accounts for 8% to 27% of emergency department presentations for acute joint pain. Overall adult mortality ranges from 7% to 15%, increasing up to 30% to 50% in patients with severe comorbidities or polyarticular involvement. Up to 50% of survivors suffer permanent loss of joint function or mobility.
  - Major Predisposing Risk Factors: Preexisting joint disease (rheumatoid arthritis (doubles risk, accounts for ~75% of S. aureus cases), crystal-induced arthropathies (gout and pseudogout)), advanced age (>65 years), diabetes mellitus, chronic renal failure/hemodialysis, previous joint surgery or arthroplasty, penetrating joint trauma, intravenous drug use, endocarditis, and immunosuppression (organ/bone marrow transplant, systemic corticosteroids, disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), anti-TNF biological therapies (which double the hazard ratio to 2.3)).
  - Minor Risk Factors: Osteoarthritis, Charcot neuropathic arthropathy, collagen vascular diseases, chronic liver disease, alcoholism, sickle cell disease, malignancy, hypogammaglobulinemia, intra-articular corticosteroid injections, skin lesions/infections, and low socioeconomic status. Up to 22% of patients have no identifiable predisposing risk factor.
  - Specific High-Risk Groups: People who inject drugs (PWID) account for 11% to 15% of joint sepsis, with high involvement of atypical fibrocartilaginous/axial joints (sternoclavicular, sacroiliac, pubic symphysis, cervical facet joints), elevated rates of MRSA, P. aeruginosa, polymicrobial infection, concomitant endocarditis, and higher mortality (odds ratio 2.86). Young, sexually active adults, menstruating or pregnant females, individuals with terminal complement deficiencies (C5-C8), SLE, or those receiving eculizumab are at elevated risk for DGI. Immigrants from endemic regions, alcoholics, and immunocompromised hosts are at elevated risk for tuberculous arthritis.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Gram-Positive Bacteria: Staphylococcus aureus is the predominant organism across all adult native joint infections (37% to 65% of cases overall, up to 75% in rheumatoid arthritis patients, and 50% to 90% in PWID). Includes MSSA, HA-MRSA, and CA-MRSA (frequently expressing Panton-Valentine leukocidin). Streptococcus species account for ~22% of adult native joint cases: Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A streptococci, 8%), Streptococcus pneumoniae (7%), Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B streptococci, 3%), and Lancefield Groups C, F, and G streptococci (5%). Coagulase-negative staphylococci cause 4% of cases (Staphylococcus lugdunensis is notable for native joint virulence).
  - Gram-Negative Bacteria: Gram-negative bacilli cause 5% to 20% of cases overall: Escherichia coli (4%), Haemophilus influenzae (5%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (2%), Salmonella species (1%), Neisseria meningitidis (1%), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter species, Proteus species, and Serratia marcescens. Neisseria gonorrhoeae accounts for ~3% of compiled adult cases.
  - Polymicrobial & Anaerobic: Polymicrobial infection occurs in up to 10% of cases. Anaerobes and miscellaneous organisms account for ~6% of cases.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups (e.g., Neonates, Elderly):**
  - Neonates: Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B streptococci), Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and other Gram-negative enteric bacilli.
  - Young Children (<4 years): Kingella kingae is the primary cause of septic arthritis in children under 4 years of age attending daycare; Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA and CA-MRSA), Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Haemophilus influenzae type b (rare in vaccinated populations).
  - Children & Adolescents: Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA and CA-MRSA), Streptococcus pyogenes, Kingella kingae (<4 years), Neisseria gonorrhoeae (sexually active adolescents).
  - Young Adults (Sexually Active): Neisseria gonorrhoeae (Disseminated Gonococcal Infection / suppurative gonococcal arthritis), Staphylococcus aureus.
  - Elderly Patients (>65 years): Staphylococcus aureus (high proportion of MRSA), Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B streptococci, particularly with diabetes or malignancy), Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Gram-negative bacilli (E. coli, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas aeruginosa).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Fastidious / Zoonotic / Bite Bacteria: Pasteurella multocida and Capnocytophaga species (cat and dog bites); Eikenella corrodens, Fusobacterium nucleatum, and viridans group streptococci (human bites); Streptobacillus moniliformis (rat-bite fever); Pantoea agglomerans (plant thorn punctures); Cutibacterium acnes (formerly Propionibacterium acnes, causing shoulder joint and postsurgical/arthroscopic shoulder infections); Brucella species (brucellar sacroiliitis); Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme arthritis); Tropheryma whipplei (Whipple disease); Mycoplasma hominis (postpartum women); Ureaplasma urealyticum; Streptococcus suis (exposure to pigs/pork); Streptococcus iniae (aquaculture fish handling); Streptococcus gallolyticus / S. bovis group (associated with endocarditis and colonic malignancy); Listeria monocytogenes; Burkholderia pseudomallei; and Nocardia species.
  - Mycobacteria: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (chronic granulomatous monoarthritis / Pott disease of peripheral joints); Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (NTM) including Mycobacterium marinum (hand/wrist tenosynovitis after fresh/saltwater or marine animal exposure, requiring incubation at 30°C), Mycobacterium avium-intracellulare complex (MAC), Mycobacterium kansasii, Mycobacterium xenopi, Mycobacterium terrae, Mycobacterium fortuitum, Mycobacterium chelonae, Mycobacterium abscessus, Mycobacterium haemophilum; and Mycobacterium leprae (type 2 lepra reaction / erythema nodosum leprosum or neuropathic Charcot joints).
  - Fungi: Candida species (C. albicans, C. parapsilosis, C. glabrata, C. tropicalis, C. krusei, C. lusitaniae; common in hemodialysis, surgical, or immunocompromised patients); Cryptococcus neoformans / C. gattii; Dimorphic fungi (Blastomyces dermatitidis (associated with juxta-articular osteomyelitis), Coccidioides immitis/posadasii ("desert rheumatism" polyarthralgia or chronic destructive monoarthritis), Histoplasma capsulatum, Sporothrix schenckii, Paracoccidioides brasiliensis); Molds (Aspergillus fumigatus, A. flavus, A. niger, Fusarium species, Scedosporium apiospermum / Pseudallescheria boydii, Lomentospora prolificans, Mucorales/Rhizopus, Exserohilum species (outbreak associated with contaminated methylprednisolone injections), Curvularia, Exophiala, Trichosporon asahii).
  - Viruses: Parvovirus B19 (slapped-cheek rash in children, lacy reticular rash and symmetrical polyarthritis in adults), Chikungunya virus (severe contorting symmetrical polyarthritis, persistent in 20% to 40%), Rubella virus (natural infection or post-RA 27/3 vaccination), Hepatitis B virus (pre-icteric serum sickness prodrome with polyarthralgias), Hepatitis C virus (cryoglobulinemia or RA-like nonerosive arthritis), Ross River virus, O'nyong-nyong virus, Dengue, Zika, EBV, CMV, HIV-1, HTLV-1 (HTLV-1-associated arthropathy mimicking RA).
  - Parasites & Algae: Helminths, Filariae (Onchocerca, Loa loa), Prototheca wickerhamii (opportunistic achlorophyllous alga causing septic olecranon bursitis or monoarthritis).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Routes of Infection: (1) Hematogenous seeding during overt or occult bacteremia (most common route for native joints and axial joint sepsis); (2) Direct inoculation via penetrating trauma, surgery, arthroscopy, arthrocentesis, or intra-articular corticosteroid injections (including contaminated medication outbreaks); and (3) Contiguous spread from adjacent soft tissue infection or bone (e.g., osteomyelitis, diabetic foot ulcers, retroperitoneal abscess spreading to the hip).
  - Synovial Seeding: The synovial membrane is highly vascular and lacks a limiting basement membrane, making it extremely vulnerable to hematogenous bacterial deposition under low-shear fluid flow conditions.
  - Bacterial Adherence: Bacterial attachment is facilitated by host extracellular matrix proteins (fibronectin, collagen, laminin, elastin, hyaluronic acid) exposed during traumatic, surgical, or degenerative joint injury. Pathogens express specific surface adhesins (e.g., S. aureus MSCRAMMs such as fibronectin-binding protein, collagen-binding protein, protein A, and fibrinogen-binding adhesins).
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Inflammatory Cascade: Bacterial proliferation in synovial fluid triggers an intense host inflammatory response. Bacterial DNA CpG motifs, exotoxins (e.g., PVL, alpha-toxin, RTX toxins), and peptidoglycans activate macrophages and synovial cells to release pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs).
  - Cartilage & Bone Destruction: Rapid recruitment of polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) releases lysosomal enzymes, elastase, and reactive oxygen species. High intra-articular pressure caused by purulent exudate compromises synovial capillary perfusion, leading to ischemic necrosis, rapid degradation of articular cartilage proteoglycans and collagen, subchondral bone erosion, and permanent joint ankylosis or deformity if not urgently drained.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Acute Bacterial Native Joint Arthritis: Abrupt onset (over hours to days) of severe, excruciating joint pain, joint swelling, redness, warmth, and marked limitation of range of motion. Joint motion is excruciatingly painful. Systemic symptoms include fever, chills, and malaise in 50% to 70% of patients, though high fever with rigors may be totally absent in 30% to 50% of cases.
  - Disseminated Gonococcal Infection (DGI): Presents with two distinct clinical patterns: (1) Bacteremic / Dermatitis-Tenosynovitis Syndrome: Migratory polyarthralgias or polyarthritis, asymmetric joint pain, tenosynovitis (predominantly dorsal hands, wrists, fingers, ankles), dermatitis, moderate fever, chills, and malaise; and (2) Suppurative Gonococcal Arthritis: Acute purulent monoarthritis or oligoarthritis (knees, wrists, ankles) without skin lesions or tenosynovitis.
  - Subacute / Chronic Infectious Arthritis (Mycobacterial/Fungal): Insidious onset over weeks to months of indolent joint swelling, mild to moderate joint pain, stiffness during movement, persistent joint effusions, and progressive loss of function, typically lacking systemic fever or acute toxicity.
  - Viral Arthritis: Acute onset of symmetrical polyarthralgias or polyarthritis (wrists, ankles, small joints of hands/feet) occurring concurrently with systemic febrile illness, fatigue, myalgias, and viral exanthems.
  - Septic Bursitis: Pain, swelling, and erythema localized over subcutaneous bursae (olecranon, prepatellar). Pain is characteristically exacerbated by joint flexion (which increases bursal pressure) rather than joint extension.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Peripheral Joints: Marked focal joint tenderness, joint enlargement/effusion, local hyperthermia, diffuse periarticular erythema, and severe restriction of active and passive range of motion due to pain. A distant focus of infection (skin lesion, ulcer, soft tissue infection) is present in up to 50% of cases.
  - Deep / Axial Joints (Sternoclavicular, Sacroiliac, Pubic Symphysis): Findings may be subtle and limited to focal tenderness over the involved joint. Pain is intensely exacerbated by weight-bearing, ambulation, or movement, often radiating to the back, groin, hip, or leg.
  - Pediatric Hip Septic Arthritis: Child characteristically holds the affected hip flexed, abducted, and externally rotated, and strongly resists any attempt at passive movement. Refusal to bear weight.
  - DGI Skin Lesions: Sparse (few in number), painless, nonpruritic macules, papules, and pustules on an erythematous base, often developing central necrosis, located predominantly on the distal extremities, palms, and soles.
  - Viral Exanthems: Parvovirus B19 presents with bright red "slapped cheeks" in children and a faint, lacy reticular rash on the trunk and extremities in adults. Chikungunya presents with a maculopapular rash in ~50% of cases.
  - Special Populations: In elderly patients, diabetics, and patients with rheumatoid arthritis (especially those on steroids or anti-TNF agents), classic physical signs and fever are frequently blunted or absent; presentation may mimic a minor flare of underlying rheumatoid arthritis or osteoarthritis.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - DGI Triad: The clinical triad of dermatitis (necrotic pustules on erythematous base), tenosynovitis (dorsal hands/wrists/ankles), and migratory polyarthralgias in a sexually active young adult.
  - Flexion vs. Extension Bursal Pain Sign: Elbow pain exacerbated by flexion indicates septic olecranon bursitis (increased bursal pressure), whereas elbow pain exacerbated by extension indicates septic arthritis of the elbow joint (increased intra-articular pressure).
  - Child Hip Position: Pediatric septic hip fixed in flexion, abduction, and external rotation.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Peripheral Blood Tests: Complete Blood Count (CBC) reveals mild to moderate leukocytosis with a left shift in ~50% of cases, though WBC count may be normal. Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) and C-reactive Protein (CRP) are elevated in >90% of cases; useful as baselines for monitoring treatment response. Serum Procalcitonin (PCT) is elevated in acute bacterial joint infection and helps discriminate bacterial septic arthritis from non-infectious inflammatory arthritis. Blood cultures should be drawn prior to antibiotic administration; positive in 25% to 70% of native joint bacterial cases (up to 90% in PWID) and represent the sole pathogen source in ~10% of cases.
  - Serology & Ancillary Blood Tests: Serology for Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme arthritis), Parvovirus B19 (IgM/IgG), Chikungunya, Hepatitis B/C, HIV, and Interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) or TST for M. tuberculosis (positive in 85% to 90%).
- **Imaging Modalities (e.g., X-ray, CT, Ultrasound):**
  - Plain Film Radiography: Initial test of choice. Early findings: soft tissue swelling and fat-pad displacement with normal bones. Late findings: periarticular osteopenia, joint space narrowing, subchondral bone erosions, periosteal reaction, and subchondral bone destruction. Essential for baseline comparison, ruling out foreign bodies, and detecting contiguous osteomyelitis.
  - Ultrasonography: Rapid, highly sensitive tool for detecting joint effusions, guiding bedside or clinic arthrocentesis (especially hip joint in children), and evaluating soft tissue abscesses or bursal collections.
  - Computed Tomography (CT): Delineates cortical bone destruction, subchondral erosion, joint space widening, sequestra, and guides percutaneous aspiration/biopsy of deep/axial joints (sacroiliac, pubic symphysis, sternoclavicular).
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): Modality of choice for detailed musculoskeletal evaluation. Highly sensitive and specific for detecting early joint inflammation, synovial thickening, cartilage degradation, bone marrow edema, periarticular abscesses, sinus tracts, and contiguous osteomyelitis. Contrast-enhanced/dynamic MRI helps differentiate septic arthritis from transient tenosynovitis.
  - Radionuclide / PET Imaging: Three-phase bone scan (99mTc-MDP), Gallium-67, Indium-111 labeled WBCs, or 18F-FDG PET/CT used when MRI is contraindicated or inconclusive.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Synovial Fluid Analysis: Synovial fluid is purulent, opaque, and low-viscosity. Synovial WBC count typically >50,000 cells/mm3 with >=90% polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs). However, WBC counts between 25,000 and 75,000 cells/mm3 represent a "gray zone", and counts <50,000 cells/mm3 do NOT rule out septic arthritis (especially in gonococcal, partially treated, low-virulence, or mycobacterial/fungal infections). Mandatory polarized light microscopy for crystals (gout/CPPD); presence of crystals DOES NOT rule out infection (coexistence occurs in 1.5% to 43.5% of cases).
  - Stains & Cultures: Synovial fluid Gram stain is positive in 50% of nongonococcal bacterial cases, 25% of suppurative gonococcal cases, and rare in DGI skin lesions. Synovial fluid culture is positive in 80% to 90% of untreated nongonococcal cases, ~50% of gonococcal cases, 20% to 30% of DGI cases, 80% of tuberculous cases, and variable in fungal cases. Direct inoculation of synovial fluid into blood culture bottles (BACTEC) improves recovery of fastidious organisms (Kingella kingae, Brucella). Shoulder joint specimens require culture incubation for >7 to 10 days to detect Cutibacterium acnes. Mycobacterium marinum requires incubation at 30°C.
  - Mucosal Testing for DGI: Uterine cervical (positive 80% to 90%), male urethral (50% to 70%), pharyngeal, and rectal cultures or Nucleic Acid Amplification Testing (NAAT/PCR) from mucosal swabs or urine are diagnostic for DGI.
  - Molecular Testing / PCR: Multiplex or broad-range 16S rDNA real-time PCR on synovial fluid provides rapid identification with high sensitivity (up to 95%) and specificity (97%), particularly for fastidious pathogens (K. kingae, Mycoplasma, N. gonorrhoeae, T. whipplei, NTM) and in patients previously treated with antibiotics. Off-label NAAT/PCR on synovial fluid is recommended for M. tuberculosis diagnosis.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Identification of the infecting microorganism on synovial fluid Gram stain and culture (or synovial tissue biopsy culture/histopathology for tuberculous and fungal arthritis) remains the definitive gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Emergency Evaluation: Bacterial septic arthritis is a rheumatologic emergency requiring immediate joint fluid aspiration, joint decompression/drainage, and prompt systemic antimicrobial administration. Pediatric hip septic arthritis requires decompression within 6 to 12 hours.
  - Supportive Care: Analgesia, joint immobilization/splinting in functional position during acute phase, followed by early passive and active range-of-motion physical therapy once acute inflammation subsides to prevent joint contractures and muscle atrophy.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Gram-Positive Cocci on Gram Stain: Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h (targeting serum AUC/MIC ratio of 400 to 600 mg-h/L).
  - Gram-Negative Cocci on Gram Stain (Suspected Gonococcal): Ceftriaxone 1 g IV/IM q24h (or Cefotaxime 1 g IV q8h) PLUS Azithromycin 1 g PO single dose.
  - Gram-Negative Rods on Gram Stain: Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h OR Cefepime 2 g IV q8h OR Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h.
  - Gram-Stain Negative (Equivocal or Unknown): Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h PLUS an antipseudomonal beta-lactam (Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h, Cefepime 2 g IV q8h, OR Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h).
  - Human or Animal Bite Wounds: Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h OR Amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg PO q12h.
- **Alternative Regimens (e.g., for Penicillin Allergy):**
  - Gram-Positive / MRSA Coverage (Vancomycin Allergy / Intolerance / VRE / VISA): Daptomycin 6 to 8 mg/kg IV q24h OR Linezolid 600 mg IV or PO q12h. (Other options: Dalbavancin, Ceftaroline).
  - Gram-Negative Coverage (Severe Penicillin / Beta-Lactam Allergy or ESBL risk): Carbapenem (Imipenem 500 mg IV q6h OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h) (preferred alternative), Fluoroquinolone (Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q8h / 750 mg PO q12h OR Levofloxacin 750 mg IV/PO q24h), OR Aztreonam 2 g IV q8h.
  - Bite Wounds in Penicillin-Allergic Patients: Clindamycin 600 mg IV q8h PLUS Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q8h OR Moxifloxacin 400 mg IV/PO q24h.
  - Directed Oral Step-Down Options:
    - MSSA: Nafcillin/Oxacillin 2 g IV q4h or Cefazolin 2 g IV q8h. (Ceftriaxone controversial/not recommended for S. aureus).
    - MRSA Oral Step-Down (after 2-3 weeks IV): TMP-SMX 1-2 DS tabs PO q8-12h, Doxycycline 100 mg PO q12h, Minocycline 100 mg PO q12h, Clindamycin 300-450 mg PO q6-8h, or Linezolid 600 mg PO q12h.
    - Pseudomonas aeruginosa: Cefepime 2 g IV q8h, Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h; oral step-down to Ciprofloxacin 750 mg PO q12h.
    - Disseminated Gonococcal Infection: Ceftriaxone 1 g IV/IM q24h for 7 days total PLUS Azithromycin 1 g PO single dose.
    - Tuberculous Arthritis: 2 months of INH + RIF + Pyrazinamide + Ethambutol, followed by 4 months of INH + RIF (total 6 months).
    - Candida Arthritis: Surgical debridement PLUS Fluconazole 400-800 mg daily for >=6 weeks, or IV Echinocandin (Caspofungin/Micafungin/Anidulafungin) for >=2 weeks followed by Fluconazole.
    - Treatment Duration: Total duration for native joint bacterial septic arthritis is 2 to 4 weeks parenteral/oral therapy. S. aureus (including MRSA) and Gram-negative bacilli require a minimum of 4 weeks of therapy. Gonococcal arthritis requires 1 week. Mycobacterial arthritis requires 6 months. Fungal arthritis requires 6 to 12 months.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions (e.g., Drainage, Debridement):**
  - Joint Fluid Drainage Modalities: Mandatory in all cases of acute bacterial arthritis. Options include:
    1. Closed-Needle Aspiration: Daily closed-needle aspiration until synovial effusion and purulence resolve; suitable for accessible peripheral joints without loculations.
    2. Arthroscopic Drainage & Debridement: Preferred surgical modality for knees, hips, shoulders, wrists, and ankles. Allows visualization, copious irrigation, adhesion lysis, and synovectomy with lower morbidity than open surgery.
    3. Open Arthrotomy: Indicated for complex/inaccessible joints (e.g., pediatric hip), presence of loculations/thick purulent debris, extensive soft tissue necrosis, contiguous osteomyelitis, or failure of needle/arthroscopic drainage.
  - Risk Factors for Surgical Debridement Failure: History of inflammatory arthropathy (RA) or diabetes mellitus; involvement of knee, shoulder, or hip; synovial fluid WBC >85,000 cells/mm3; and S. aureus infection. Joint washout within 24 hours of diagnosis significantly shortens hospital length of stay.
  - Direct intra-articular injection of antibiotics is NOT recommended due to chemical synovitis risk.
  - Adjunctive Corticosteroids: Dexamethasone as adjunctive therapy with antibiotics in pediatric hematogenous septic arthritis reduces clinical sequelae and inflammatory parameters without increasing relapse.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Local: Rapid articular cartilage destruction, subchondral bone erosion, capsular rupture, periarticular soft tissue abscess formation, osteomyelitis of contiguous bone, sinus tract / articular-cutaneous fistula formation, and pathologic joint dislocation (especially hip in neonates/children).
  - Systemic: Sepsis, septic shock, bacteremic seeding to distant sites (infective endocarditis, vertebral osteomyelitis, epidural abscess), prolonging hospital stay, and acute inpatient mortality (7% to 15% overall; up to 30% to 50% in patients with severe comorbidities, polyarticular involvement, or PWID).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Permanent loss of joint function, joint stiffness, and severe restriction of mobility in up to 50% of survivors.
  - Secondary osteoarthritis, chronic joint deformity, fibrous or bony ankylosis, leg-length discrepancy, and avascular necrosis of the femoral head (particularly following pediatric hip septic arthritis).
  - Chronic disability requiring excision arthroplasty, joint fusion (arthrodesis), or total joint replacement.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Desert Rheumatism" / "Valley Fever": Self-limited acute hypersensitivity syndrome presenting with migratory polyarthralgia or polyarthritis in ~1/3 of patients with primary Coccidioides immitis/posadasii infection.
  - "Slapped Cheeks" & Lacy Reticular Rash: Pathognomonic exanthem of Parvovirus B19 in children and adults with viral polyarthritis.
  - "That which contorts or bends up": Literal translation of Chikungunya, describing the extreme, bending joint pain caused by Chikungunya virus polyarthritis.
  - "Dermatitis-Tenosynovitis-Polyarthralgia Triad": Classic triad of Disseminated Gonococcal Infection (DGI).
  - "Gray Zone Synovial WBC": Synovial WBC count between 25,000 and 75,000 cells/mm3 where septic arthritis cannot be confirmed or ruled out by WBC count alone.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Presence of Crystals Does NOT Rule Out Infection: Crystalline arthritis (gout/pseudogout) and septic arthritis coexist in 1.5% to 43.5% of cases; finding monosodium urate or CPPD crystals in synovial fluid never eliminates the need for Gram stain and culture.
  - Low Synovial WBC (<50,000 cells/mm3) Does NOT Exclude Septic Arthritis: Frequently observed in Neisseria gonorrhoeae, early/partially treated bacterial arthritis, low-virulence organisms, mycobacterial/fungal infections, or severely leukopenic hosts.
  - Ceftriaxone for S. aureus: Intrinsically less active than nafcillin/oxacillin or cefazolin against MSSA; routine use for S. aureus septic arthritis remains controversial and is NOT recommended despite once-daily convenience.
  - Extension vs. Flexion Pain in Elbow: Exacerbation of elbow pain on extension indicates septic joint effusion (septic arthritis), whereas pain on flexion indicates septic olecranon bursitis.
  - Direct Intra-Articular Antibiotic Injections: Strictly contraindicated because systemically administered antibiotics penetrate synovial fluid readily, and intra-articular injection risks chemical synovitis.
  - Culture Yield Discrepancy in DGI: In DGI, mucosal site cultures (cervix 80% to 90%, urethra 50% to 70%) have a dramatically higher diagnostic yield than synovial fluid culture (20% to 30%) or blood culture (<30%).
