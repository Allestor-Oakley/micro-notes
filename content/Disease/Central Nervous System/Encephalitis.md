# Encephalitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Encephalitis is an inflammatory process involving the brain parenchyma associated with clinical or laboratory evidence of neurologic dysfunction. According to the International Encephalitis Consortium, a consensus diagnosis requires a community-acquired illness with altered mental status (defined as encephalopathy with altered level of consciousness, lethargy, or personality/behavioral changes) lasting 24 hours or more with no alternative cause identified, accompanied by at least two (for possible encephalitis) or three (for probable/confirmed encephalitis) of the following criteria: (1) documented fever >=38°C (100.4°F) within 72 hours before or after presentation; (2) generalized or partial seizures not fully attributable to a preexisting seizure disorder; (3) new-onset focal neurological deficits; (4) cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pleocytosis (>5 white blood cells/mm3); (5) abnormality of brain parenchyma on neuroimaging (MRI or CT) suggestive of encephalitis; (6) electroencephalogram (EEG) abnormality consistent with encephalitis (e.g., focal or generalized slowing, temporal sharp waves, periodic lateralizing epileptiform discharges). Encephalitis must be clinically distinguished from encephalopathy, which represents diffuse cerebral dysfunction due to toxic, metabolic, hypoxic, or ischemic causes without associated parenchymal brain inflammation or CSF pleocytosis.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - By Etiology & Pathogenetic Mechanism: (1) Infectious Encephalitis—direct microbial invasion, replication, and cytopathic injury of brain parenchyma (predominantly viral, but also bacterial, fungal, protozoal, or helminthic); (2) Autoimmune / Immune-Mediated Encephalitis—antibody-mediated or cell-mediated inflammatory attack targeting neuronal cell surface, synaptic, or intracellular antigens (e.g., Anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (anti-NMDAR) encephalitis, anti-LGI1, anti-AMPAR, anti-GABABR, and paraneoplastic limbic encephalitides such as anti-Hu, anti-Ma2, anti-Yo); (3) Postinfectious / Postvaccinial Encephalitis—acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), an autoimmune demyelinating process occurring 1 to 2 weeks following viral infection (influenza A/B, measles, mumps, varicella, enteroviruses, Mycoplasma) or vaccination.
  - By Anatomic Predilection / Pattern: (1) Limbic Encephalitis—involves medial temporal lobes, hippocampi, cingulate gyrus, and amygdala (classic for HSV-1, HHV-6, and autoimmune/anti-NMDAR encephalitis); (2) Rhombencephalitis / Brainstem Encephalitis—involves pons, medulla, and midbrain (classic for Enterovirus 71, Listeria monocytogenes, Rabies virus, and Japanese encephalitis virus); (3) Basal Ganglia / Thalamic Encephalitis—involves deep grey nuclei (classic for Flaviviruses such as West Nile virus, St. Louis encephalitis virus, Japanese encephalitis virus, and Influenza-associated acute necrotizing encephalopathy); (4) Diffuse / Multifocal Encephalitis—widespread cortical and subcortical white matter involvement (e.g., VZV CNS vasculopathy, Cytomegalovirus, measles SSPE, or arboviral encephalitides).
  - By Temporal Course: Acute (onset over hours to days), Subacute / Chronic (evolving over weeks to months, e.g., HIV encephalitis, subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE), progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), Balamuthia mandrillaris, Acanthamoeba).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Overall Incidence & Hospitalization Burden: In the United States, an estimated 263,352 encephalitis-associated hospitalizations occurred between 1998 and 2010 (approximately 20,000 cases per year), with total annual medical charges exceeding $2 billion. The overall in-hospital mortality rate ranges from 5% to 12%.
  - Age Distribution: Bimodal distribution with highest incidence at age extremes: infants younger than 1 year (11.1 per 100,000) and elderly adults older than 65 years (13.2 per 100,000), compared to intermediate rates (4.0 to 8.4 per 100,000) in older children and young adults.
  - Etiologic Unknowns: Approximately 50% to 60% (32% to 75% across national registries) of encephalitis cases remain without an identified etiologic agent despite extensive diagnostic evaluation. In the California Encephalitis Project (CEP) among 1571 patients, a confirmed or probable cause was identified in only 16% of cases (viruses 69%, bacteria 20%, prions 7%, parasites 3%, fungi 1%). In a UK prospective study, an infectious cause was found in 42% (HSV 19%, VZV 5%), acute immune-mediated in 21%, and unknown in 37%.
  - Specific Predisposing Risk Factors & Exposures:
    1. Host Age: Neonates (HSV-2, CMV, Rubella, Zika, Enteroviruses, Parechovirus); Children (Enteroviruses like EV-71, La Crosse virus, Mumps, Measles, Influenza-associated ANE); Elderly >60 years (HSV-1, VZV, West Nile virus, St. Louis encephalitis virus, Listeria monocytogenes).
    2. Immunocompromised Status (HIV/AIDS with CD4 <50-100 cells/mm3, solid organ transplantation, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), chemotherapy, anti-CD20/rituximab therapy): Predisposes to VZV, CMV (ventriculoencephalitis), HHV-6 (limbic encephalitis post-allogeneic HSCT), JC virus (PML), Toxoplasma gondii, Cryptococcus, Aspergillus, Listeria, LCMV, and severe West Nile virus.
    3. Genetic Susceptibility: Single nucleotide polymorphisms in human TLR3-interferon pathways (predisposes to HSV-1 encephalitis); 2-5-oligoadenylate synthetase gene polymorphisms and homozygous CCR5 chemokine receptor deficiency (strongly enhances susceptibility to severe neuroinvasive West Nile virus disease).
    4. Seasonality & Vector Exposures: Late summer and early fall (mosquito- and tick-borne arboviruses such as WNV, EEE, SLE, La Crosse, Powassan, and Enteroviruses); Late fall and winter (Influenza A/B, LCMV from indoor rodent exposure).
    5. Travel, Occupation & Animal Contacts: Rabies (dog, bat, raccoon, skunk, or fox bites; cave spelunking); B virus / Cercopithecine herpesvirus 1 (Old World macaque bites/scratches in laboratory workers/veterinarians); Hendra and Nipah viruses (fruit bats, swine, or horse exposures in Australia and South/Southeast Asia); Japanese encephalitis virus (pig and wading bird reservoirs in rural Asia and Australia); Tick-borne encephalitis virus (unpasteurized goat/cow milk or Ixodes tick bites in Central Europe and Russia); Primary amebic meningoencephalitis / PAM (warm freshwater swimming/lakes for Naegleria fowleri, soil/water exposure for Acanthamoeba and Balamuthia mandrillaris).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Viruses (Account for the vast majority of confirmed infectious cases):
    1. Herpes Simplex Virus Type 1 (HSV-1): The single most common identified cause of sporadic, non-epidemic fatal viral encephalitis in developed nations (accounting for 19% to 24% of confirmed viral cases).
    2. Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV): Causes VZV encephalitis and VZV CNS vasculopathy (14% of viral cases in CEP, 5% in UK series).
    3. Enteroviruses (Nonpolio Enteroviruses & Human Parechoviruses): Leading cause of viral CNS infections overall; Echovirus 30, Coxsackievirus A/B, Enterovirus 71 (causes severe rhombencephalitis with neurogenic pulmonary edema and shock), Enterovirus D68 (associated with acute flaccid myelitis).
    4. West Nile Virus (WNV): The most common cause of arboviral/epidemic neuroinvasive viral encephalitis in North America (11% of CEP cases; causes 50% to 60% encephalitis, 30% to 40% meningitis, 5% to 10% acute flaccid paralysis).
    5. Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) and Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6): Account for 10% and a significant proportion of post-transplant/immunocompromised cases.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Neonates (<1 month): HSV-2 (perinatal transmission during birth), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Rubella virus, Zika virus (ZIKV), Enteroviruses (overwhelming sepsis-like syndrome with encephalitis, 10% mortality), Parechovirus type 3, Group B Streptococcus, Escherichia coli K1, Listeria monocytogenes.
  - Children & Adolescents: Enteroviruses (EV-71, Coxsackievirus A16/A6, Echoviruses), La Crosse virus (California encephalitis group, mean age 7.5 years), Measles virus, Mumps virus, Influenza A/B (acute necrotizing encephalopathy), Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6, roseola infantum), Bartonella henselae (cat-scratch encephalopathy).
  - Adults & Elderly (>60 years): HSV-1 (peaks in middle-aged and elderly), Varicella-Zoster Virus (reactivation zoster vasculopathy), West Nile virus (odds ratio 2.2 for age >64 years), St. Louis encephalitis virus, Eastern equine encephalitis virus, Listeria monocytogenes, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Cryptococcus neoformans.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Bacteria & Spirochetes: Listeria monocytogenes (rhombencephalitis), Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Bartonella henselae, Rickettsia rickettsii (RMSF), Ehrlichia chaffeensis, Anaplasma phagocytophilum, Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme neuroborreliosis), Treponema pallidum (neurosyphilis), Tropheryma whipplei (Whipple disease), Coxiella burnetii, Brucella species, Legionella pneumophila.
  - Opportunistic Viruses (Immunocompromised Hosts): Cytomegalovirus (CMV; ventriculoencephalitis in HIV with CD4 <50 cells/mm3 or SOT/HSCT), Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6; limbic encephalitis post-allogeneic HSCT), JC Virus (Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy / PML), Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV-1; acute retroviral syndrome, AIDS encephalopathy, or CD8 encephalitis during immune reconstitution), Lymphocytic Choriomeningitis Virus (LCMV; in SOT/transplant recipients or rodent exposure), Rabies Virus (furious or paralytic rabies), B Virus / Cercopithecine Herpesvirus 1 (macaque bites/scratches), Nipah Virus, Hendra Virus, Powassan Virus, Toscana Virus, Japanese Encephalitis Virus, Tick-Borne Encephalitis Virus.
  - Fungi: Cryptococcus neoformans and C. gattii, Histoplasma capsulatum, Coccidioides immitis/posadasii, Aspergillus fumigatus, Mucorales (Rhizopus, Mucor), Blastomyces dermatitidis, Scedosporium apiospermum, Cladophialophora bantiana.
  - Protozoa & Free-Living Amebas: Toxoplasma gondii (most common parasitic cause of focal brain lesions in HIV with CD4 <100 cells/mm3 or SOT), Naegleria fowleri (Primary Amebic Meningoencephalitis / PAM), Acanthamoeba species, Balamuthia mandrillaris (Granulomatous Amebic Encephalitis / GAE), Trypanosoma cruzi (Chagasic encephalitis reactivation), Plasmodium falciparum (cerebral malaria).
  - Helminths: Angiostrongylus cantonensis (eosinophilic meningoencephalitis), Gnathostoma spinigerum, Baylisascaris procyonis, Toxocara canis, Taenia solium (neurocysticercosis), Schistosoma species.
  - Noninfectious & Autoimmune Encephalitis Mimics: Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis (most common autoimmune encephalitis, associated with ovarian teratomas in young females and post-HSV/VZV triggers), Anti-LGI1, Anti-AMPAR, Anti-GABABR, Paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis (anti-Hu, anti-Ma2, anti-Yo), Acute Disseminated Encephalomyelitis (ADEM), Hashimoto encephalopathy, CNS vasculitis, Neurosarcoidosis.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Routes of Central Nervous System Entry:
    1. Hematogenous Dissemination across the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB) (Most Common): Viruses multiply at primary mucosal/extraneural entry sites (respiratory epithelium, GI tract lamina propria, lymph nodes), establish primary and secondary viremia, and cross the BBB. Mechanisms across microvascular endothelial cells include: (a) Direct infection of cerebral endothelial cells (e.g., WNV, Measles); (b) Paracellular or transcellular transport across tight junctions; (c) "Trojan-Horse" mechanism within infected circulating leukocytes (macrophages/T-cells, e.g., HIV, CMV, Measles); (d) Crossing fenestrated capillaries in the choroid plexus into CSF (e.g., Mumps).
    2. Retrograde Axonal / Neural Transport: Pathogens bypass the hematogenous route and ascend along peripheral or cranial nerve axons directly into the CNS: (a) Olfactory Pathway (e.g., HSV-1 via olfactory mucosa and fila olfactoria to the olfactory bulb and orbitofrontal/temporal cortex; Naegleria fowleri via cribriform plate); (b) Trigeminal Nerve Pathway (e.g., HSV-1 reactivation from trigeminal ganglia, ascending along tentorial nerves to temporal and frontal lobe dura/parenchyma; VZV reactivation); (c) Peripheral Motor/Sensory Nerves (e.g., Rabies virus ascending via neuromuscular junctions and peripheral axoplasm to spinal cord and brain; Poliovirus).
  - Neuroparenchymal Spread & Cell Tropism: Once inside the CNS, viruses spread through extracellular gaps, contiguous cell-to-cell contact, or axonal ramifications. Specific viral cardiotropism/neurotropism dictates anatomical distribution: HSV-1 targets limbic structures (medial temporal lobes, insula, cingulate gyrus); Flaviviruses (WNV, JEV, SLE) target deep grey nuclei (thalamus, basal ganglia, brainstem) and anterior horn cells of the spinal cord; CMV targets periventricular subependymal glia and ependymal lining (ventriculitis); Rabies targets limbic and brainstem neurons; Enterovirus 71 targets brainstem motor nuclei (rhombencephalitis).
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Mechanisms of Neuronal Injury: Neuronal damage results from a combination of direct viral cytopathic effects (viral replication, endosomal escape, host protein synthesis inhibition, intracellular protein cleavage) and secondary immune-mediated tissue destruction (cytotoxic T-lymphocytes, microglial activation, pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and bystander neuronal lysis).
  - Role of Apoptosis & Inflammation: Programmed cell death (apoptosis) is a primary pathway of neuronal death in HSV-1, WNV, and CMV encephalitis. Host innate TLR3-interferon responses trigger intense perivascular mononuclear cell cuffing (T-lymphocytes, macrophages), microglial nodule formation, neuronophagia (phagocytosis of necrotic neurons by microglia), diffuse astrogliosis, microvascular thrombosis, and vasogenic/cytotoxic cerebral edema.
  - Necrotizing & Hemorrhagic Pathology: HSV-1 produces a severe, asymmetric necrotizing encephalitis with petechial hemorrhages, liquefactive parenchymal necrosis, and intranuclear Cowdry A inclusion bodies in neurons and glia within temporal and orbitofrontal lobes. VZV vasculopathy produces granulomatous inflammation of large or small cerebral arteries, causing multifocal ischemic and hemorrhagic cortical/subcortical infarctions.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Acute Presentation Triad: Fever (90% to 100%), acute altered mental status / altered level of consciousness (80% to 100%; ranging from mild confusion, lethargy, inattention, and personality/behavioral changes to obtundation and frank coma), and severe headache (50% to 81%).
  - Neuropsychiatric & Behavioral Manifestations: Prodromal influenza-like or GI illness followed rapidly by acute psychosis, agitation, anxiety, hallucinations, delusions, insomnia, disorientation, amnesia / short-term memory impairment, and expressive or receptive dysphasia/aphasia (characteristic of limbic involvement in HSV-1 or anti-NMDAR encephalitis).
  - Seizures & Focal Deficits: Seizures occur in 40% to 68% of HSV-1 cases (focal, complex partial, or generalized, including non-convulsive status epilepticus); focal weakness / hemiparesis (20% to 40%), dysarthria, and gait ataxia.
  - Prodromal & Systemic Clues: Antecedent respiratory prodrome (Influenza, Measles, Mumps, Mycoplasma); vesicular skin rash (VZV, HSV); maculopapular rash (WNV, Enteroviruses, Dengue, Zika); parotitis, orchitis, or oophoritis (Mumps, LCMV); severe polyarthralgias and joint pain (Chikungunya); asymmetric flaccid limb weakness / acute flaccid paralysis (WNV, Poliovirus, Enterovirus 71, EV-D68); pharyngeal spasms, hydrophobia, and aerophobia (Rabies).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Neurological Examination Signs: Depressed Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score; nuchal rigidity / meningismus (30% to 60%, reflecting meningoencephalitis); focal motor deficits (hemiparesis, monoparesis, facial weakness); hyperreflexia or Babinski signs; involuntary movement disorders (myoclonus, rest tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia / parkinsonism in WNV, St. Louis, and Japanese encephalitis); cranial nerve palsies (CN III, IV, VI, VII, VIII); and optic chorioretinitis (seen on fundoscopy in WNV, CMV, Bartonella, Toxoplasma).
  - Autonomic Instability: Loss of vasomotor and temperature control, hyperpyrexia (fever up to 107°F in rabies), labile blood pressure, cardiac arrhythmias, and hypothermia or SIADH / diabetes insipidus secondary to hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction.
  - Specific Etiologic Physical Sign Combinations:
    1. West Nile Virus: Tremor, myoclonus, parkinsonian features (20% to 40%), maculopapular rash, chorioretinitis, and asymmetrical lower motor neuron flaccid limb paralysis with absent reflexes and preserved sensation.
    2. Rabies Virus (Furious Rabies): Hydrophobia and aerophobia (violent involuntary pharyngeal/nuchal spasms triggered by swallowing attempts or drafts of air), autonomic hyperactivity, hypersalivation, and agitation; Paralytic ("dumb") rabies: ascending flaccid paralysis starting in the bitten limb.
    3. Enterovirus 71: Hand-foot-and-mouth vesicles, myoclonic jerks, ataxia, cranial nerve palsies, followed by sudden neurogenic pulmonary edema and fulminant shock (rhombencephalitis).
    4. Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis: Autonomic instability, prominent oro-facial-lingual dyskinesias, choreoathetosis, dystonia, catatonia, and "extreme delta brush" pattern on EEG in young females with ovarian teratoma.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Hydrophobia & Aerophobia: Violent pharyngeal spasms on drinking water or exposure to air drafts pathognomonic for furious Rabies virus infection.
  - Unilateral Asymmetric Temporal Lobe Hemorrhagic Necrosis on MRI: Pathognomonic neuroimaging finding for HSV-1 encephalitis.
  - Asymmetrical Acute Flaccid Paralysis with Preserved Sensation: Lower motor neuron paralysis with loss of reflexes pathognomonic for West Nile virus anterior horn cell poliomyelitis-like syndrome.
  - Oro-Facial-Lingual Dyskinesias & Extreme Delta Brush on EEG: Pathognomonic clinical and electrographic markers for Anti-NMDA Receptor Autoimmune Encephalitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - CBC & General Blood Panel: Peripheral leukocytosis or leukopenia (LCMV, viral sepsis); thrombocytopenia (LCMV, Dengue, RMSF, Ehrlichia); hyponatremia (common in WNV, St. Louis encephalitis, Eastern equine encephalitis, and Legionella due to SIADH). Elevated transaminases in viral hepatitis, VEE, or SLE.
  - Inflammatory Markers: Serum C-reactive protein (CRP) and Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) may be mildly or moderately elevated in infectious or autoimmune encephalitis, but lack specificity.
  - Lumbar Puncture (LP) & Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) Analysis (Core Diagnostic Test):
    - Opening Pressure: Normal to moderately elevated.
    - Leukocyte Count & Differential: Typical viral profile shows lymphocytic pleocytosis (median 50 to 500 WBCs/mm3, mean ~130 to 237/mm3 in HSV-1; mean ~227/mm3 in WNV). Neutrophilic predominance (>50% PMNs) occurs in early enteroviral encephalitis, WNV (37% to 50% neutrophilic predominance), Eastern Equine encephalitis (median 70% PMNs), Tick-borne encephalitis, and bacterial/amebic mimics. Note: Up to 5% to 10% of HSV-1 cases may have normal initial CSF cell count (<5 WBCs/mm3), especially if tested <48 hours from onset!
    - CSF Protein: Moderately elevated (typically 50 to 150 mg/dL; mean 80 to 85 mg/dL in HSV-1; significantly higher in VZV vasculopathy and fungal cases).
    - CSF Glucose: Normal in >95% of viral encephalitis cases (HSV, WNV, SLE). Low CSF glucose (hypoglycorrhachia, CSF-to-serum ratio <0.4) indicates fungal (Cryptococcus, Coccidioides), mycobacterial (TB), bacterial (Listeria), amebic (Naegleria PAM), or mumps/CMV/EEE encephalitis.
    - CSF Red Blood Cells: Xanthochromia and elevated CSF RBCs (non-traumatic) reflect hemorrhagic necrosis in HSV-1 encephalitis or EEE.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) with Contrast (Diagnostic Modality of Choice): Superior sensitivity to CT. Preferred sequences include T1-weighted with gadolinium, T2-weighted, Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR), and Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI).
    - HSV-1 Encephalitis: T2 and FLAIR hyperintensity with restricted diffusion involving the hypermedial temporal lobes, insular cortex, amygdala, and cingulate gyrus; characteristically asymmetric/unilateral initially, strictly sparing the lentiform nucleus/basal ganglia (distinguishing it from MCA stroke).
    - West Nile Virus & Flaviviruses (JEV, SLE): T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in deep grey matter structures—bilateral thalami, basal ganglia (substantia nigra), midbrain, and brainstem; spinal cord MRI shows anterior horn T2 hyperintensity in acute flaccid paralysis.
    - Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV Vasculopathy): Multifocal ischemic and hemorrhagic cortical/subcortical white matter infarctions at gray-white junctions with vessel wall enhancement on MRA.
    - Cytomegalovirus (CMV): Periventricular subependymal T2/FLAIR hyperintensity and smooth ependymal contrast enhancement (ventriculitis).
    - Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6): Medial temporal lobe T2/FLAIR signal hyperintensity (limbic encephalitis) post-HSCT.
    - Acute Disseminated Encephalomyelitis (ADEM): Asymmetric, large, multifocal, fluffy T2/FLAIR hyperintense subcortical white matter lesions with open-ring contrast enhancement.
    - Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy (PML): Asymmetric, non-enhancing T2/FLAIR hyperintense white matter lesions without mass effect in subcortical centrum semiovale.
  - Computed Tomography (CT) of Head: Less sensitive than MRI (normal in 20% to 60% of early HSV-1 cases). Used primarily in emergency settings to rule out mass effect, brain abscess, or hemorrhage prior to LP. Shows temporal lobe hypodensity and mass effect in subacute HSV-1.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Molecular Testing (CSF Nucleic Acid Amplification / PCR - Diagnostic Gold Standard for Viral Encephalitis):
    1. HSV-1 & HSV-2 CSF PCR: Sensitivity 98%, specificity 94% to 99%. Sensitivity declines after 7 to 14 days of acyclovir therapy (98% positive at <=7 days, 47% at 8-14 days). Note: False-negative PCR can occur during the first 24 to 48 hours of symptoms or in bloody CSF (heme porphyrin inhibition); if clinical suspicion for HSV remains high despite a negative initial PCR, continue acyclovir and repeat LP with CSF HSV PCR in 3 to 7 days!
    2. VZV CSF PCR: Sensitivity >95%, specificity >95%. In VZV vasculopathy, combine CSF PCR with CSF VZV IgG antibody index (intrathecal antibody synthesis), as antibody index remains positive longer than PCR.
    3. Enterovirus & Parechovirus CSF RT-PCR: Rapid, highly sensitive (>95%) and specific.
    4. Multiplex CSF PCR Panels (e.g., BioFire FilmArray ME Panel): Simultaneously tests for 14 targets (HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, CMV, HHV-6, Enterovirus, Parechovirus, S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, S. agalactiae, H. influenzae, L. monocytogenes, E. coli K1, Cryptococcus neoformans/gattii).
    5. Metagenomic Next-Generation Sequencing (mNGS): Unbiased detection of rare, novel, or fastidious pathogens in culture/PCR-negative or immunocompromised encephalitis cases.
  - Serology & Intrathecal Antibody Synthesis:
    1. West Nile Virus: CSF WNV IgM capture ELISA is the diagnostic test of choice (WNV serum IgM or fourfold rise in serum IgG; WNV CSF PCR has low sensitivity <50% due to transient short-lived viremia).
    2. Arboviruses (JEV, SLE, EEE, La Crosse, Powassan, Chikungunya, Zika): CSF and serum IgM capture ELISA or paired acute/convalescent serum IgG titers.
    3. Lyme Neuroborreliosis (Borrelia burgdorferi): Serum 2-tiered antibody test, CSF-to-serum antibody index, and CSF Lyme C6 peptide.
    4. Syphilis: Serum treponemal/non-treponemal tests and CSF VDRL.
    5. Rabies: Direct fluorescent antibody (DFA) or RT-PCR on full-thickness nuchal skin biopsy (hairline), corneal impressions, serum/CSF neutralizing antibodies, and saliva RT-PCR.
  - Autoimmune Encephalitis Panel: Testing for autoantibodies in serum and CSF (Anti-NMDAR, Anti-LGI1, Anti-CASPR2, Anti-AMPAR, Anti-GABABR, Anti-Hu, Anti-Ma2).
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** CSF Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) demonstrating amplifiable viral DNA/RNA (or specific CSF IgM capture ELISA for arboviruses like West Nile virus) combined with characteristic MRI neuroimaging findings and histopathologic/immunohistochemical confirmation on brain biopsy (when performed) represents the gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Emergency Stabilization & ICU Admission: All patients with suspected acute encephalitis should be admitted to an Intensive Care Unit (ICU) or specialized neurocritical care unit for continuous cardiac, respiratory, and neurological monitoring.
  - Airway & Mechanical Ventilation: Early endotracheal intubation for GCS score <=8, severe bulbar palsy, loss of protective airway reflexes, status epilepticus, or neurogenic pulmonary edema (EV-71).
  - Intracranial Pressure (ICP) Management: Elevation of head of bed to 30 degrees; continuous ICP monitoring in patients with stupor/coma, papilledema, or severe cerebral edema on MRI; IV Mannitol (0.5 to 1.0 g/kg IV bolus) or Hypertonic Saline (3% NaCl) for acute ICP elevation; short-term hyperventilation (PaCO2 30-35 mm Hg) for impending herniation.
  - Seizure Management & EEG Monitoring: Seizures require immediate treatment with IV Lorazepam (4 mg) or Diazepam, followed by maintenance loading with IV Fosphenytoin (20 mg PE/kg), Levetiracetam (20-40 mg/kg), or Valproate. Continuous video-EEG monitoring is recommended in obtunded/comatose patients to detect non-convulsive status epilepticus. Primary prophylactic anticonvulsants are not routinely recommended in the absence of clinical/electrographic seizures.
  - Temperature & Autonomic Control: Aggressive fever control with antipyretics and cooling blankets (hyperthermia increases metabolic demand and neuronal injury). Hemodynamic support with IV fluids and vasopressors for autonomic vasoplegia or shock.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Immediate Empiric Antiviral Therapy (Mandatory Emergency Intervention):
    - Empiric High-Dose Intravenous Acyclovir MUST be initiated IMMEDIATELY in ALL patients with suspected encephalitis upon presentation, without waiting for neuroimaging, LP, or CSF PCR results! Delay in acyclovir administration directly increases mortality and permanent neurological disability!
    - Adult Dosing: Acyclovir 10 mg/kg IV every 8 hours (infused over 1 hour) for 14 to 21 days (dose adjusted for renal clearance; ensure adequate IV hydration to prevent acyclovir-induced obstructive crystalline nephropathy).
    - Pediatric Dosing (Infants and Children 3 months to 12 years): Acyclovir 20 mg/kg IV every 8 hours x 14 to 21 days. Neonatal HSV (0 to 3 months): Acyclovir 20 mg/kg IV every 8 hours x 21 days.
  - Combined Empiric Coverage for Encephalitis / Meningitis Overlap:
    - Empiric Acyclovir 10 mg/kg IV q8h
    - PLUS Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h (or Cefotaxime 2 g IV q4h) (for pneumococcal/meningococcal coverage)
    - PLUS Vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg IV q8-12h (for resistant pneumococcus)
    - PLUS Ampicillin 2 g IV q4h (in patients >50 years, immunocompromised, pregnant, or alcoholic to cover Listeria monocytogenes rhombencephalitis)
    - PLUS Doxycycline 100 mg IV q12h (during summer/fall in endemic areas for Rickettsia/RMSF, Ehrlichia, Anaplasma).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Pathogen-Directed Targeted Therapies:
    1. Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV Encephalitis / Vasculopathy): Acyclovir 10 to 15 mg/kg IV q8h for 14 to 21 days PLUS adjunctive oral Corticosteroids (Prednisone 60 mg daily x 3 to 5 days) in VZV vasculopathy to suppress vascular wall inflammation and prevent ischemic stroke.
    2. Cytomegalovirus (CMV Encephalitis / Ventriculitis): Combination therapy with Ganciclovir 5 mg/kg IV q12h PLUS Foscarnet 60 mg/kg IV q8h (or 90 mg/kg IV q12h) for 14 to 21 days, followed by maintenance therapy.
    3. Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6 Limbic Encephalitis): Ganciclovir 5 mg/kg IV q12h OR Foscarnet 60 mg/kg IV q8h (or 90 mg/kg q12h) for 14 to 21 days.
    4. Influenza-Associated Encephalopathy: Oseltamivir 75 mg PO/NG q12h x 5 days (or IV Peramivir).
    5. Anti-NMDA Receptor / Autoimmune Encephalitis: First-line immunotherapy: High-dose Methylprednisolone (1 g IV daily x 3 to 5 days) PLUS IVIG (0.4 g/kg/day x 5 days) OR Plasma Exchange (5 to 7 sessions). Second-line (for non-responders or delayed diagnosis): Rituximab 375 mg/m2 IV weekly x 4 doses and/or Cyclophosphamide. Screen for and resect underlying ovarian teratoma.
    6. Primary Amebic Meningoencephalitis (Naegleria fowleri): Intravenous and intrathecal Amphotericin B PLUS Miltefosine (50 mg PO q8h x 14 days, available from CDC) PLUS Azithromycin 500 mg IV daily PLUS Fluconazole 600 mg IV daily PLUS Rifampin 600 mg IV daily PLUS dexamethasone and aggressive hypothermia/ICP management.
    7. Toxoplasma gondii Encephalitis: Pyrimethamine (200 mg loading, then 50-75 mg/day PO) PLUS Sulfadiazine (1-1.5 g PO q6h) PLUS Leucovorin (10-25 mg/day PO) x 6 weeks. Alternative: TMP-SMX (5 mg/kg TMP IV/PO q12h).
  - Corticosteroid Role in Viral Encephalitis: Routine corticosteroids are NOT recommended for uncomplicated viral encephalitis (e.g., HSV-1, where steroids do not improve outcomes in RCTs). However, corticosteroids ARE indicated for: (1) Acute Disseminated Encephalomyelitis (ADEM; high-dose methylprednisolone 1 g/day x 3-5 days); (2) Autoimmune encephalitis; (3) VZV CNS vasculopathy; (4) Severe cerebral edema with impending herniation.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Stereotactic / Open Brain Biopsy: Reserved for unexplained cases of encephalitis with progressive neurological deterioration despite empiric antimicrobial therapy and non-invasive workup, or for evaluating focal mass lesions in immunocompromised hosts (e.g., ruling out lymphoma vs. infection).
  - Decompressive Craniectomy: Surgical removal of a large bone flap with duraplasty indicated as a life-saving rescue procedure for refractory intracranial hypertension, temporal lobe herniation, or massive hemorrhagic necrosis in severe HSV-1 or acute necrotizing encephalitis.
  - Ovarian Teratoma Resection: Surgical salpingo-oophorectomy / teratomatous tumor resection in female patients with Anti-NMDA receptor autoimmune encephalitis (essential for cure and recovery).
  - Ventriculostomy / External Ventricular Drain (EVD): Placement of EVD for CSF drainage and pressure relief in acute obstructive hydrocephalus (seen in mumps, miliary TB, or fungal encephalitis).

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Severe Intracranial Hypertension & Transtentorial / Uncal Brain Herniation.
  - Status Epilepticus (convulsive and non-convulsive) and refractory seizures.
  - Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH) or Diabetes Insipidus (DI) secondary to hypothalamic injury.
  - Neurogenic Pulmonary Edema, Autonomic Instability, and Cardiovascular Collapse (classic in Enterovirus 71 rhombencephalitis).
  - Ischemic & Hemorrhagic Stroke (VZV vasculopathy, Aspergillus angioinvasion, or CMV thrombosis).
  - High Acute Mortality: 5% to 15% overall; up to 20% to 30% in untreated/late-treated HSV-1; 15% to 30% in Japanese encephalitis; 30% to 50% in Eastern Equine encephalitis; nearly 100% in Rabies and Balamuthia/Naegleria PAM.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Persistent Neurocognitive & Memory Deficits: Short-term memory impairment, anterograde/retrograde amnesia, executive dysfunction, dementia, and severe intellectual decline (occurring in >30% to 50% of HSV-1, WNV, and JEV survivors).
  - Chronic Post-Encephalitic Epilepsy: Unprovoked recurrent seizures requiring lifelong anticonvulsant therapy (occurring in 20% to 40% of survivors).
  - Permanent Motor & Focal Neurological Deficits: Hemiparesis, dysphasia/aphasia, ataxia, cranial nerve palsies, and persistent extrapyramidal movement disorders (parkinsonism, tremor, dystonia, myoclonus in WNV/JEV).
  - Permanent Flaccid Muscle Weakness & Atrophy: Lower motor neuron paralysis, muscle wasting, and respiratory weakness following West Nile virus or Enterovirus 71/EV-D68 polio-like paralysis.
  - Behavioral & Psychiatric Disturbances: Personality changes, apathy, emotional lability, psychosis, depression, and severe fatigue (post-WNV fatigue lasting an average of 5 years).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Temporal Lobe Hyperintensity: Classic T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in mesial temporal lobes and insula on MRI, pathognomonic for HSV-1 encephalitis.
  - PLEDs (Periodic Lateralizing Epileptiform Discharges): Classic electroencephalographic pattern (2-3 Hz sharp waves over temporal leads) characteristic of HSV-1 encephalitis.
  - Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis: Autoimmune encephalitis in young women presenting with psychosis, dyskinesias, and hypoventilation, associated with ovarian teratomas.
  - Extreme Delta Brush: Specific electroencephalographic pattern on EEG characteristic of Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis.
  - Substantia Nigra / Thalamic T2 Hyperintensity: Bilateral deep grey matter MRI abnormalities pathognomonic for Flavivirus encephalitis (West Nile, St. Louis, Japanese encephalitis).
  - Rhombencephalitis: Brainstem encephalitis (pons/medulla) with neurogenic pulmonary edema characteristic of Enterovirus 71 infection.
  - Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE): Late fatal neurodegenerative sequela of measles virus occurring 5 to 15 years after childhood infection.
  - Zoster Sine Herpete: VZV neuroinvasive infection (meningitis, encephalitis, vasculopathy) occurring in the complete absence of a cutaneous vesicular rash.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Normal Initial CSF Cell Count in HSV-1: Up to 5% to 10% of PCR-proven HSV-1 encephalitis cases present with completely NORMAL CSF WBC counts (<5 cells/mm3) on initial LP, especially if performed early (<48 hours) in the disease course; LP must be repeated if clinical suspicion remains!
  - False-Negative Initial HSV CSF PCR: HSV CSF PCR can be falsely negative during the first 24 to 48 hours of symptom onset or in bloody CSF; empiric acyclovir MUST be continued and LP repeated in 3 to 7 days!
  - Normal CSF Glucose Rule Exception: While viral encephalitis characteristically displays normal CSF glucose, low CSF glucose (hypoglycorrhachia) occurs in Mumps, CMV, Eastern Equine encephalitis, and Lymphocytic Choriomeningitis Virus (LCMV)!
  - Neutrophilic Predominance in Viral Encephalitis: Neutrophils, rather than lymphocytes, predominate in the initial CSF in up to 37% to 50% of West Nile virus cases, Eastern Equine encephalitis, and early Enterovirus 71 cases!
  - Absence of Rash in VZV Vasculopathy: Up to 30% to 60% of patients with VZV CNS vasculopathy and encephalitis have NO history or physical evidence of a preceding or concurrent zoster skin rash (zoster sine herpete)!
  - Subendocardial / Lentiform Sparing in HSV-1: HSV-1 encephalitis strictly spares the lentiform nucleus and basal ganglia, which helps distinguish it from Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA) ischemic infarction on MRI!
  - Acyclovir Delay Danger: Delaying empiric acyclovir administration for >2 days after hospital admission increases mortality and poor neurological outcomes by >3-fold; acyclovir must be given in the emergency department prior to neuroimaging or LP!
