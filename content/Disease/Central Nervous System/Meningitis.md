# Meningitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Meningitis is an acute, subacute, or chronic inflammatory process involving the meninges (specifically the pia mater, arachnoid mater, and intervening subarachnoid space), clinically identified by an abnormal number of white blood cells (pleocytosis, typically defined as >5 WBCs/mm3) in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Acute Meningitis: Onset of meningeal symptoms and signs within hours to a few days (duration <5 days; accounts for ~75% of community-acquired cases). Subdivided into: (1) Acute Bacterial / Purulent Meningitis—rapidly progressive, life-threatening neutrophilic inflammation of the subarachnoid space; and (2) Acute Aseptic / Viral Meningitis—self-limited lymphocytic/mononuclear inflammation with negative routine bacterial CSF cultures.
  - Subacute Meningitis: Duration of symptoms from 5 days up to 4 weeks. Patients are more likely to have comorbidities, immunosuppression, fungal or mycobacterial etiologies, focal neurological deficits, and hypoglycorrhachia (CSF glucose <45 mg/dL).
  - Chronic Meningitis: Indolent onset of symptoms and signs of meningeal inflammation persisting for 4 weeks or more, accompanied by persistent CSF pleocytosis, elevated protein, and hypoglycorrhachia. Major etiologies include fungal infections (Cryptococcus, Coccidioides, Histoplasma), tuberculosis, neurosyphilis, Lyme neuroborreliosis, brucellosis, and noninfectious autoimmune/neoplastic conditions.
  - Recurrent Aseptic Meningitis / Mollaret Meningitis: Repeated, self-limited episodes of benign lymphocytic meningitis separated by completely symptom-free intervals, predominantly caused by Herpes Simplex Virus Type 2 (HSV-2; rarely HSV-1 or EBV) or noninfectious triggers (intermittently leaking epidermoid cysts, craniopharyngiomas, NSAIDs, IVIG).
  - Health Care-Associated Ventriculitis and Meningitis (formerly Nosocomial Meningitis): Infections acquired after neurosurgical operations, penetrating head trauma, or secondary to indwelling CSF diversion hardware (ventriculoperitoneal shunts, external ventricular drains (EVDs), deep brain stimulators, Ommaya reservoirs), characterized by staphylococcal or aerobic Gram-negative bacillary predominance.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Global & US Burden: The Global Burden of Disease Study estimated that meningitis causes approximately 318,000 deaths annually worldwide (4.5 per 100,000 persons). Incidence varies geographically: 0.7 to 0.9 cases per 100,000 per year in the United States and Europe, compared to 10 to 40 cases per 100,000 per year in Sub-Saharan Africa (peaking at 100 per 100,000 during epidemic waves). Viral/aseptic meningitis is far more common, with 10 to 15 million symptomatic enteroviral infections occurring annually in the US (resulting in 30,000 to 75,000 hospitalizations for enteroviral meningitis).
  - Impact of Conjugate Vaccines: Widespread vaccination has dramatically altered bacterial meningitis epidemiology. Conjugate vaccines against Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) reduced Hib meningitis cases by >90%. Heptavalent (PCV7) and 13-valent (PCV13) pneumococcal conjugate vaccines reduced vaccine-serotype pneumococcal meningitis from 0.61 to 0.05 cases per 100,000 (though non-vaccine serotype replacement occurred). Quadrivalent meningococcal conjugate vaccine (ACWY) reduced US meningococcal disease incidence from 0.92 to 0.33 per 100,000. Mass administration of MenAfriVac (serogroup A conjugate) in Sub-Saharan Africa reduced meningococcal disease by 57% and nearly eliminated serogroup A, although epidemics of serogroups C and W have emerged subsequently.
  - Demographic & Clinical Risk Factors: Bimodal age distribution with highest risk in infants <1 year (up to 5.38 per 100,000 for meningococcal disease) and adults >50-60 years; splenectomy / functional or anatomical asplenia; complement deficiencies (C5-C9, properdin, or factor D deficiencies strongly predispose to recurrent Neisseria meningitidis); hypogammaglobulinemia or X-linked agammaglobulinemia; HIV/AIDS (25% of community-acquired meningitis in certain US urban series have HIV coinfection; high risk for Cryptococcus and Salmonella); basilar skull fractures with CSF rhinorrhea/otorrhea (predisposes to recurrent Streptococcus pneumoniae); indwelling neurosurgical hardware/EVDs (86% of healthcare-associated cases relate to EVDs); dietary exposures (Listeria monocytogenes outbreaks linked to unpasteurized cheese, milk, coleslaw, deli meats, cantaloupe, hog head cheese); occupational or pig contact (Streptococcus suis in Southeast Asia, causing 25% to 30% of adult cases in Vietnam); and crowded living conditions (military recruits, college dormitories, Hajj pilgrims).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Viruses (Leading Cause of Aseptic Meningitis Overall): Nonpolio Enteroviruses (Coxsackievirus A6, A1, B3, Echoviruses 18, 30, EV-71, EV-D68, and Human Parechovirus 3) account for 48% to 95% of all cases with an identified viral pathogen. Herpes Simplex Virus Type 2 (HSV-2; primary cause of Mollaret recurrent meningitis and acute aseptic meningitis in young adults), Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV), West Nile Virus (WNV), Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6), HIV (during acute seroconversion or CSF viral escape/CD8 encephalitis), Mumps virus (in unimmunized populations), and Lymphocytic Choriomeningitis Virus (LCMV; rodent-borne).
  - Bacteria (Community-Acquired Bacterial Meningitis): Streptococcus pneumoniae (accounts for 58% of adult bacterial cases in the US; leading cause overall), Neisseria meningitidis (14% of US cases; serogroups B, C, Y predominate in US; A, C, W, X in Africa), Streptococcus agalactiae / Group B Streptococcus (18% of US cases, predominant in neonates and elderly), Listeria monocytogenes (3% to 8% of US cases; high in elderly/immunocompromised), and Haemophilus influenzae (3% to 7% of cases).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Neonates (<1 month): Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus; causes 50% to 77% of neonatal cases), Escherichia coli (75% carry the K1 capsular antigen), Listeria monocytogenes, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enteroviruses, Human Parechovirus type 3, and Herpes Simplex Virus type 2 (HSV-2).
  - Infants & Toddlers (1 to 23 months): Streptococcus agalactiae, Escherichia coli, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae type b (in unimmunized populations), Enteroviruses, and Human Parechoviruses.
  - Children & Adults (2 to 50 years): Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Enteroviruses, and HSV-2.
  - Older Adults (>50-60 years): Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Listeria monocytogenes, Aerobic Gram-negative bacilli (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Proteus mirabilis), and Streptococcus agalactiae.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Bacteria & Mycobacteria: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (tuberculous meningitis), Treponema pallidum (neurosyphilis), Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme neuroborreliosis), Streptococcus suis (leading cause of bacterial meningitis in Southeast Asia/Vietnam), Nocardia species, Brucella species (neurobrucellosis), Capnocytophaga canimorsus (post-dog bite/asplenia), Pasteurella multocida, Cutibacterium acnes (formerly Propionibacterium acnes; associated with CSF shunts/drains), Salmonella species (in HIV/AIDS or infants in resource-poor settings), Leptospira interrogans, Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae, Rickettsia rickettsii, Orientia tsutsugamushi, Ehrlichia, Anaplasma, and Strongyloides stercoralis hyperinfection syndrome (carrying enteric Gram-negative bacilli like Citrobacter koseri or E. coli into CSF).
  - Fungi: Cryptococcus neoformans and Cryptococcus gattii (leading cause of fungal meningitis in HIV/AIDS, SOT, or immunocompetent hosts), Coccidioides immitis/posadasii, Histoplasma capsulatum, Candida species (Candida albicans; in neonates, neurosurgery, or homozygous CARD9 deficiency), Blastomyces dermatitidis, Sporothrix schenckii, Aspergillus species (iatrogenic contaminated methylprednisolone injections or invasive mold sinusitis extension), Exserohilum rostratum, Scedosporium apiospermum, and Cladophialophora.
  - Parasites & Helminths: Naegleria fowleri (Primary Amebic Meningoencephalitis / PAM; warm freshwater exposure, 97% mortality), Angiostrongylus cantonensis (eosinophilic meningitis from raw mollusks/snails), Baylisascaris procyonis (raccoon roundworm), Toxocara species, Taenia solium (neurocysticercosis), Strongyloides stercoralis, Acanthamoeba species, Balamuthia mandrillaris, and Gnathostoma spinigerum.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Nasopharyngeal Colonization & Invasion: Encapsulated bacterial pathogens (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae) colonize the nasopharyngeal mucosal epithelium using pili, fimbriae, and cell-surface adhesins. Bacteria secrete IgA proteases to cleave mucosal IgA, evade mucociliary clearance, and invade the intravascular space via transcellular or paracellular transport, establishing systemic bacteremia.
  - Intravascular Survival & BBB Penetration: Bacteria resist intravascular complement-mediated lysis and neutrophil phagocytosis due to dense polysaccharide capsules. Circulating bacteria cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) at the level of brain microvascular endothelial cells or choroid plexus epithelial cells by binding to specific surface receptors (e.g., S. pneumoniae ChoP binding to PAF receptor; N. meningitidis PilC/PilD binding to CD147; E. coli OmpA/Sfa binding to endothelial receptors).
  - Multiplication in Subarachnoid Space: Once inside the subarachnoid space, bacteria multiply rapidly in an environment essentially devoid of host humoral defenses (normal CSF lacks functional complement proteins, immunoglobulins, and opsonic activity).
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Release of Bacterial Inflammatory Products: Bacterial multiplication and antibiotic-induced cell lysis release potent Pathogen-Associated Molecular Patterns (PAMPs)—such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS / endotoxin / LOS) from Gram-negatives, or peptidoglycan, teichoic acid, and pneumolysin from Gram-positives—into the subarachnoid space.
  - Cytokine Cascade & Neutrophil Recruitment: PAMPs activate microglial cells, astrocytes, and cerebrovascular endothelial cells via Toll-like receptors (TLR2, TLR4, MyD88 pathway), releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines: Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-alpha), Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1beta), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), Interleukin-8 (IL-8), Platelet-Activating Factor (PAF), and Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs). Cytokines upregulate endothelial adhesion molecules (E-selectin, ICAM-1), triggering massive recruitment of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (neutrophils) across the BBB into the CSF.
  - Vasogenic, Cytotoxic & Interstitial Edema: Increased BBB permeability leads to protein-rich fluid extravasation into the CSF (vasogenic edema). Toxic products from neutrophils (reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, MMPs, cathepsins) cause direct damage to neurons and glia (cytotoxic edema). Accumulation of thick, purulent exudate in the subarachnoid space blocks CSF outflow pathways at the arachnoid villi, producing communicating hydrocephalus (interstitial edema).
  - Cerebrovascular Vasculitis & Ischemia: Subarachnoid inflammation extends to blood vessels coursing through the subarachnoid space, inducing cerebral vasculitis, arterial narrowing, microvascular thrombosis, and loss of cerebrovascular autoregulation. This results in cortical hypoperfusion, regional hypoxia, intracellular acidosis (elevated CSF lactate), cerebral infarction, and elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) with potential brainstem herniation.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Classic Triad of Acute Bacterial Meningitis: Fever (>=80%), Severe Headache (>=85%; generalized, constant), and Nuchal Rigidity / Meningismus (>=80%). Accompanied by Altered Sensorium / Mental Status Changes (>=75%; ranging from confusion, disorientation, and lethargy to stupor and coma). The presence of at least ONE finding from the triad (fever, neck stiffness, or altered mental status) is 99% to 100% sensitive for eliminating acute bacterial meningitis in adults!
  - Associated Symptoms: Nausea and vomiting (~35%), photophobia, phonophobia, generalized or focal seizures (~30%), and focal motor/sensory deficits. In infants and young children: irritability, poor feeding, abnormal high-pitched cry, bulging fontanelle, vomiting, and lethargy (fever or classic neck stiffness may be completely absent!).
  - Viral / Aseptic Meningitis Presentation: Acute onset of fever, frontal or retro-orbital headache, photophobia, neck stiffness, nausea, vomiting, myalgias, and constitutional fatigue; sensorium is preserved or only mildly dulled (absence of severe encephalopathy differentiates uncomplicated viral meningitis from viral encephalitis).
  - Chronic Meningitis Symptoms: Insidious, progressive onset of headache, low-grade fever, weight loss, night sweats, confusion, memory loss, cranial nerve palsies (diplopia, facial weakness, hearing loss), and progressive dementia due to hydrocephalus.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Classic Meningeal Signs:
    1. Nuchal Rigidity: Inability to flex the neck forward passively to touch the chin to the chest due to severe spasm of extensor neck muscles.
    2. Kernig Sign: Inability to extend the knee beyond 135 degrees when the patient is supine with the hip flexed to 90 degrees, due to painful hamstring spasm.
    3. Brudzinski Sign: Passive flexion of the neck induces involuntary, reflexive flexion of the hips and knees.
    4. Jolt Accentuation of Headache: Exacerbation of baseline headache produced by rotating the head horizontally at a frequency of 2 to 3 turns per second.
    - Diagnostic Accuracy Note: Classic meningeal signs (Kernig, Brudzinski) have low sensitivity (Kernig 27%, Brudzinski 51%, Nuchal rigidity 65% in children) and may be absent in neonates, elderly, or severely neutropenic/immunocompromised patients!
  - Cutaneous & Systemic Physical Findings: Petechial or purpuric non-blanching skin rash (classic for Neisseria meningitidis bacteremia, appearing on trunk, lower extremities, or palpebral conjunctivae); Purpura Fulminans (widespread skin necrosis, ecchymoses, and distal gangrene secondary to DIC); Osler nodes or Janeway lesions (infective endocarditis with embolic bacterial meningitis); Papilledema (<5% on admission, indicates elevated ICP or parameningeal focus); Cranial nerve palsies (CN III, IV, VI, VII, VIII; CN VII palsy is classic in Lyme meningitis; CN VI palsy indicates elevated ICP).
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Petechial / Purpuric Skin Eruption with Meningismus: Pathognomonic for Neisseria meningitidis bacteremia and acute meningococcal meningitis.
  - Rule of 7s in Children: Clinical prediction rule classifying children at low risk for Lyme meningitis vs. viral meningitis (<7 days of headache, <70% CSF mononuclear cells, and absence of 7th or other cranial nerve palsy).
  - Froin Syndrome: Coagulation of xanthochromic, hyperviscous CSF immediately upon exposure to air due to extremely high protein concentrations (>1000 mg/dL), pathognomonic for total spinal block or massive meningeal inflammation.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis (>12,000/mm3) with a left shift (bandemia >10%) in acute bacterial cases; severe bandemia (>20%) correlates with high risk for bacteremia. Leukopenia (<4000/mm3) or low CSF cell count indicates poor host response and carries a worse prognosis. Thrombocytopenia indicates DIC.
  - Inflammatory Biomarkers: Serum C-reactive Protein (CRP) (>20-50 mg/L) and Serum Procalcitonin (>0.2 to 0.5 ng/mL). A serum procalcitonin cutoff of 0.28 ng/mL demonstrates 95% sensitivity and 100% specificity in discriminating bacterial from viral meningitis. Normal CRP or procalcitonin has a high negative predictive value to exclude bacterial infection.
  - Serum Glucose & Blood Cultures: Serum glucose MUST be drawn simultaneously immediately before lumbar puncture to calculate the CSF-to-serum glucose ratio. Two sets of blood cultures should be drawn in all patients prior to antimicrobial administration (positive in 50% to 80% of untreated bacterial cases).
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Indications for Cranial CT / MRI Prior to Lumbar Puncture (IDSA Guidelines): Neuroimaging prior to LP is recommended ONLY in patients with: (1) Immunocompromised state (HIV/AIDS, immunosuppressive therapy, post-transplant); (2) History of CNS disease (mass lesion, stroke, focal infection); (3) New-onset seizure; (4) Abnormal level of consciousness (GCS score <10-12); (5) Papilledema; or (6) Focal neurological deficit (dilated nonreactive pupil, gaze palsy, arm/leg drift).
    - Emergency Protocol: If CT is indicated, blood cultures MUST be drawn and empiric antimicrobial therapy + dexamethasone MUST be administered IMMEDIATELY before sending the patient to the scanner, to avoid fatal treatment delays!
  - Neuroimaging Findings: Contrast-enhanced MRI (preferred over CT) demonstrates diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement over cerebral convexities or basilar cisterns (classic basilar enhancement in tuberculous and fungal meningitis). Evaluates complications: cerebral edema, sulcal effacement, hydrocephalus, brain infarction, subdural empyema, epidural abscess, or suppurative venous sinus thrombosis.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) Analysis & Parameter Profiles:
    - Opening Pressure: Normal 50 to 195 mm H2O. Elevated (>200 to 600+ mm H2O) in bacterial, fungal, and tuberculous meningitis.
    - Leukocyte Count & Differential: Normal 0 to 5 WBCs/mm3. Acute Bacterial: 1000 to 5000+ WBCs/mm3 (>90% neutrophils/PMNs; low WBC <20/mm3 predicts poor prognosis). Viral / Aseptic: 50 to 500 WBCs/mm3 (lymphocyte predominance, though early enterovirus/WNV may show transient PMNs). Fungal / Tuberculous: 100 to 500 WBCs/mm3 (lymphocyte/mononuclear predominance).
    - CSF Glucose & Ratio: Normal CSF-to-serum ratio ~0.6 (absolute >45 mg/dL). Acute Bacterial: Markedly low (<40 mg/dL or ratio <0.4; ratio <0.23 predicts bacterial with 99% certainty). Fungal / TB: Low (<40 mg/dL). Viral: Normal (>45 mg/dL or ratio >0.5).
    - CSF Protein: Normal 15 to 45 mg/dL. Acute Bacterial: High (100 to 500+ mg/dL). Fungal / TB: High (100 to 500+ mg/dL). Viral: Mildly elevated (50 to 100 mg/dL).
    - CSF Lactate: Elevated (>3.5 to 4.2 mmol/L) demonstrates 93% to 97% sensitivity and 94% to 96% specificity in differentiating bacterial from aseptic meningitis.
  - Stains & Microscopy: CSF Gram stain permits rapid identification (sensitivity 60% to 90% overall; 90% in S. pneumoniae, 86% in H. influenzae, 75% in N. meningitidis, 24% to 32% in L. monocytogenes). Acid-fast stain (Ziehl-Neelsen / fluorochrome) positive in <15% to 25% of TB cases. India ink positive in 50% to 75% of Cryptococcal cases (88% in AIDS). Giemsa/Wright stain for Naegleria fowleri or Angiostrongylus.
  - CSF Cultures & Antigen Tests: CSF bacterial culture is positive in 80% to 90% of untreated community-acquired cases (declines to <50% after prior antibiotics). Cryptococcal antigen (CRAG) lateral flow assay (>95% sensitive).
  - Molecular Diagnostics (Multiplex PCR): Rapid multiplex PCR panels (e.g., BioFire FilmArray ME Panel) simultaneously detect 14 targets: 6 bacteria (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, S. agalactiae, H. influenzae, L. monocytogenes, E. coli K1), 7 viruses (HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, CMV, HHV-6, Enterovirus, Parechovirus), and 2 fungi (Cryptococcus neoformans/gattii). GeneXpert MTB/RIF or Xpert Ultra PCR for M. tuberculosis.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) Culture demonstrating growth of the specific pathogenic microorganism (or molecular PCR detection of pathogen nucleic acid) obtained via lumbar puncture remains the definitive gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Emergency Stabilization & ICU Admission: Immediate ICU admission for monitoring of consciousness, ICP, seizures, and hemodynamic stability. Resuscitate with IV crystalloids to maintain intravascular volume and cerebral perfusion pressure (MAP >=65 mm Hg).
  - Adjunctive Dexamethasone Protocol:
    - Dosing & Timing: Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV q6h (or 10 mg IV q6h) for 4 days. MUST be administered CONCOMITANTLY WITH or 20 to 30 minutes BEFORE the first dose of antimicrobial therapy to attenuate subarachnoid space inflammatory response and cytokine release!
    - Indications & Benefits: Recommended in adults with pneumococcal meningitis (reduces mortality from 34% to 14% and unfavorable outcomes from 52% to 26%) and in infants/children with H. influenzae type b and pneumococcal meningitis (reduces severe hearing loss). Discontinue dexamethasone if non-pneumococcal / non-Hib etiology is confirmed (though some European guidelines continue for 4 days regardless). (Note: Dexamethasone lacks benefit in high-HIV, resource-poor settings like Malawi).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Empiric Regimens by Age & Predisposing Factor (Table 87.12):
    - Neonates (<1 month): Ampicillin (150-200 mg/kg/day divided q6-8h) PLUS Cefotaxime (100-200 mg/kg/day divided q6-8h) OR Ampicillin PLUS Gentamicin.
    - Infants & Children (1 to 23 months): Vancomycin (15-20 mg/kg IV q8-12h, trough 15-20 mcg/mL) PLUS Third-Generation Cephalosporin (Ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg/day IV divided q12h OR Cefotaxime 225-300 mg/kg/day divided q6-8h) PLUS Dexamethasone.
    - Children & Adults (2 to 50 years): Vancomycin (15-20 mg/kg IV q8-12h or 30-45 mg/kg/day, targeting trough 15-20 mcg/mL) PLUS Third-Generation Cephalosporin (Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h or 4 g/day OR Cefotaxime 2 g IV q4-6h or 8-12 g/day) PLUS Dexamethasone.
    - Older Adults (>50 years) or Immunocompromised / Alcoholism / Pregnancy: Vancomycin PLUS Ceftriaxone (or Cefotaxime) PLUS Ampicillin (2 g IV q4h or 12 g/day, to cover Listeria monocytogenes) PLUS Dexamethasone.
    - Healthcare-Associated / Post-Neurosurgery / Head Trauma / CSF Drain: Vancomycin PLUS Antipseudomonal Beta-Lactam (Cefepime 2 g IV q8h OR Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h OR Meropenem 2 g IV q8h).
    - Basilar Skull Fracture: Vancomycin PLUS Third-Generation Cephalosporin (Ceftriaxone/Cefotaxime).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Severe Beta-Lactam / Penicillin Allergy Regimens: Vancomycin PLUS Moxifloxacin (400 mg IV q24h) or Ciprofloxacin PLUS Aztreonam (2 g IV q6-8h, for Gram-negatives) PLUS Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX 5 mg/kg IV q6-8h, for Listeria).
  - Pathogen-Directed Targeted Therapies & Treatment Durations:
    - Streptococcus pneumoniae: Vancomycin + Ceftriaxone x 10 to 14 days (if Penicillin MIC <=0.06 mcg/mL: Penicillin G 24 MU/day or Ampicillin; if Ceftriaxone MIC >=2.0 mcg/mL: Vancomycin + Ceftriaxone +/- Rifampin 600 mg q12-24h).
    - Neisseria meningitidis: Penicillin G (24 MU/day) or Ampicillin x 7 days (Ceftriaxone if Penicillin MIC 0.1-1.0 mcg/mL).
    - Haemophilus influenzae: Ceftriaxone or Cefotaxime x 7 days (Ampicillin if beta-lactamase negative).
    - Listeria monocytogenes: Ampicillin (12 g/day) or Penicillin G PLUS Gentamicin (5 mg/kg/day) x 21 days minimum. (TMP-SMX in penicillin allergy).
    - Streptococcus agalactiae: Ampicillin or Penicillin G PLUS Gentamicin x 14 to 21 days.
    - Aerobic Gram-Negative Bacilli (E. coli, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas): Cefepime, Ceftazidime, or Meropenem x 21 days (3 weeks). (Intraventricular/intrathecal Colistin or Polymyxin B for carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter or K. pneumoniae).
    - Tuberculous Meningitis: 4-drug therapy (Isoniazid, Rifampin, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol) x 2 months, then Isoniazid + Rifampin x 7 to 10 months (total 9-12 months) PLUS adjunctive Dexamethasone/Prednisolone.
    - Cryptococcal Meningitis: Induction with Liposomal Amphotericin B (3-4 mg/kg IV daily) PLUS Flucytosine (100 mg/kg/day divided q6h) x 2 weeks, followed by Fluconazole 400-800 mg daily x 8 weeks, then 200 mg daily maintenance.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Lumbar Puncture / Serial Drainage LPs: Essential initial diagnostic procedure (unless contraindicated by mass effect/herniation signs). Serial therapeutic drainage LPs indicated in Cryptococcal meningitis for elevated opening pressure (>250 mm H2O) to maintain CSF pressure <200 mm H2O.
  - Neurosurgical Debridement & Hardware Removal: Surgical evacuation of parameningeal infectious foci (subdural empyema, brain abscess, mastoidectomy, sinus debridement). In healthcare-associated ventriculitis/drain infections: complete removal of infected CSF shunt/EVD hardware, placement of temporary external ventricular drain, and re-implantation of new hardware only after CSF sterilization.
  - Intraventricular / Intrathecal Antimicrobial Administration: Administration of preservative-free antimicrobials (e.g., Vancomycin 10-20 mg/day, Gentamicin 4-8 mg/day, Colistin 10 mg/day, Polymyxin B) directly into lateral ventricles via EVD for refractory multidrug-resistant Gram-negative (Acinetobacter, Klebsiella) or staphylococcal ventriculitis/meningitis failing systemic therapy.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Elevated Intracranial Pressure (ICP) & Brain Herniation (transtentorial, uncal, or tonsillar herniation; most feared early complication).
  - Cerebral Vasculitis, Vasospasm, Microvascular Thrombosis, and Ischemic / Hemorrhagic Stroke (occurring in 15% to 20% of adult cases; delayed cerebral thrombosis occurs in 1% to 4% of adults 1 to 2 weeks post-recovery).
  - Communicating or Non-Communicating Hydrocephalus (requiring EVD or VP shunt).
  - Subdural Effusions and Subdural Empyema (common in infants with H. influenzae or pneumococcal meningitis).
  - Seizures and Status Epilepticus (~30% of cases).
  - Systemic Compromise: Septic shock, Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome (bilateral hemorrhagic adrenal necrosis in meningococcemia), Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) with purpura fulminans, ARDS, and Hyponatremia (SIADH vs. cerebral salt wasting).
  - High Case Fatality: Mortality ranges from 3% to 13% for N. meningitidis, 15% to 30% for S. pneumoniae, 15% to 29% for L. monocytogenes, up to 54% in Malawi, and 40% to 58% for neonatal meningitis in developing countries.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Sensorineural Hearing Loss (most common permanent sequela, occurring in 10% to 30% of childhood/adult pneumococcal and H. influenzae survivors; reduced by adjunctive dexamethasone).
  - Cognitive Impairment, Memory Loss, and Intellectual Disability (executive dysfunction in >30% of adult survivors).
  - Chronic Epilepsy / Seizure Disorder.
  - Persistent Focal Neurological Deficits (hemiparesis, cranial nerve palsies, ataxia).
  - Hydrocephalus requiring permanent VP shunt placement.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Classic Triad: Fever + Headache + Nuchal Rigidity (present in >80% individually, 99-100% sensitive for at least one).
  - Kernig Sign: Inability to extend knee >135 degrees when hip is flexed 90 degrees.
  - Brudzinski Sign: Involuntary hip/knee flexion upon passive neck flexion.
  - Jolt Accentuation: Worsening of headache by rotating head horizontally 2-3 times per second.
  - Waterhouse-Friderichsen Syndrome: Bilateral adrenal hemorrhage and fulminant shock in meningococcemia.
  - Meningitis Belt: Sub-Saharan Africa region (Senegal to Ethiopia) with dry season epidemic meningococcal waves.
  - Harmattan: Dry desert wind in Sub-Saharan Africa associated with peak meningococcal outbreaks.
  - Froin Syndrome: Coagulation of xanthochromic CSF due to massive protein elevation (>1000 mg/dL) from spinal blockage.
  - Rule of 7s: Clinical prediction rule classifying children at low risk for Lyme meningitis (<7d headache, <70% mononuclear cells, no CN VII palsy).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Absence of Pleocytosis in Culture-Proven Meningitis: Up to 8% of CNS infections and 0.2% of pneumococcal / 10% of meningococcal meningitis cases present with a completely NORMAL CSF cell count (<5 WBCs/mm3) or very low WBC count (0-20/mm3); low CSF cell count strongly predicts a POOR prognosis!
  - Dexamethasone Timing Rule: Dexamethasone MUST be given before or with the first antibiotic dose; starting dexamethasone >4 hours (US) or >12 hours (UK) after antibiotics yields NO benefit and is NOT recommended!
  - Dexamethasone Ineffectiveness in Low-Income / High-HIV Settings: Adjunctive dexamethasone failed to show mortality benefit in Malawian trials where HIV prevalence was high (>80%) and presentation was delayed; dexamethasone is NOT routinely recommended in resource-poor HIV-endemic settings!
  - Cephalosporin Resistance & Vancomycin Synergy: Third-generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone/cefotaxime) CANNOT be used alone for empiric pneumococcal meningitis in areas with cephalosporin resistance (>1%); vancomycin MUST be added for bactericidal synergy!
  - Second-Generation Cephalosporins Contraindicated: Second-generation cephalosporins (e.g., cefuroxime) are EXPLICITLY CONTRAINDICATED for bacterial meningitis due to delayed CSF sterilization and higher rates of hearing loss compared to ceftriaxone/cefotaxime!
  - Normal CSF Glucose in Viral Meningitis Exception: While viral meningitis typically features normal CSF glucose, hypoglycorrhachia (<40 mg/dL) CAN occur in Mumps, LCMV, CMV, and Eastern Equine Encephalitis!
