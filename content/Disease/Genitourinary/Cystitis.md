# Cystitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Acute cystitis is an inflammatory infection or non-infectious condition involving the mucosal lining (urothelium) and submucosa of the urinary bladder. Clinically, it is characterized by lower urinary tract irritative symptoms including dysuria (painful or burning micturition), urinary frequency (frequent voiding of small volumes), urinary urgency (compulsive, painful desire to void), and occasionally suprapubic discomfort or heaviness, turbid urine, and gross or microscopic hematuria. Microbiologically, in acute uncomplicated cystitis, it is defined by the presence of 10^3 or more colony-forming units (CFU)/mL (or 10^2 CFU/mL or more in IDSA consensus guidelines) of a uropathogen in midstream clean-catch urine from a symptomatic female.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Acute Uncomplicated Cystitis: Lower urinary tract infection occurring in a healthy, nonpregnant, premenopausal, immunocompetent adult female with a structurally, functionally, and neurologically normal urinary tract.
  - Complicated Cystitis: Cystitis occurring in the presence of anatomical, functional, or host metabolic factors that increase the risk for treatment failure, persistent or relapsing infection, or acquisition of multidrug-resistant uropathogens. This includes all male patients (all male UTIs are considered complicated), pregnant women, postmenopausal women, children, hospitalized or institutionalized patients, individuals with indwelling urinary catheters or drainage devices, urinary tract obstruction or calculi, neurogenic bladder, renal insufficiency, diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, or recent urologic instrumentation/antibiotic exposure.
  - Recurrent Cystitis: Defined as 2 or more episodes of acute cystitis within 6 months, or 3 or more episodes within 12 months. Subdivided into: (1) Reinfection (recurrence with a different bacterial strain or species, or the same strain after initial sterilization, accounting for >80% of recurrences); and (2) Relapse (recurrence with the same original bacterial strain within 2 weeks of completing therapy due to bacterial persistence in bladder umbrella cells / quiescent intracellular reservoirs, upper tract, calculi, or prostate).
  - Special Etiologic & Clinical Variants:
    - Hemorrhagic Cystitis: Marked by severe gross hematuria and sloughing of bladder mucosa; caused by uropathogenic E. coli, Adenovirus (types 11 and 35, especially in young boys, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant / HSCT recipients, and renal transplants), BK polyomavirus, cyclophosphamide or ifosfamide chemotherapy toxicity, or pelvic radiation.
    - Emphysematous Cystitis: Severe necrotizing infection characterized by gas accumulation within the bladder wall and lumen; predominantly seen in elderly diabetic females, caused by gas-forming enteric bacilli (E. coli, Klebsiella, Proteus).
    - Encrusted Cystitis: Chronic inflammatory cystitis marked by calcified/struvite mucosal plaques and high urine pH (>8.0), caused by urea-splitting Corynebacterium urealyticum (formerly group D2) in renal transplant or catheterized hosts.
    - Interstitial Cystitis / Bladder Pain Syndrome: Noninfectious chronic inflammatory syndrome characterized by pelvic/suprapubic pain and dysuria in the absence of bacterial infection.
    - Eosinophilic Cystitis: Rare noninfectious transmural inflammatory lesion characterized by eosinophilic infiltration, hematuria, and frequency.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - General Population & Disease Burden: Cystitis is one of the most common bacterial infections in human medicine. Up to 60% of adult women experience at least one symptomatic episode of cystitis during their lifespan, and 10% of adult women experience a UTI each year. Recurrent cystitis develops in 2% to 5% of healthy women (24% develop a second episode within 6 months of a first E. coli cystitis, and up to 70% experience recurrence within 1 year).
  - Sexual Activity & Behavioral Factors: Recent sexual intercourse (frequency of coitus is the single strongest independent risk factor for acute cystitis in young women; "honeymoon cystitis"); a new sex partner within the past year; use of spermicidal contraceptives (nonoxynol-9 increases E. coli adherence and coliform vaginal colonization while depleting protective lactobacilli); diaphragm use (causes mild urethral compression and spermicide exposure); condom use with spermicide; lack of postcoital voiding; and recent antimicrobial exposure (profoundly disrupts protective vaginal and gut microflora).
  - Estrogen Status: Estrogen deficiency in postmenopausal women leads to loss of glycogen-rich vaginal squamous epithelium, loss of protective hydrogen peroxide-producing Lactobacillus species, elevation of vaginal pH (>4.5), and replacement by coliform uropathogenic bacteria.
  - Genetic Susceptibility: Familial predisposition (history of UTIs in first-degree female relatives); Nonsecretor status of blood group antigens (ABH and Lewis nonsecretors express unique globoseries glycosphingolipid receptors on uroepithelial cells that avidly bind class II PapG and Dr adhesins of UPEC); and single nucleotide polymorphisms in Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) or CXCR1/CXCR2 IL-8 receptors.
  - Anatomic & Functional Factors in Men & Older Adults: Lack of circumcision in young men (increases E. coli periurethral carriage); insertive rectal intercourse; prostatic hypertrophy and incomplete bladder emptying; urinary incontinence and bladder prolapse; neurogenic bladder; urinary catheterization; and lower socioeconomic status.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Escherichia coli (Uropathogenic E. coli / UPEC): Causes 75% to 90% of acute uncomplicated cystitis episodes in ambulatory outpatients.
  - Staphylococcus saprophyticus: A coagulase-negative staphylococcus that exhibits enhanced adherence to uroepithelial cells; accounts for 5% to 15% of acute uncomplicated cystitis in young, sexually active females (predominantly in late summer and fall).
  - Klebsiella pneumoniae: Accounts for 4% to 8% of cases.
  - Proteus mirabilis: Accounts for 3% to 5% of cases.
  - Enterococcus faecalis: Accounts for 1% to 3% of cases.
  - Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus / GBS): Accounts for 1% to 2% of cases, particularly in pregnant women and diabetics.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Infants & Children (<5 years): E. coli (leading cause in both sexes), Enterococcus faecalis (important in infants <3 months), Proteus mirabilis (more common in uncircumcised young boys), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Adenovirus type 11 (causes hemorrhagic cystitis in young boys).
  - Young Sexually Active Premenopausal Females (18–45 years): E. coli (80%–90%), Staphylococcus saprophyticus (5%–15%), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis.
  - Postmenopausal & Elderly Females: E. coli (frequency decreases to 50%–60%), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, Enterobacter species, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococcus faecalis, Streptococcus agalactiae.
  - Adult Males & Complicated Hosts: E. coli (40%–50%), Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter species, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococcus faecalis, Serratia marcescens, Providencia stuartii, Morganella morganii, and coagulase-negative staphylococci.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Fastidious Bacteria & Higher Organisms: Corynebacterium urealyticum (slow-growing, urea-splitting, gram-positive bacillus causing encrusted cystitis and struvite stones in immunosuppressed/transplant patients); Actinobaculum schaalii (facultative anaerobic gram-positive bacillus causing urosepsis/cystitis in elderly >65 years, missed on routine cultures); Aerococcus urinae; Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and Mycoplasma genitalium (causes of acute urethral syndrome / sterile pyuria mimicking cystitis); Gardnerella vaginalis, Ureaplasma urealyticum, and Mycoplasma hominis.
  - Viruses: Adenovirus (types 11 and 35; major cause of acute hemorrhagic cystitis in children, HSCT/BMT recipients, and renal transplants); BK Polyomavirus and JC Polyomavirus (hemorrhagic cystitis in allogeneic HSCT recipients); Cytomegalovirus (CMV); Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1/2).
  - Fungi: Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, Candida krusei, Candida tropicalis (fungal cystitis in catheterized, diabetic, or ICU patients receiving broad-spectrum antibiotics).
  - Parasites: Schistosoma haematobium (causes chronic schistosomiasis of the bladder with terminal hematuria, bladder wall calcifications, and squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Ascending Retrograde Infection: Bacteria originating from the bowel fecal reservoir colonize the perineum, vaginal introitus, and periurethral area. Mechanical forces (sexual intercourse, urethral massage, catheterization) propel bacteria up the short female urethra into the bladder lumen.
  - Uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) Virulence Factors & Adhesins:
    - Type 1 Fimbriae (Mannose-Sensitive): Encoded by the fim gene cluster (fimA-I, fimH adhesin). Universal in UPEC; binds mannosylated glycoproteins (uroplakin Ia and Ib) on superficial umbrella cells of the bladder urothelium. Type 1 fimbriae-mediated binding is essential for establishing cystitis, promoting cell invasion, and inducing intracellular bacterial community (IBC) formation.
    - Dr Family Adhesins (DraA-D, AfaE): Bind decay-accelerating factor (DAF) and type IV collagen, mediating urothelial cell invasion and chronic persistence.
    - Type 3 Fimbriae (mrkABCDF): Contribute to biofilm formation on urinary catheters and bladder surfaces.
    - Bacterial Toxins & Enzymes: Alpha-hemolysin (hlyA; pore-forming toxin that lyses bladder epithelial cells, inhibits cytokine production, and releases iron); Cytotoxic Necrotizing Factor type 1 (cnf1; disrupts host cell cytoskeleton and modulates neutrophil function); Secreted Autotransporter Toxin (sat); Aerobactin (iucC) and catecholate siderophore receptors (iroN, iha) for iron acquisition in iron-poor urine.
  - Intracellular Bacterial Communities (IBCs) & Quiescent Intracellular Reservoirs (QIRs): Following Type 1 fimbriae-mediated attachment to uroplakins, UPEC invade superficial umbrella cells. Once intracellular, bacteria replicate rapidly into dense, biofilm-like intracellular bacterial communities (IBCs) protected from urinary antibiotics and host neutrophils. As umbrella cells exfoliate, bacteria escape to invade deeper intermediate urothelial cells, establishing quiescent intracellular reservoirs (QIRs) that persist for months and serve as a reservoir for recrescent, recurrent cystitis.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Innate Urothelial Inflammatory Response: Bacterial binding (via Type 1 or P fimbriae) and LPS engage Toll-like Receptor 4 (TLR4) on urothelial cells, activating NF-kB and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3). Urothelial cells secrete CXCL8 (IL-8), IL-1beta, and IL-6.
  - Neutrophil Recruitment & Pyuria: CXCL8 recruits polymorphonuclear leukocytes (neutrophils) across the urothelium into the bladder lumen, producing pyuria. Neutrophils release reactive oxygen species and proteases, contributing to mucosal hyperemia, edema, superficial urothelial desquamation, and petechial hemorrhages.
  - Tamm-Horsfall Protein (THP / Uromodulin): Secreted by the ascending loop of Henle; contains high mannose residues that bind Type 1 fimbriated E. coli in urine, acting as a soluble competitive decoy to prevent bacterial adhesion to uroplakins and facilitate clearing during micturition.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Classic Lower Urinary Tract Irritative Triad: Dysuria (burning or painful sensation during micturition, localized to the urethra or suprapubic region), Urinary Frequency (frequent micturition of small volumes of urine), and Urinary Urgency (sudden, compelling, painful desire to void).
  - Suprapubic Symptoms: Suprapubic pain, aching, pressure, or heaviness.
  - Urine Characteristics: Turbid, cloudy, or foul-smelling urine; gross hematuria or terminal hematuria (bloody tinge at the end of micturition in ~30% of acute cystitis cases).
  - Absence of Upper Tract / Systemic Signs: Fever is CHARACTERISTICALLY ABSENT in acute uncomplicated cystitis! Presence of fever, chills, rigors, flank pain, or CVA tenderness indicates acute pyelonephritis or acute prostatitis (in males).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Suprapubic Tenderness: Mild to moderate tenderness on palpation over the suprapubic area / urinary bladder.
  - Absence of Costovertebral Angle (CVA) Tenderness: Flank tenderness is ABSENT on percussion (distinguishes cystitis from pyelonephritis).
  - Normal Vital Signs: Patient is afebrile and hemodynamically stable.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Classic Irritative Triad + Suprapubic Tenderness in a Premenopausal Female: Acute onset of dysuria, frequency, urgency, and suprapubic pain in a healthy premenopausal female in the absence of vaginal discharge or fever is >=90% predictive of acute bacterial cystitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Systemic Inflammatory Biomarkers: CBC, CRP, and procalcitonin are NOT routinely drawn and are normal in uncomplicated cystitis.
  - Urinalysis (Dipstick & Microscopy):
    - Leukocyte Esterase Test: Detects neutrophil esterase; highly sensitive (75%–96%) and specific (94%–98%) for pyuria (>10 WBCs/mm3).
    - Nitrite Test: Detects bacterial reduction of dietary nitrate to nitrite by Enterobacteriaceae (E. coli, Klebsiella, Proteus); highly specific (>95%), but false-negatives occur with low-count bacteriuria, frequent voiding, or non-nitrate-reducing pathogens (S. saprophyticus, Enterococcus, Pseudomonas).
    - Urine Microscopy: Pyuria (>=10 WBCs/mm3 in counting chamber, or >=5-10 WBCs/HPF of centrifuged sediment) is present in virtually all true bacterial cystitis cases; absence of pyuria strongly points to an alternative diagnosis. Microscopic or gross hematuria present in 30% of cases. Absence of white cell casts (white cell casts indicate pyelonephritis).
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Routine Imaging NOT Indicated: Ultrasonography, CT, and MRI are NOT indicated for acute uncomplicated cystitis in premenopausal women.
  - Selective Indications for Imaging (CT / Ultrasound / VCUG): Indicated in complicated cystitis, persistent/relapsing infection, men, children with febrile or recurrent UTI, emphysematous cystitis (CT shows gas in bladder wall), suspected bladder calculi, or suspected urologic anomaly.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Urine Culture (Quantitative Colony Count):
    - Uncomplicated Cystitis Rule: Urine culture is NOT routinely required for healthy premenopausal women presenting with classic uncomplicated cystitis symptoms; diagnosis and management proceed based on history and urinalysis alone!
    - Indications for Urine Culture: Suspected complicated cystitis; pregnancy; males; children; postmenopausal women; recurrent cystitis; symptoms persisting or relapsing <2-4 weeks after therapy; or recent antimicrobial resistance/hospitalization.
    - Quantitative Culture Thresholds for Cystitis:
      - Midstream Clean-Catch Voided Urine: >=10^3 CFU/mL of a single uropathogen carries 90% sensitivity and 90% specificity for acute cystitis in symptomatic women. (Note: Up to one-third of young women with acute lower tract cystitis have low-count bacteriuria between 10^2 and 10^4 CFU/mL).
      - Catheterized Urine: >=10^2 CFU/mL of a single uropathogen.
      - Suprapubic Aspiration: Any detectable bacterial growth (>=10^2 CFU/mL).
  - Direct Microscopy & Stains: Gram stain of uncentrifuged urine (>=1 bacterium per oil immersion field correlates with >=10^5 CFU/mL).
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Quantitative Midstream Urine Culture demonstrating >=10^3 CFU/mL of a single uropathogen in a symptomatic female with lower urinary tract irritative symptoms and pyuria represents the gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - General Management: Hydration and voiding. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs, e.g., ibuprofen) alone are INFERIOR to antibiotics for symptom resolution and carry a higher risk of subsequent pyelonephritis, so NSAID monotherapy is NOT routinely recommended. Phenazopyridine hydrochloride (Pyridium 100-200 mg PO tid x 2 days) may provide short-term urinary analgesic relief for severe dysuria, but does not treat the underlying infection (warn patients about orange/red discoloration of urine and fluids).
  - Behavioral & Non-Antimicrobial Prevention of Recurrent Cystitis: Avoidance of spermicides and diaphragms; postcoital voiding; topical vaginal estrogen replacement in postmenopausal women (restores Lactobacillus flora and reduces recurrences); daily cranberry supplements / D-mannose (mixed evidence); and increased fluid intake.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - First-Line First-Choice Regimens for Acute Uncomplicated Cystitis in Nonpregnant Women (IDSA / ESMID Guidelines):
    1. Nitrofurantoin Monohydrate / Macrocrystals: 100 mg PO twice daily for 5 days. (High urinary efficacy, minimal resistance <5%, minimal collateral damage to intestinal/vaginal flora; contraindicated if creatinine clearance <30-60 mL/min).
    2. Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX): 160/800 mg (one double-strength tablet) PO twice daily for 3 days. (Recommended ONLY if local E. coli resistance rates are known to be <20%).
    3. Fosfomycin Trometamol: 3 g PO as a single dose mixed in water. (Convenient single dose, active against ESBL-producing E. coli; slightly inferior clinical cure compared to 7-day nitrofurantoin).
    4. Pivmecillinam: 400 mg PO twice daily for 3 to 7 days. (First-line in Europe; not available in the US).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Second-Line / Alternative Regimens for Acute Uncomplicated Cystitis:
    1. Oral Beta-Lactams: Amoxicillin-Clavulanate 500/125 mg PO bid x 3 to 7 days OR Cefdinir 300 mg PO bid x 3-7 days OR Cefpodoxime proxetil 100 mg PO bid x 3-7 days OR Cephalexin 500 mg PO qid x 3-7 days. (Beta-lactams have inferior efficacy and higher adverse event rates compared to nitrofurantoin/TMP-SMX; amoxicillin/ampicillin alone are NOT recommended due to high global resistance >30%-50%).
    2. Fluoroquinolones (Ciprofloxacin 250-500 mg PO bid x 3 days OR Levofloxacin 250-500 mg PO daily x 3 days): Fluoroquinolones are highly effective (3-day course) BUT SHOULD BE RESERVED as last-resort agents due to FDA warnings regarding serious adverse effects (tendonitis, QT prolongation, neuropathy) and ecological "collateral damage" (selecting for ESBLs and C. difficile).
  - Regimens for Complicated Cystitis (including Cystitis in Men):
    - Oral Fluoroquinolones (Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO bid OR Levofloxacin 750 mg PO daily) OR Nitrofurantoin 100 mg PO bid OR Fosfomycin 3 g PO every 48 hours for 3 doses. Duration of therapy is 7 to 14 days in complicated cystitis and in men (to cover potential occult prostatic involvement).
  - Regimens for Acute Cystitis in Pregnancy:
    - Nitrofurantoin 100 mg PO bid x 5-7 days (avoid in 1st trimester per ACOG, and at term 38-42 weeks) OR Fosfomycin 3 g PO single dose OR Cephalexin 500 mg PO qid x 3-7 days OR Amoxicillin-Clavulanate 500/125 mg PO bid x 3-7 days.
  - Antimicrobial Prophylaxis for Recurrent Cystitis:
    - Postcoital Single-Dose Prophylaxis (taken within 2 hours of intercourse): Nitrofurantoin 50 to 100 mg PO OR TMP-SMX 40/200 mg (half single-strength) PO.
    - Continuous Low-Dose Daily Prophylaxis (for 6 to 12 months): Nitrofurantoin 50 to 100 mg PO at bedtime OR TMP-SMX 40/200 mg PO at bedtime.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Removal / Exchange of Indwelling Urinary Catheters: Primary procedural management for catheter-associated cystitis (clears infection/biofilm).
  - Surgical Correction of Anatomic Abnormalities: Transurethral resection of prostate (TURP) for prostatic obstruction in men; surgical repair of vesicovaginal or colovesical fistulas; cystoscopic removal of bladder calculi/foreign bodies; or percutaneous drainage/debridement for emphysematous cystitis.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Ascending Pyelonephritis: Up to 1% to 3% of acute uncomplicated cystitis episodes progress to ascending pyelonephritis if untreated or treated with ineffective regimens (e.g., NSAID monotherapy). Higher progression rates in pregnancy (up to 20%-40%).
  - Acute Urinary Retention (secondary to severe urethral/bladder inflammatory edema or prostatic involvement in men).
  - Bacteremia and Urosepsis (uncommon in uncomplicated cystitis, but higher risk in complicated, catheterized, or obstructive cystitis).
  - Bladder Wall Necrosis & Perforation (rare complication of severe emphysematous or encrusted cystitis).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Recurrent Cystitis: Experienced by 20% to 30% of women following an initial episode, causing significant morbidity, healthcare costs, and quality-of-life impairment.
  - Emergence of Multidrug-Resistant Uropathogens: Frequent antibiotic courses select for ESBL-producing E. coli ST131, carbapenemase-producing Enterobacteriaceae, and fluoroquinolone resistance.
  - Chronic Bladder Dysfunction or Secondary Interstitial Bladder Pain Syndrome.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Honeymoon Cystitis: Acute cystitis in young premenopausal women triggered by recent frequent sexual intercourse.
  - Irritative Lower Tract Triad: Dysuria + Frequency + Urgency without fever.
  - Quiescent Intracellular Reservoirs (QIRs): Intracellular UPEC pools inside intermediate urothelial cells causing recurrent relapsing cystitis.
  - Nonoxynol-9 Depletion: Spermicides killing protective vaginal lactobacilli and enhancing E. coli uroepithelial adherence.
  - Volcano-like Ulcers / Encrusted Plaques: Calcified struvite mucosal plaques in Corynebacterium urealyticum encrusted cystitis.
  - Type 1 Fimbriae / FimH: Mannose-sensitive bacterial adhesin binding uroplakins Ia/Ib, mandatory for establishing cystitis.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - No Urine Culture Needed for Uncomplicated Cystitis: Healthy premenopausal women with classic uncomplicated cystitis symptoms do NOT require urine culture or dipstick testing before initiating empiric treatment!
  - Absence of Fever Rule: Fever is characteristically ABSENT in acute uncomplicated cystitis; presence of fever indicates pyelonephritis, prostatitis, or tissue-invasive infection!
  - Low-Count Bacteriuria in Cystitis: Up to one-third of young women with true acute bacterial cystitis have low urine bacterial counts (10^2 to 10^4 CFU/mL), below the historical 10^5 CFU/mL threshold!
  - Fluoroquinolone Reservation: Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) should NOT be used as first-line empiric therapy for uncomplicated cystitis due to safety warnings and ecological collateral damage!
  - NSAID Monotherapy Inferiority: Treating acute cystitis with NSAIDs (ibuprofen) alone without antibiotics results in prolonged symptoms and significantly higher rates of ascending pyelonephritis!
  - Beta-Lactam Inferiority: Oral beta-lactams (cephalosporins, amoxicillin-clavulanate) have inferior clinical and microbiologic cure rates for cystitis compared to 5-day nitrofurantoin or 3-day TMP-SMX!
