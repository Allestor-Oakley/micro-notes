# Urinary tract infection

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** A urinary tract infection (UTI) is an inflammatory response of the urothelium to pathogenic microbial invasion, typically accompanied by pyuria and quantitative bacteriuria. It encompasses a broad spectrum of clinical entities ranging from superficial mucosal infection of the lower urinary tract (cystitis) to tissue-invasive infection of the upper urinary tract (pyelonephritis) or male accessory glands (prostatitis, epididymitis).
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Acute Uncomplicated UTI / Cystitis: Lower urinary tract infection occurring in a healthy, nonpregnant, premenopausal, adult female with a structurally, functionally, and neurologically normal urinary tract.
  - Complicated UTI: Infection occurring in the presence of structural, functional, or metabolic host abnormalities that increase the risk of treatment failure, persistent/relapsing infection, or acquisition of resistant pathogens. This includes all UTIs in males, pregnant women, children, healthcare-associated/catheterized patients, and patients with neurogenic bladder, urinary tract obstruction, renal failure, renal transplantation, diabetes mellitus, or immunosuppression.
  - Acute Pyelonephritis: Invasive infection of the renal parenchyma, pelvis, and calyces, presenting with systemic toxicity, fever, chills, and flank pain/costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness.
  - Asymptomatic Bacteriuria (ABU): Quantitative growth of a uropathogen (typically >=10^5 CFU/mL in two consecutive clean-catch voided urine specimens in females, or one specimen in males/catheterized patients) in an individual completely lacking clinical symptoms or signs attributable to a UTI.
  - Recurrent UTI: Defined as 2 or more symptomatic episodes within 6 months, or 3 or more episodes within 12 months. Subclassified into: (1) Reinfection (recurrence with a different strain or species >2 weeks post-therapy, accounting for >80% of recurrences); and (2) Relapse (recurrence with the same original strain within 2 weeks of completing therapy due to bacterial persistence in quiescent intracellular reservoirs, renal tissue, calculi, or the prostate).
  - Catheter-Associated UTI (CAUTI): Infection occurring in a patient with an indwelling urinary catheter in place for >48 hours or within 48 hours of catheter removal, presenting with systemic or localized signs in the presence of >=10^3 CFU/mL of a uropathogen.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Demographic & Gender Incidence: Extremely common in women; up to 60% of adult women experience at least one symptomatic UTI during their lifetime, and 10% to 20% experience an episode annually. In young adults aged 18 to 50 years, UTIs are 30 times more frequent in females than males due to shorter urethral length (~4 cm vs. ~20 cm), anatomical proximity of the female urethra to the warm, moist vulvoperineal and anal areas, and absence of prostatic antibacterial secretions. In older adults >65 years, the female-to-male ratio decreases to ~2:1 due to rising incidence of prostatic hyperplasia and urinary retention in men.
  - Sexual Activity & Behavioral Factors: Recent sexual intercourse (frequency of coitus is the single strongest independent risk factor for acute cystitis in young women); new sex partner in the past year; spermicidal contraceptive use (nonoxynol-9 alters normal vaginal flora by killing protective hydrogen peroxide-producing lactobacilli and promoting uropathogenic E. coli colonization); diaphragm use; condom use with spermicide; lack of postcoital voiding; and recent antimicrobial exposure (disrupts normal indigenous vaginal and distal urethral flora).
  - Estrogen Status: Estrogen deficiency in postmenopausal women causes loss of glycogen in vaginal epithelial cells, loss of protective Lactobacillus species, elevation of vaginal pH (>4.5), and dense colonization by coliform uropathogens.
  - Anatomic, Structural & Functional Abnormalities: Urinary tract obstruction (nephrolithiasis, ureteral strictures, benign prostatic hyperplasia, posterior urethral valves, pelvic tumors); vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) in children; neurogenic bladder dysfunction (spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, spina bifida); urinary incontinence and pelvic organ prolapse; indwelling urinary catheters and ureteral stents; and urinary diversions/conduits.
  - Host Genetics & Comorbidities: Familial predisposition (history of recurrent UTIs in first-degree female relatives); Nonsecretor status of blood group antigens (ABH and Lewis nonsecretors express unique glycosphingolipid receptors on urothelial cells that bind PapG and Dr adhesins of UPEC); single nucleotide polymorphisms in TLR4 or CXCR1/CXCR2 IL-8 receptors; diabetes mellitus (increases risk 2-fold to 4-fold due to autonomic neuropathy, incomplete bladder emptying, glycosuria, and impaired neutrophil migration/phagocytosis); pregnancy; sickle cell trait/disease; and renal transplantation.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Escherichia coli (Uropathogenic E. coli / UPEC): The predominant causative organism across all clinical categories, accounting for 75% to 90% of acute uncomplicated cystitis and pyelonephritis cases in outpatients, and 40% to 50% of complicated or healthcare-associated UTIs.
  - Klebsiella pneumoniae: Second most common gram-negative bacillus, causing 4% to 8% of uncomplicated cases and up to 15% of complicated/nosocomial UTIs.
  - Proteus mirabilis: Causes 3% to 5% of uncomplicated UTIs and higher rates in elderly or catheterized patients (produces urease, predisposing to struvite staghorn calculi).
  - Staphylococcus saprophyticus: Coagulase-negative, novobiocin-resistant staphylococcus causing 5% to 15% of acute uncomplicated cystitis in young, sexually active females (showing seasonal peak in late summer/autumn).
  - Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium: Cause 1% to 5% of uncomplicated UTIs and up to 15% to 20% of complicated, catheterized, or post-procedural UTIs.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Infants & Children (<5 years): E. coli (75%-80%), Enterococcus faecalis (more common in infants <3 months), Proteus mirabilis (more common in uncircumcised young boys), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter species, and Adenovirus type 11 (causes acute hemorrhagic cystitis in young boys).
  - Young Sexually Active Premenopausal Females (15-45 years): Uropathogenic E. coli (80%-90%), Staphylococcus saprophyticus (5%-15%), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus / GBS).
  - Postmenopausal & Elderly Females: E. coli (frequency declines to 50%-60%), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, Enterobacter species, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococcus faecalis, Streptococcus agalactiae.
  - Adult Males & Complicated / Institutionalized Hosts: E. coli (40%-50%), Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter cloacae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Enterococcus faecalis, Enterococcus faecium, Morganella morganii, Providencia stuartii, Serratia marcescens, Staphylococcus epidermidis / coagulase-negative staphylococci, and multidrug-resistant (MDR) Enterobacteriaceae (extended-spectrum beta-lactamase / ESBL producers, carbapenemase producers).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Fastidious Bacteria & Higher Organisms: Corynebacterium urealyticum (slow-growing, urea-splitting, gram-positive rod causing encrusted cystitis/pyelitis and struvite stones in renal transplant or catheterized hosts); Actinobaculum schaalii (facultative anaerobic gram-positive rod causing urosepsis/cystitis in elderly >65 years, missed on standard cultures); Aerococcus urinae; Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and Mycoplasma genitalium (causes of acute urethral syndrome / sterile pyuria mimicking cystitis); Gardnerella vaginalis, Ureaplasma urealyticum, and Mycoplasma hominis.
  - Fungi: Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, Candida krusei, Candida tropicalis (fungal candiduria/cystitis in catheterized, diabetic, ICU, or broad-spectrum antibiotic-treated patients).
  - Viruses: Adenovirus (types 11 and 35; causing hemorrhagic cystitis in children, HSCT/BMT recipients, and renal transplants); BK Polyomavirus and JC Polyomavirus (causing hemorrhagic cystitis and nephropathy in allogeneic HSCT and renal transplant recipients); Cytomegalovirus (CMV); Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1/2).
  - Parasites: Schistosoma haematobium (causing endemic urogenital schistosomiasis, terminal hematuria, bladder wall calcifications, and squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Ascending Route (Primary Mechanism >95% of Cases): Bacteria originating from the gut bowel reservoir colonize the vaginal introitus, periurethral region, and distal urethra. Bacteria ascend through the urethra into the bladder. In susceptible hosts or in the presence of specific adhesins, bacteria breach bladder defenses and ascend retrogradely against urine flow up the ureters into the renal pelvis and parenchymal collecting tubules. Ureteral reflux (vesicoureteral reflux / VUR) and impaired ureteral peristalsis (mediated by bacterial endotoxin / LPS) facilitate renal invasion.
  - Uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) Virulence Determinants:
    - Type 1 Fimbriae / Pili (fim gene cluster, fimH adhesin): Universal in UPEC; mannose-sensitive binding to uroplakins Ia and Ib on superficial umbrella cells of the bladder. Essential for establishing lower tract cystitis, promoting urothelial cell invasion, and triggering intracellular bacterial community (IBC) formation.
    - P Fimbriae / Pyelonephritis-Associated Pili (pap gene cluster, papG adhesin): Essential for upper tract pyelonephritis. PapG tip adhesin specifically binds alpha-D-galactopyranosyl-(1->4)-beta-D-galactopyranoside (Gal-Gal) receptor moieties on glycolipids of human renal collecting duct and tubule epithelial cells.
    - Dr Family Adhesins (dra gene cluster): Bind decay-accelerating factor (DAF) and type IV collagen, mediating interstitial cell invasion and persistent infection.
    - Toxins & Iron Acquisition Systems: Alpha-hemolysin (hlyA; pore-forming toxin that lyses urothelial and renal tubular cells, damages host cell membranes, and releases iron); Cytotoxic Necrotizing Factor 1 (cnf1); Secreted Autotransporter Toxin (sat); Aerobactin, Enterobactin, Yersiniabactin, and Salmochelin siderophore systems for scavenging iron in iron-deficient urine.
  - Intracellular Bacterial Communities (IBCs) & Quiescent Intracellular Reservoirs (QIRs): Following Type 1 fimbriae-mediated entry into umbrella cells, UPEC replicate rapidly into dense, biofilm-like intracellular bacterial communities (IBCs) protected from urine antibiotics and host neutrophils. As umbrella cells exfoliate, bacteria escape to invade deeper intermediate urothelial cells, forming quiescent intracellular reservoirs (QIRs) that persist for months and serve as a reservoir for recrescent, recurrent UTIs.
  - Hematogenous Route (<5% of Cases): Direct blood-borne seeding of the renal parenchyma during systemic bacteremia or fungemia (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia, Candida albicans fungemia, Salmonella bacteremia, or Mycobacterium tuberculosis).
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Urothelial Innate Inflammatory Response: Bacterial adhesins and LPS engage Toll-like Receptor 4 (TLR4) and TLR2 on urothelial cells, activating NF-kB and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3). Urothelial cells secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines CXCL8 (IL-8), IL-1beta, and IL-6.
  - Neutrophilic Infiltration & Pyuria: CXCL8 recruits polymorphonuclear leukocytes (neutrophils) across the urothelium into the urinary lumen, producing pyuria. Neutrophil degranulation releases reactive oxygen species and proteases, causing mucosal edema, hyperemia, desquamation, petechial hemorrhages (in cystitis), or suppurative interstitial microabscesses and tubular destruction (in pyelonephritis).
  - Protective Soluble Factors: Tamm-Horsfall Protein (THP / Uromodulin; secreted by ascending loop of Henle, contains high-mannose structures that bind Type 1 fimbriated E. coli in urine, acting as a competitive decoy to prevent uroplakin binding); urinary secretory IgA; mucosal defensins; and high urinary osmolality, urea, and low pH.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Acute Uncomplicated Cystitis: Irritative lower urinary tract triad of Dysuria (burning or pain on micturition), Urinary Frequency (frequent voiding of small volumes), and Urinary Urgency (sudden, compelling desire to void); accompanied by suprapubic pain, aching, or heaviness, cloudy or foul-smelling urine, and gross or microscopic hematuria. Systemic symptoms and fever are CHARACTERISTICALLY ABSENT in uncomplicated cystitis.
  - Acute Pyelonephritis: Systemic upper tract symptom complex of high fever (>=38.5°C / 101°F), shaking chills / rigors, flank pain or back pain (typically unilateral, over the costovertebral angle), nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and malaise; concurrent or antecedent dysuria, frequency, and urgency are present in 60% to 70% of cases.
  - Acute Bacterial Prostatitis: Fever, chills, perineal, pelvic, or sacral pain, dysuria, urinary frequency, urgency, and variable degree of urinary hesitation or acute urinary retention.
  - Atypical Symptoms in Special Populations: Elderly patients may lack fever or classic urinary symptoms, presenting instead with acute delirium / altered mental status, lethargy, incontinence, or septic shock. Infants present with fever, poor feeding, vomiting, irritability, and failure to thrive. Catheterized patients present with fever, rigors, costovertebral tenderness, or acute mental status changes.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Acute Cystitis: Mild to moderate suprapubic tenderness on palpation over the urinary bladder; costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness is ABSENT; patient is afebrile and hemodynamically stable.
  - Acute Pyelonephritis: Costovertebral Angle (CVA) Tenderness (exquisite flank pain elicited by gentle fist percussion over the costovertebral angle on the affected side); fever (38.5°C to 40°C); tachycardia; abdominal flank or upper quadrant tenderness; patient may exhibit hypotension / septic shock.
  - Acute Prostatitis: Extremely tender, warm, swollen, boggy prostate on digital rectal examination (DRE MUST BE PERFORMED GENTLY; vigorous prostatic massage is STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED due to risk of inducing bacteremia and septic shock).
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - White Blood Cell (WBC) Casts in Urine Sediment: Detection of white blood cell casts on urine microscopy is pathognomonic for upper tract renal parenchymal inflammation / acute pyelonephritis.
  - Irritative Lower Tract Triad in Premenopausal Female: Acute dysuria, frequency, urgency, and suprapubic pain in a premenopausal female in the absence of vaginal discharge or fever is >=90% predictive of acute bacterial cystitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis (12,000 to 20,000/mm3 or higher) with a left shift (bandemia >10%) in acute pyelonephritis, acute prostatitis, or urosepsis. Normal WBC count in uncomplicated cystitis.
  - Systemic Inflammatory Biomarkers: Normal in uncomplicated cystitis. Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT >0.5 to 2.0 ng/mL) in acute pyelonephritis and urosepsis.
  - Urinalysis (Dipstick & Microscopy):
    - Leukocyte Esterase Test: Highly sensitive (75%-96%) and specific (94%-98%) for pyuria (>10 WBCs/mm3).
    - Nitrite Test: Highly specific (>95%) for Enterobacteriaceae (E. coli, Klebsiella, Proteus) that reduce nitrate to nitrite; false-negatives occur with non-nitrate reducers (S. saprophyticus, Enterococcus, Pseudomonas), low bacterial counts, or frequent voiding.
    - Urine Microscopy: Pyuria (>=10 WBCs/mm3 in counting chamber, or >=5-10 WBCs/HPF of centrifuged sediment) is present in virtually all true bacterial UTIs. Microscopic hematuria present in 30% of cystitis cases. White blood cell casts indicate pyelonephritis.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Routine Imaging NOT Indicated: Ultrasonography, CT, and MRI are NOT indicated for acute uncomplicated cystitis or for prompt-responding uncomplicated pyelonephritis in young women.
  - Indications for Urgent Renal Imaging (CT / Ultrasound): Mandatory for patients with pyelonephritis remaining febrile after 48 to 72 hours of appropriate antibiotics, severely ill patients with septic shock, known or suspected urinary tract obstruction or urolithiasis, diabetics (to rule out emphysematous pyelonephritis), immunocompromised hosts, history of renal transplantation, or recurrent pyelonephritis.
  - Contrast-Enhanced CT of Abdomen/Pelvis (Diagnostic Imaging Modality of Choice): Demonstrates striated or wedge-shaped parenchymal perfusion defects, renal enlargement, perinephric fat stranding, renal/perinephric abscesses, emphysematous gas, or obstructive hydronephrosis.
  - Renal Ultrasonography: Safe alternative in pregnancy or renal failure to evaluate for hydronephrosis, urinary obstruction, or abscesses.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Quantitative Urine Culture (Core Microbiologic Standard):
    - Culture NOT required for uncomplicated cystitis in healthy premenopausal women; managed empirically.
    - Mandatory Culture Indications: Suspected pyelonephritis, complicated UTI, pregnancy, males, children, postmenopausal women, recurrent UTIs, treatment failure/relapse, CAUTI, or recent antibiotic exposure.
    - Quantitative Thresholds:
      - Midstream Clean-Catch Voided Urine: >=10^3 CFU/mL of a single uropathogen carries 90% sensitivity for acute cystitis in symptomatic women; >=10^4 to 10^5 CFU/mL defines pyelonephritis; >=10^5 CFU/mL in two consecutive specimens defines ABU in asymptomatic women.
      - Catheterized Urine: >=10^2 CFU/mL of a single uropathogen defines UTI/CAUTI.
      - Suprapubic Aspiration: Any detectable bacterial growth (>=10^2 CFU/mL).
  - Blood Cultures: Mandatory in all hospitalized pyelonephritis, complicated UTI, acute prostatitis, or urosepsis cases prior to antibiotics (positive in 15% to 30% of pyelonephritis cases).
  - Gram Stain of Uncentrifuged Urine: Rapidly identifies bacterial morphology (gram-negative rods vs. gram-positive cocci) to guide initial empiric selection.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Quantitative Urine Culture demonstrating significant growth of a uropathogen (>=10^3 CFU/mL for cystitis, >=10^4-10^5 CFU/mL for pyelonephritis) in the presence of matching clinical symptoms and pyuria represents the gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - General Management: Oral or IV fluid resuscitation to maintain urinary flow and renal perfusion; antipyretics (acetaminophen); antiemetics. Phenazopyridine (100-200 mg PO tid x 2 days) provides short-term urinary analgesic relief for severe dysuria (discolors urine orange/red).
  - Categorization for Outpatient vs. Inpatient Care: Mild-to-moderate uncomplicated cystitis and stable pyelonephritis are managed outpatient with oral therapy. Inpatient admission required for severe toxicity, high fever >39°C, septic shock, intractable nausea/vomiting, acute kidney injury, pregnancy, severe diabetes, immunocompromise, or failure of outpatient oral therapy after 48 to 72 hours.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - First-Line First-Choice Regimens for Acute Uncomplicated Cystitis in Nonpregnant Women:
    1. Nitrofurantoin Monohydrate / Macrocrystals: 100 mg PO twice daily for 5 days. (Minimal resistance <5%, minimal collateral damage to intestinal flora; contraindicated if CrCl <30-60 mL/min).
    2. Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX): 160/800 mg (one double-strength tablet) PO twice daily for 3 days. (Recommended ONLY if local E. coli resistance is <20%).
    3. Fosfomycin Trometamol: 3 g PO as a single dose mixed in water.
    4. Pivmecillinam: 400 mg PO twice daily for 3 to 7 days.
  - First-Line Regimens for Acute Uncomplicated Pyelonephritis (Outpatient Oral):
    1. Oral Fluoroquinolones (if local resistance <10%): Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO bid for 7 days OR Ciprofloxacin ER 1000 mg PO daily for 7 days OR Levofloxacin 750 mg PO daily for 5 days.
    2. Initial IV Consolidation Dose (if local fluoroquinolone resistance >10% or unknown): Single dose of Ceftriaxone 1 g IV OR Gentamicin 5 to 7 mg/kg IV BEFORE starting oral fluoroquinolone, TMP-SMX, or oral beta-lactam.
    3. Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX): 160/800 mg PO bid for 14 days (ONLY if organism is known to be susceptible).
  - Inpatient Parenteral Regimens for Severe / Complicated Pyelonephritis / CAUTI:
    1. Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h OR Cefotaxime 2 g IV q8h.
    2. Antipseudomonal Beta-Lactams (for complicated, catheterized, or healthcare-associated UTIs): Cefepime 2 g IV q8-12h OR Piperacillin-Tazobactam 3.375 to 4.5 g IV q6h OR Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h.
    3. Carbapenems (for high risk of ESBL producers or severe uroseptic shock): Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Imipenem-Cilastatin 500 mg IV q6h OR Ertapenem 1 g IV q24h.
    4. Aminoglycosides: Gentamicin or Tobramycin 5 to 7 mg/kg IV q24h.
    5. Plazomicin: 15 mg/kg IV q24h x 7 to 14 days (indicated for complicated UTIs including pyelonephritis caused by MDR Enterobacteriaceae).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Alternative Oral Regimens for Cystitis: Amoxicillin-Clavulanate 500/125 mg PO bid x 3-7 days OR Cefdinir 300 mg PO bid x 3-7 days OR Cefpodoxime proxetil 100 mg PO bid x 3-7 days OR Cephalexin 500 mg PO qid x 3-7 days. (Amoxicillin or ampicillin alone are NOT recommended due to high global resistance >30%-50%).
  - Regimens for UTI in Pregnancy:
    - Cystitis / ABU: Nitrofurantoin 100 mg PO bid x 5-7 days (avoid in 1st trimester per ACOG, and at term 38-42 weeks) OR Fosfomycin 3 g PO single dose OR Cephalexin 500 mg PO qid x 3-7 days OR Amoxicillin-Clavulanate 500/125 mg PO bid x 3-7 days.
    - Pyelonephritis in Pregnancy: Inpatient admission mandatory. Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h OR Ampicillin 2 g IV q6h PLUS Gentamicin 1.5 mg/kg IV q8h. Step-down to oral cephalosporins to complete 14 days, followed by daily suppressive nitrofurantoin (50-100 mg PO at bedtime) through delivery.
  - Regimens for Acute Bacterial Prostatitis: Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO bid OR Levofloxacin 500 mg PO daily OR TMP-SMX 160/800 mg PO bid for 4 to 6 weeks (long duration required to penetrate prostatic tissue).
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Emergency Decompression of Obstructed Kidneys: Immediate percutaneous nephrostomy tube placement or retrograde ureteral stent insertion for infected hydronephrosis / pyonephrosis secondary to obstructing ureteral calculi (medical therapy alone fails without drainage).
  - Removal / Exchange of Indwelling Urinary Catheters: Primary management for CAUTI and catheter-associated ABU (clears biofilm).
  - Drainage of Abscesses & Nephrectomy: Image-guided percutaneous catheter drainage for renal abscesses >=3 cm or perinephric abscesses; emergency nephrectomy for severe Emphysematous Pyelonephritis (EPN class 3/4) or advanced Xanthogranulomatous Pyelonephritis (XGP).

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Urosepsis and Septic Shock (Gram-negative bacteremic shock carrying 20% to 40% mortality).
  - Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) and Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN).
  - Suppurative & Necrotizing Complications: Intrarenal abscess, perinephric abscess, pyonephrosis, renal papillary necrosis, emphysematous pyelonephritis, emphysematous cystitis.
  - Acute Urinary Retention (secondary to prostatic swelling in acute prostatitis or severe urethral edema).
  - Obstetric Complications: Maternal sepsis, adult respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), preterm labor, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), low birth weight.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Chronic Renal Parenchymal Scarring (focal cortical scarring over blunted calyces in up to 10%-20% of children/adults post-pyelonephritis).
  - Secondary Hypertension (renovascular/parenchymal hypertension secondary to reflux nephropathy / scarring).
  - Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) and End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD).
  - Chronic Bacterial Prostatitis and Prostatic Abscess.
  - Recurrent UTIs and Emergence of Multidrug-Resistant Uropathogens (ESBLs, CRE).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Honeymoon Cystitis: Acute lower tract UTI in young women following frequent sexual coitus.
  - WBC Casts: Diagnostic urinary microscopy finding pathognomonic for acute renal parenchymal pyelonephritis.
  - CVA Tenderness: Exquisite flank pain elicited by gentle fist percussion over the costovertebral angle.
  - PapG / P Fimbriae: Gal-Gal binding tip adhesin of UPEC, essential for upper tract tissue invasion.
  - FimH / Type 1 Fimbriae: Mannose-sensitive tip adhesin binding uroplakins Ia/Ib, mandatory for lower tract cystitis.
  - Quiescent Intracellular Reservoirs (QIRs): Intracellular UPEC pools inside intermediate urothelial cells causing recurrent relapsing UTIs.
  - Bear Paw Sign: Pathognomonic CT appearance of expanded calyces surrounding a central staghorn calculus in Xanthogranulomatous Pyelonephritis.
  - Striated Nephrogram: Characteristic CT finding of alternating hyperdense and hypodense parenchymal bands in acute pyelonephritis.
  - Do Not Screen, Do Not Treat: Fundamental stewardship rule for ABU in nonpregnant adults, elderly, diabetics, and catheterized patients.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Nitrofurantoin & Fosfomycin Pyelonephritis Contraindication: Nitrofurantoin, Fosfomycin, and Pivmecillinam achieve high urinary concentrations but POOR RENAL PARENCHYMAL TISSUE CONCENTRATIONS and are STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED for pyelonephritis.
  - No Culture Needed for Uncomplicated Cystitis: Healthy premenopausal women with classic uncomplicated cystitis do NOT require urine culture or dipstick testing before starting empiric treatment.
  - Mandatory Culture Rule for Pyelonephritis & Complicated UTI: Quantitative urine culture MUST be obtained in 100% of pyelonephritis, complicated UTI, male UTI, and pregnant UTI cases.
  - Fluoroquinolone Reservation for Cystitis: Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) should NOT be used as first-line empiric therapy for uncomplicated cystitis due to safety warnings and collateral damage, but REMAIN first-line oral agents for uncomplicated pyelonephritis.
  - Pyuria Does NOT Equal Treatment Indication in ABU: Pyuria is present in >90% of elderly, diabetic, catheterized, and pregnant patients with ABU, but pyuria ALONE in an asymptomatic patient does NOT justify antibiotic therapy (except in pregnancy or pre-urologic procedures).
  - Pregnancy ABU Exception: Asymptomatic bacteriuria MUST be screened for and treated in pregnant women (12 to 16 weeks gestation) because untreated ABU leads to acute pyelonephritis in 20% to 40% of cases and adverse fetal outcomes.
  - Prostatic Massage Contraindication: Vigorous prostatic massage is STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED in suspected acute bacterial prostatitis due to the high risk of inducing bacteremia and uroseptic shock.
  - Staphylococcal Bacteremic Seeding: Isolation of Staphylococcus aureus in urine culture indicates hematogenous bacteremic seeding to the kidney (renal abscess) rather than an ascending UTI, requiring immediate blood cultures and echocardiogram.
  - Echinocandin Urinary Ineffectiveness: Echinocandins (caspofungin, micafungin) do not achieve active concentrations in urine and are NOT recommended for lower or upper urinary tract fungal infections (oral fluconazole is the drug of choice for Candida UTIs).
