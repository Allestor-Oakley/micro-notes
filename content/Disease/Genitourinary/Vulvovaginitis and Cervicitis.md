# Vulvovaginitis and Cervicitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Vulvovaginitis and cervicitis encompass a spectrum of infectious and noninfectious inflammatory conditions involving the vulva, vagina, and cervix. Vulvovaginitis is characterized by inflammation of the vulvovaginal mucosa and skin resulting in abnormal vaginal discharge, odor, itching, or irritation. Cervicitis is inflammation affecting the columnar epithelial cells of the endocervical glands (endocervicitis) or squamous epithelium of the ectocervix (ectocervicitis), characterized by mucopurulent endocervical exudate or sustained, easily induced endocervical bleeding (friability).
- **Major Classifications:** Major clinical classifications include: 1. Infectious Vaginitis/Vaginosis: Bacterial vaginosis (BV, a complex dysbiosis replacing lactobacilli with anaerobic bacteria), Vulvovaginal Candidiasis (VVC, classified as uncomplicated vs. complicated/recurrent/non-albicans), and Trichomoniasis (a classic sexually transmitted protozoal infection). 2. Noninfectious/Idiopathic Vaginitis: Desquamative Inflammatory Vaginitis (DIV / aerobic vaginitis, characterized by diffuse mucosal inflammation, purulent discharge, and loss of mature squamous cells), Estrogen-Deficiency / Atrophic Vaginitis (Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause), and Chemical or Irritant/Allergic Vulvovaginitis. 3. Cervicitis Classifications: Infectious Endocervicitis (sexually transmitted, primarily Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, or Mycoplasma genitalium) and Noninfectious Ectocervicitis (inflammation in an ectropion secondary to mechanical or chemical irritation). 4. Chronic Vulvar Pain Syndromes: Vulvar pain syndrome / Vulvodynia / Vestibulodynia (localized or generalized persistent vulvar pain, nonsexually transmitted).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:** Epidemiology varies by specific etiology: BV has a prevalence of 29% among US women aged 14 to 49 years and up to 50% in African-American women; risk factors for BV include new or multiple sexual partners, female-to-female sexual contact (2.5-fold increased likelihood in lesbians with high partner concordance), douching, smoking, and lack of condom use (seldom occurs in sexually inexperienced women). VVC affects approximately 75% of adult women at least once in their lifetime; risk factors include recent broad-spectrum antimicrobial use (suppressing protective hydrogen peroxide-producing lactobacilli), elevated estrogen levels (oral contraceptive use, pregnancy), poorly controlled diabetes mellitus with hyperglycemia, tight insulating clothing, systemic corticosteroids, immunosuppression, and HIV infection (especially with low CD4 counts, predisposing to non-albicans species). Trichomoniasis prevalence is 26% in symptomatic women, 6.5% in asymptomatic women, and up to 29% to 50% in HIV-infected women; transmitted strictly via sexual contact. Infectious cervicitis risk factors include age younger than 25 years, new or multiple sexual partners, concurrent partners, partner with an STI, and inconsistent condom usage. DIV, atrophic vaginitis, and vulvodynia are nonsexually transmitted; vulvodynia risk factors include excessive tissue inflammation, combined hormonal contraceptive use, psychological factors (depression, anxiety, PTSD), and pelvic floor muscle dysfunction.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):** The primary causative pathogens and microbial complexes include: 1. Bacterial Vaginosis: Polymicrobial dysbiosis dominated by Gardnerella vaginalis, Mobiluncus curtisii, Mobiluncus mulieris, Atopobium vaginae, Mycoplasma hominis, Prevotella, Bacteroides, and other anaerobic coccobacilli, replacing hydrogen peroxide-producing lactobacilli (Lactobacillus crispatus). 2. Vulvovaginal Candidiasis: Candida albicans (accounts for 80% to 90% of cases), Candida glabrata (formerly Torulopsis glabrata, ~10%), Candida tropicalis (1% to 5%), Candida krusei, and occasionally Saccharomyces cerevisiae. 3. Trichomoniasis: Trichomonas vaginalis (flagellated protozoan parasite). 4. Infectious Cervicitis: Chlamydia trachomatis (obligate intracellular bacterium, serovars D-K), Neisseria gonorrhoeae (gram-negative diplococcus), and Mycoplasma genitalium; ectocervical involvement occurs with Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1 and HSV-2), Human Papillomavirus (HPV), and Trichomonas vaginalis.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:** 1. Neonates: Acquisition of Trichomonas vaginalis or Candida albicans during passage through an infected birth canal (manifesting as neonatal vaginal thrush or neonatal trichomoniasis). 2. Prepubescent Girls: Prepubescent vaginal epithelium is thin and unestrogenized, making the entire vagina susceptible to Neisseria gonorrhoeae (causing profuse gonococcal vulvovaginitis; STI detection strongly raises suspicion of child abuse); vaginal candidiasis is extremely rare in prepubescent girls. 3. Women of Reproductive Age: Highly susceptible to physiologic discharge, BV, VVC, T. vaginalis, C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae, M. genitalium, and DIV. 4. Postmenopausal / Estrogen-Deficient Women: Predominantly affected by Atrophic Vaginitis / Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause, where thin unestrogenized mucosal defenses allow secondary colonization and infection by enteric gram-negative bacilli (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella, Proteus, Enterobacter), enterococci, and streptococci.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:** Non-albicans Candida species (Candida glabrata, Candida tropicalis, Candida krusei, which are selected by short-course OTC azole usage and are common in immunocompromised/HIV-positive women), Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baking-associated yeast), Mobiluncus species, Atopobium vaginae, Ureaplasma urealyticum, Mycoplasma hominis (symbiotic intracellular survival inside T. vaginalis), Herpes Simplex Virus (causing severe, painful labial/cervical ulcerations), Human Papillomavirus, Cytomegalovirus, Group A beta-hemolytic Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes), Haemophilus influenzae, Listeria monocytogenes, Histoplasma capsulatum, Treponema pallidum (syphilis), Haemophilus ducreyi (chancroid), Klebsiella granulomatis (donovanosis / granuloma inguinale showing Donovan bodies), and Chlamydia trachomatis LGV serovars (L1, L2, L3).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:** 1. Bacterial Vaginosis: Loss of protective hydrogen peroxide-producing lactobacilli leads to an elevation in vaginal pH (>4.5), permitting massive overgrowth of Gardnerella vaginalis and anaerobic bacteria. G. vaginalis forms a dense, adherent polymicrobial biofilm on the vaginal squamous epithelium. Anaerobic metabolism of vaginal peptides produces volatile, malodorous amines (methylamine, putrescine, cadaverine) that volatilize at high pH, causing a characteristic fishy odor and increasing epithelial transudation and exfoliation. 2. Vulvovaginal Candidiasis: C. albicans transitions from saprophytic yeast blastospores to invasive hyphae/pseudohyphae that penetrate noncornified vaginal epithelial cells, triggering localized mucosal inflammation. Host immune hyperreactivity with excessive IL-1, IL-17, IL-18, and IL-22 inflammatory responses can paradoxically cause tissue damage. 3. Trichomoniasis: T. vaginalis protozoa adhere to vaginal and urethral squamous epithelium via specific adhesins, causing direct cytotoxic mucosal damage, petechial capillary hemorrhages, and marked polymorphonuclear leukocyte recruitment. 4. Infectious Cervicitis: C. trachomatis (via elementary body entry and intracellular reticulate body replication), N. gonorrhoeae, and M. genitalium infect columnar epithelial cells of the endocervical canal. The infected nonimmune host epithelial cells secrete proinflammatory cytokines, initiating an intense inflammatory cascade that causes cervical edema, mucopurulent exudate, and capillary fragility (friability). Microorganisms can ascend canalicularly from the endocervix/vagina into the endometrium, fallopian tubes, and pelvic cavity to cause PID.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:** 1. BV: Minimal mucosal leukocyte infiltration (hence vaginosis rather than vaginitis), but causes significant squamous cell exfoliation (clue cells) and degradation of protective cervical mucus, predisposing to ascending STI pathogens (HIV, HSV-2, N. gonorrhoeae, C. trachomatis) and upper tract infections (endometritis, salpingitis, post-hysterectomy cuff cellulitis). 2. VVC: Intense vulvovaginal mucosal erythema, edema, superficial radial ulcerations, excoriations from scratching, and satellite papulopustules. 3. Trichomoniasis: Diffuse mucosal redness, profuse purulent exudate, and strawberry cervix (punctate mucosal capillary hemorrhages). 4. Cervicitis: Endocervical mucosal erythema, purulent/mucopurulent discharge, sustained friability (bleeding upon swab contact), and micro-ulcerations (HSV/LGV). In DIV, loss of estrogenic maturation causes severe mucosal denudation, superficial erosions, and extensive epithelial desquamation yielding immature parabasal cells and dense leukocyte exudate.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):** 1. Bacterial Vaginosis: Mild-to-moderate thin gray-white discharge with a prominent fishy odor (especially noticeable after sexual intercourse or during menstruation when pH rises); minimal or absent vulvar itching/irritation, dysuria, or dyspareunia. 2. Vulvovaginal Candidiasis: Intense vulvar/perivaginal pruritus (itching), burning, irritation, and soreness; thick, white, curdy cottage cheese-like discharge (may be scant or absent); external dysuria (pain felt on inflamed labia during micturition) and introital dyspareunia. 3. Trichomoniasis: Profuse, yellow-green, frothy, malodorous, purulent vaginal discharge; vulvar irritation, pruritus, dysuria, and dyspareunia; occasionally mild lower abdominal discomfort. 4. Cervicitis: Frequently asymptomatic (up to 70% of C. trachomatis cases); when present, symptoms include mucopurulent vaginal discharge, postcoital bleeding, intermenstrual spotting, dysuria, and deep pelvic dyspareunia (without vulvar discomfort). 5. Desquamative Inflammatory Vaginitis (DIV): Severe purulent discharge, vulvar discomfort, burning, and severe dyspareunia in reproductive-age women. 6. Vulvodynia: Severe burning/stinging pain, raw sensation, or provoked pain upon vulvar contact (cotton swab test) or intercourse, lasting >=3 months without identifiable infectious cause.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):** 1. Bacterial Vaginosis: Thin, homogeneous, gray-white discharge smoothly coating the vaginal walls and introitus; uninflamed vaginal mucosa and normal vulva; uninflamed endocervix. 2. Vulvovaginal Candidiasis: Vulvar and labial erythema, edema, excoriations, shallow linear fissures/ulcerations (especially posterior introitus), satellite papulopustules, and adherent white cottage-cheese patches on erythematous vaginal or cervical mucosa. 3. Trichomoniasis: Vulvovaginal erythema and edema, copious purulent yellow-green discharge, and strawberry cervix (punctate macular hemorrhages on the cervix/vagina, seen in a minority of cases). 4. Cervicitis: Mucopurulent or purulent exudate visible in the endocervical os or emanating from an ectropion; sustained endocervical friability (bleeding easily induced by gentle swab contact); cervical erythema and edema; vesicular/ulcerative lesions (HSV) or strawberry cervix (T. vaginalis). 5. DIV: Diffuse or segmental mucosal erythema, superficial mucosal erosions, and copious purulent discharge. 6. Atrophic Vaginitis: Pale, smooth, dry vaginal mucosa with loss of rugae, petechiae, and purulent discharge.
- **Pathognomonic Features:** 1. Clue cells (vaginal epithelial cells studded with coccobacilli obscuring cell borders) on wet mount for Bacterial Vaginosis. 2. Strawberry cervix (cervical mucosal capillary dilation / punctate hemorrhages) for Trichomoniasis or cervicitis. 3. Thick adherent cottage cheese-like mucosal plaques and pseudohyphae/budding yeast on KOH mount for Vulvovaginal Candidiasis. 4. Mucopurulent endocervical discharge with sustained swab-induced endocervical friability for Mucopurulent Cervicitis. 5. Abundant parabasal cells and leukocytes with loss of mature squamous cells on wet mount for Desquamative Inflammatory Vaginitis. 6. Positive cotton-tip swab pressure point test (severe localized pain upon light touching of the vulvar vestibule) for Vestibulodynia / Vulvodynia.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):** CBC, ESR, and C-reactive protein (CRP) are generally normal in uncomplicated vulvovaginitis and localized cervicitis and are not routinely required. Elevated WBC count, ESR, or CRP suggest upper genital tract extension (Pelvic Inflammatory Disease / Salpingitis) or systemic illness. Mandatory point-of-care initial tests include: 1. Vaginal pH measurement using narrow-range pH paper (applied to lateral vaginal wall, avoiding endocervical mucus): normal pH is 3.8 to 4.5; pH >4.5 indicates BV, Trichomoniasis, DIV, or Atrophic Vaginitis; pH remains normal (<4.5) in VVC. 2. Whiff Test (10% Potassium Hydroxide / KOH test): adding 10% KOH to vaginal fluid releases a pungent, volatile fishy amine odor in BV and often Trichomoniasis. 3. Saline Wet Mount Microscopy: examination of vaginal fluid within 10 to 20 minutes under bright-field or phase-contrast microscopy to evaluate PMNs, motile flagellated trichomonads, clue cells, and epithelial maturity (parabasal cells). 4. 10% KOH Wet Mount Microscopy: KOH dissolves cellular elements, leaving fungal budding yeast and pseudohyphae/mycelia visible.
- **Imaging Modalities:** Transvaginal Ultrasonography (TVUS), CT, or MRI are not indicated for uncomplicated vulvovaginitis or cervicitis. TVUS or MRI is indicated if Pelvic Inflammatory Disease, tubo-ovarian abscess (TOA), pelvic mass, pyosalpinx, or anatomical complications are suspected.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):** 1. Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests (NAATs): The gold standard diagnostic method for Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Trichomonas vaginalis (e.g., APTIMA T. vaginalis Assay), and Mycoplasma genitalium. Optimal specimens include self-collected or clinician-collected vaginal swabs or endocervical swabs in women, and first-catch urine. 2. Microscopic Stains: Vaginal Gram stain evaluated by Nugent Scoring system (0 to 10 score based on morphotypes: Lactobacilli, Gardnerella/Bacteroides, Mobiluncus) is an objective diagnostic standard for BV; Cervical Gram stain showing >=20 PMNs per oil immersion field or intracellular gram-negative diplococci confirms gonococcal cervicitis (sensitivity ~60%). 3. Rapid Point-of-Care & Nonculture Tests: OSOM Trichomonas Rapid Test (antigen detection), Affirm VPIII DNA probe test (detects High Gardnerella, Candida, Trichomonas), OSOM BVBlue test (detects vaginal fluid sialidase activity). 4. Fungal Cultures: Fungal culture on Sabouraud agar is mandatory for complicated, recurrent, or non-albicans VVC, or when clinical suspicion of VVC is high but KOH microscopy is negative. 5. Trichomonas Culture: Inoculation into modified Diamond liquid medium (labor-intensive, largely superseded by NAAT).
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** 1. Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Trichomonas vaginalis, and Mycoplasma genitalium: Nucleic Acid Amplification Testing (NAAT). 2. Bacterial Vaginosis: Vaginal Gram stain evaluated by Nugent Criteria (gold standard in research/objective lab settings); Amsel Clinical Criteria (requiring >=3 of 4: homogeneous discharge, pH >4.6, positive Whiff test, clue cells >20%) in routine clinical practice. 3. Vulvovaginal Candidiasis: Fungal culture with species identification (for complicated/resistant cases) combined with microscopic identification of pseudohyphae/blastospores on 10% KOH wet prep. 4. Desquamative Inflammatory Vaginitis: Saline wet mount microscopy demonstrating abundance of leukocytes, loss of lactobacilli, and predominance of immature parabasal epithelial cells (parabasal cells >10%).

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:** Uncomplicated vulvovaginitis and cervicitis are outpatient conditions requiring no systemic hemodynamic resuscitation. Supportive measures include vulvar hygiene education (avoiding soaps, perfumed douches, scented liners, and chemical irritants), wearing loose cotton underwear, warm sitz baths or cold compresses for acute symptomatic relief of severe vulvar inflammation, vaginal lubricants or topical estrogen for atrophic vaginitis, and patient reassurance regarding normal physiologic secretions (finger-test recognition). Abstinence from sexual intercourse until patient and partner(s) complete therapy and symptoms resolve.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:** 1. Bacterial Vaginosis: Oral Metronidazole 500 mg PO twice daily for 7 days; OR Metronidazole 0.75% gel 5 g intravaginally once daily for 5 days; OR Clindamycin 2% cream 5 g intravaginally at bedtime for 7 days. Single 2 g oral dose of Secnidazole is an FDA-approved single-dose alternative. 2. Vulvovaginal Candidiasis (Uncomplicated): Oral Fluconazole 150 mg PO as a single dose; OR short-course topical azoles (e.g., Clotrimazole 1% cream, Miconazole 2% cream, Terconazole, or Nystatin vaginal suppositories for 1 to 7 days). 3. Trichomoniasis: Oral Metronidazole 2 g PO single dose; OR Oral Tinidazole 2 g PO single dose. In HIV-infected women, Metronidazole 500 mg PO BID for 7 days is preferred due to higher cure rates and reduction of HIV shedding. 4. Presumptive Empiric Therapy for High-Risk Cervicitis (women <25 years, new/multiple partners, or high STI risk): Oral Azithromycin 1 g PO single dose; OR Oral Doxycycline 100 mg PO BID for 7 days. If gonococcal risk/prevalence is >5%, add Intramuscular Ceftriaxone 250 mg IM single dose. 5. Desquamative Inflammatory Vaginitis: Intravaginal Clindamycin 2% cream 5 g inserted at bedtime for 4 weeks.
- **Alternative Regimens:** 1. BV Alternatives: Oral Tinidazole (2 g PO daily for 2 days or 1 g PO daily for 5 days); Oral Clindamycin (300 mg PO BID for 7 days); Clindamycin vaginal ovules 100 mg at bedtime for 3 days; Metronidazole extended-release 750 mg PO daily for 7 days. 2. Complicated / Recurrent VVC (>=4 episodes/year or non-albicans): Oral Fluconazole 150 mg PO given every 72 hours for 3 doses (days 1, 4, 7), followed by maintenance suppression with Fluconazole 150 mg PO weekly for 6 months. For C. glabrata or C. krusei: Intravaginal Boric Acid capsules 600 mg daily at bedtime for 14 days, or topical Flucytosine/Amphotericin B. 3. Resistant Trichomoniasis / Metronidazole Hypersensitivity: High-dose oral Metronidazole or Tinidazole (2 g daily for 14 days) plus expert consultation; Metronidazole desensitization for true allergy. Intravaginal nonoxynol-9 or boric acid acidification as off-label adjunctive options. 4. Persistent / Refractory Cervicitis (M. genitalium suspected): Oral Moxifloxacin 400 mg PO daily for 7 to 14 days. 5. DIV Alternatives: Intravaginal hydrocortisone / corticosteroid suppositories or intravaginal boric acid.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :** Surgical interventions are NOT indicated for uncomplicated vulvovaginitis or cervicitis. Surgical vestibulectomy (excision of the vulvar vestibule) is reserved as a last-resort option for severe, refractory provoked vestibulodynia (vulvodynia) failing conservative multidisciplinary therapies (physical therapy, psychological support). Biopsy of vulvar/cervical lesions may be required to exclude neoplasia, dysplasia, lichen sclerosus, or erosive lichen planus.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:** Acute ascending infection causing endometritis, acute salpingitis, pelvic peritonitis, and Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID); tubo-ovarian abscess (TOA); acute bartholinitis or Bartholin gland abscess; urethritis and acute urethral syndrome; increased genital tract viral shedding and heightened susceptibility to/transmission of HIV-1, HSV-2, N. gonorrhoeae, and C. trachomatis; post-hysterectomy vaginal cuff cellulitis / cuff infection; severe vulvar excoriation, secondary bacterial superinfection, and dysuria-induced urinary retention.
- **Long-term Sequelae:** 1. Reproductive Sequelae: Fallopian tube scarring/damage leading to tubal factor involuntary infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain. 2. Pregnancy & Neonatal Complications: Premature rupture of membranes (PROM), preterm labor, preterm delivery of low-birth-weight infants, intraamniotic infection (chorioamnionitis), postpartum endometritis, and neonatal transmission resulting in neonatal ophthalmia neonatorum (chlamydial/gonococcal conjunctivitis), neonatal chlamydial pneumonia, or neonatal thrush. 3. Oncologic Associations: Chronic persistent C. trachomatis and HPV cervicitis increase the risk of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and cervical cancer. 4. Chronic Pain & Psychological Impact: Recurrent/chronic vulvovaginitis, persistent DIV, and vulvodynia cause severe introital dyspareunia, sexual dysfunction, chronic pelvic pain, depression, PTSD, and impaired quality of life.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Clue cells (stippled squamous cells in BV), Fishy amine odor and Whiff test (BV), Donovan bodies (closed safety-pin encapsulated rods in Klebsiella granulomatis/donovanosis), Strawberry cervix (punctate mucosal hemorrhages in trichomoniasis), Cottage cheese-like discharge (VVC), Finger test (historical clue where patient extracts normal physiologic secretions with index finger), Parabasal cells (immature epithelial cells in DIV and atrophic vaginitis), Nugent score (gram stain score for BV), Amsel criteria (BV clinical criteria), Cotton-tip swab pressure point test (vulvodynia diagnosis), Elementary body vs Reticulate body (biphasic lifecycle of C. trachomatis).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:** 1. Bacterial Vaginosis is NOT a classic STI and can occur in sexually inexperienced women, yet it virtually never occurs in completely sexually inactive women and is highly prevalent/concordant in lesbian couples. 2. G. vaginalis can be cultured from 50% of healthy, asymptomatic women, and pure G. vaginalis inoculation fails to cause BV; disease requires the complex polymicrobial biofilm. 3. Metronidazole is highly effective clinically for BV despite G. vaginalis showing in vitro resistance to metronidazole. 4. Candida albicans is part of normal vaginal flora in 30% of healthy women; presence of yeast alone does not equal disease without clinical symptoms. 5. High PMN count on vaginal wet mount in BV is UNUSUAL and indicates co-existing cervicitis or trichomoniasis. 6. Metronidazole single 2 g dose is recommended for trichomoniasis, but is INEFFECTIVE for BV (requires 7-day course). 7. Treatment of male sexual partners does NOT reduce BV recurrence in women, whereas treating partners is MANDATORY in Trichomoniasis, Chlamydia, and Gonorrhea. 8. C. trachomatis cannot infect the squamous epithelium of the adult vagina; vaginal discharge in chlamydial infection originates from the endocervix (or from transitional epithelium in prepubescent girls). 9. Metronidazole treatment in asymptomatic pregnant women with trichomoniasis or BV does NOT reliably prevent preterm delivery and may even increase risk in some subsets, though symptomatic pregnant women MUST be treated. 10. DIV mimics trichomoniasis and estrogen deficiency, but occurs in young, estrogen-replete, STI-negative women and responds dramatically to 4 weeks of intravaginal clindamycin.
