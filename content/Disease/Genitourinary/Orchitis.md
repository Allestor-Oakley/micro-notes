# Orchitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Orchitis is an inflammatory reaction involving the testicle (testis), though the term is also used clinically to describe pain localized to the testicle without objective evidence of inflammation. Isolated orchitis is significantly less common than prostatitis or epididymitis and differs fundamentally from other male accessory sex gland infections in two major respects: bloodborne (hematogenous) dissemination is the primary route of infection, and viruses are implicated as key causative pathogens.
- **Major Classifications:** Major clinical classifications include: 1. Viral Orchitis: Isolated inflammation of the testicular parenchyma primarily caused by hematogenous viral dissemination (most prominently mumps virus, but also coxsackievirus B, lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus, and cytomegalovirus). 2. Pyogenic Bacterial Orchitis / Bacterial Epididymo-orchitis: Acute bacterial infection of the testis, which distinctly rarely occurs as an isolated testicular infection but almost always arises secondary to contiguous extension/spread from acute bacterial epididymitis (producing combined epididymo-orchitis) or rarely via hematogenous bacterial seeding. 3. Specific Granulomatous or Chronic Orchitis: Chronic testicular involvement resulting from contiguous spread of chronic epididymal infections, such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis (tuberculous orchitis), Blastomyces dermatitidis (blastomycosis), Brucella species (brucellosis), or Treponema pallidum (syphilitic orchitis / testicular gumma). 4. Noninfectious or Iatrogenic Orchitis/Epididymo-orchitis: Inflammatory testicular reaction following intravesical Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) therapy for bladder carcinoma, or associated with systemic autoimmune/collagen vascular disorders (such as Henoch-Schönlein purpura or Polyarteritis nodosa).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:** Isolated orchitis is rare. Sexually transmitted epididymo-orchitis is most common in young, sexually active men aged 18 to 35 years, whereas non-sexually transmitted bacterial epididymo-orchitis with coliform or Pseudomonas species predominates in men older than 35 years. Risk factors for bacterial orchitis/epididymo-orchitis include underlying genitourinary tract anatomical or functional abnormalities, recent genitourinary tract manipulation, surgery, or indwelling urinary catheterization (especially in hospitalized or bacteriuric patients), acute or chronic bacterial prostatitis, lack of circumcision, and insertive anal intercourse. For viral orchitis, mumps is the main cause; while mumps orchitis is extremely rare in prepubertal boys, it occurs in approximately 20% of postpubertal males with mumps virus infection.

## Etiology
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):** Viruses account for the majority of isolated orchitis cases, with Mumps virus being the most prominent. Pyogenic bacterial orchitis is most commonly caused by gram-negative coliform rods, specifically Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as well as staphylococci and streptococci. In sexually active men, Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae are major etiologies causing epididymo-orchitis.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:** 1. Prepubertal Boys: Mumps virus infection rarely causes orchitis in prepubertal boys; bacterial epididymo-orchitis in children is often associated with underlying congenital urologic structural or functional abnormalities (e.g., vesicoureteral reflux, posterior urethral valves) and coliform bacteria. 2. Postpubertal Males and Young Adults (Aged 18 to 35 Years): Mumps virus causes orchitis in ~20% of postpubertal males; sexually transmitted pathogens Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae predominate in young adults, along with enteric E. coli in men practicing insertive anal intercourse. 3. Older Men (Aged >35 Years): Coliform organisms (primarily E. coli, K. pneumoniae) and Pseudomonas species predominate, typically secondary to bladder outlet obstruction, prostatic enlargement, or recent urologic instrumentation.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:** Coxsackie B viruses, Lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV, reported in 4 of 65 AIDS autopsy cases), Toxoplasma gondii (reported in 2 of 17 AIDS autopsy cases), Mycobacterium tuberculosis (tubercular orchitis/epididymo-orchitis), Treponema pallidum (syphilitic orchitis / testicular gumma), Brucella melitensis / Brucella species (brucellosis via hematogenous seeding in endemic regions), Blastomyces dermatitidis, Cryptococcus neoformans, Aspergillus fumigatus, Nocardia species (in immunosuppressed patients), Haemophilus parainfluenzae, Candida species, and iatrogenic Mycobacterium bovis (complicating intravesical BCG therapy for bladder cancer). In HIV/AIDS patients, opportunistic pathogens and systemic viral infections involve the testes, producing characteristic histological changes including azoospermia, marked spermatogenic arrest, germ cell degeneration, peritubular fibrosis, and Leydig cell depletion.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:** Orchitis differs fundamentally from other male lower urogenital tract infections in its route of spread: 1. Hematogenous Dissemination: Bloodborne seeding is the primary route for viral orchitis (e.g., mumps, coxsackievirus, LCMV) and certain specific bacterial infections (e.g., Brucella species, secondary or tertiary syphilis). Systemic viremia leads to testicular parenchymal seeding and inflammation. 2. Direct Contiguous Extension: Pyogenic bacterial orchitis rarely occurs as a primary isolated testicular infection; instead, it develops secondary to retrograde ascending bacterial infection through the ejaculatory ducts, vas deferens, and epididymis (causing epididymitis first), which then directly invades the adjacent testicular tissue across the tunica albuginea to produce acute epididymo-orchitis.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:** In viral (mumps) orchitis, viral replication within the seminiferous tubules causes patchy intratubular inflammation, diffuse leukocytic infiltration, edema, and cellular congestion. Because the testicular parenchyma is enclosed within the nondistensible fibrous tunica albuginea, severe inflammatory edema causes marked elevation of intratesticular pressure, leading to pressure necrosis and ischemia of the seminiferous tubules. Resolution of severe viral orchitis results in variable degrees of testicular atrophy (occurring in approximately 50% of involved testes) due to seminiferous tubule destruction, tubular sclerosis, and germ cell loss. In pyogenic bacterial orchitis, the tissue response is characterized by severe suppurative inflammation, microabscess formation, parenchymal necrosis, and extensive edema, which can progress to full testicular infarction (due to spermatic cord venous thrombosis or severe tissue ischemia), scrotal abscess, or pyocele formation between the layers of the tunica vaginalis. In HIV/AIDS, testicular tissue shows non-specific histopathologic changes of severe systemic illness including azoospermia, marked spermatogenic arrest, germ cell degeneration, peritubular fibrosis, and Leydig cell depletion.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):** 1. Viral (Mumps) Orchitis: Abrupt onset of testicular pain and swelling, typically beginning 4 to 6 days after the onset of parotitis (though it may occur without preceding or concurrent parotid involvement). Symptoms range from mild testicular discomfort to severe, excruciating testicular pain accompanied by systemic constitutional symptoms including high fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, and prostration. Pain and swelling are unilateral in approximately 70% of cases, with contralateral testicular swelling developing 1 to 9 days later in remaining cases. 2. Pyogenic Bacterial Orchitis / Epididymo-orchitis: Acutely ill presentation with high fever, severe testicular discomfort and swelling, and pain that classically radiates along the spermatic cord into the inguinal canal, frequently accompanied by systemic signs such as nausea, vomiting, and chills. Patients may report antecedent or concurrent lower urinary tract symptoms (dysuria, frequency, urgency) or urethral discharge. 3. Granulomatous / Tuberculous Orchitis: Chronic, insidious onset of a sensation of testicular heaviness, dull aching pain, or painless gradual testicular or scrotal swelling.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):** Physical exam reveals an acutely enlarged, swollen, and exquisitely tender testicle. Overlying scrotal skin is typically erythematous, warm, and edematous. An acute reactive hydrocele (secretion of inflammatory fluid between layers of the tunica vaginalis) is commonly present on palpation. In bacterial epididymo-orchitis, an indurated, tender, swollen mass is felt in the posterior scrotum where the testicle and epididymis become indistinguishable due to extensive confluent inflammation. Concomitant epididymitis, inflammation of the spermatic cord (funiculitis), or urethral discharge (spontaneous or on urethral stripping) may be observed. Rectal exam may reveal prostate tenderness if bacterial prostatitis is the underlying focus. Chronic tuberculous or fungal disease may demonstrate a beadlike enlargement of the vas deferens or a chronic draining scrotal sinus.
- **Pathognomonic Features:** 1. Unilateral or bilateral acute testicular swelling and severe pain developing 4 to 6 days following acute parotitis in a postpubertal male (Mumps Orchitis). 2. Exquisitely tender, swollen testicle with an overlying erythematous scrotal wall and reactive hydrocele, where the testicle and epididymis merge into a single inflammatory mass (Bacterial Epididymo-orchitis). 3. Beadlike enlargement of the vas deferens with a chronic draining scrotal sinus in tuberculous epididymo-orchitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):** 1. Complete Blood Count (CBC): Typically demonstrates marked leukocytosis with a left shift in acute pyogenic bacterial orchitis or severe viral orchitis. Mild leukocytosis with lymphocytosis or monocytosis may be seen in viral cases. 2. Inflammatory Markers: Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) are elevated in acute inflammatory orchitis or epididymo-orchitis. 3. Urinalysis: Pyuria, leukocyte esterase, bacteriuria, or nitrites are frequently present in bacterial epididymo-orchitis secondary to lower urinary tract or prostatic infection; urinalysis is typically normal (sterile) in isolated viral orchitis unless secondary to UTI.
- **Imaging Modalities:** Color-Flow Doppler Ultrasonography is the essential, primary imaging modality of choice for scrotal/testicular evaluation. Sonography demonstrates testicular enlargement, heterogeneous or hypoechoic parenchymal echotexture, reactive hydrocele, and thickened scrotal skin. Crucially, Color-Flow Doppler reveals diffusely increased intratesticular vascular flow/perfusion in acute orchitis, which definitively differentiates it from Testicular Torsion (where intratesticular blood flow is completely absent or markedly diminished). Ultrasonography is also highly sensitive for detecting complications such as testicular infarction (avascular parenchymal regions), testicular abscess (complex fluid collection with thick hyperemic walls), pyocele, or focal epididymal lesions.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):** 1. Urine / Urethral NAATs: Nucleic acid amplification testing of first-catch urine or urethral swabs for Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae is mandatory in all young, sexually active men presenting with orchitis or epididymo-orchitis. 2. Midstream Urine Culture: Clean-catch midstream urine culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing to identify enteric gram-negative bacilli (E. coli, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa) or Enterococcus species. 3. Urethral Smear Gram Stain: Demonstrates polymorphonuclear leukocytes (>=2 PMNs per oil immersion field) and intracellular gram-negative diplococci (in N. gonorrhoeae) or non-gonococcal urethritis. 4. Viral Diagnostics / PCR / Serology: Confirmation of mumps orchitis via mumps RT-PCR (from buccal swab or urine) or mumps IgM/IgG serology; viral PCR for coxsackievirus, LCMV, or CMV when indicated. 5. Special Cultures / Histopathology / Stains: Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) stain and mycobacterial culture (or fine-needle aspiration) for suspected tuberculous orchitis; fungal stains/cultures for blastomycosis or cryptococcosis; serologic testing for syphilis (RPR/VDRL and treponemal tests) or Brucella species.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Color-Flow Doppler Ultrasonography combined with pathogen-specific Nucleic Acid Amplification Testing (NAAT for C. trachomatis/N. gonorrhoeae) or Midstream Urine / Viral Culture.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:** General supportive and conservative measures are essential for all patients with acute orchitis: bed rest, scrotal elevation (using a scrotal supporter or athletic supporter to reduce pain and mechanical traction on the spermatic cord), local application of ice packs, adequate hydration, systemic analgesics, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) for pain and fever control. Patients presenting with severe toxicity, high fever, intractable nausea/vomiting, or signs of sepsis require hospitalization for intravenous fluid hydration and parenteral antimicrobial therapy.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:** Empiric antimicrobial therapy is indicated for acute bacterial orchitis / epididymo-orchitis based on age, sexual history, and risk factors (viral mumps orchitis is managed with supportive care alone as antibiotics are ineffective): 1. Age <35 Years (Sexually Transmitted Epididymo-orchitis likely due to C. trachomatis or N. gonorrhoeae): Intramuscular Ceftriaxone 250 mg IM as a single dose PLUS Oral Doxycycline 100 mg PO twice daily for 10 to 14 days (or Azithromycin 1 g PO single dose). 2. Men practicing insertive anal intercourse (STI pathogens plus enteric coliforms): Intramuscular Ceftriaxone 250 mg IM single dose PLUS Oral Levofloxacin 500 mg PO once daily (or Ofloxacin 300 mg PO twice daily) for 10 to 14 days. 3. Age >35 Years or Low Risk for STIs (Enteric Gram-Negative Uropathogens likely): Oral Levofloxacin 500 mg PO once daily OR Oral Ofloxacin 300 mg PO twice daily for 10 to 14 days. 4. Severe Sepsis / Inpatient Admission: Broad-spectrum parenteral therapy with a 3rd generation cephalosporin (e.g., Ceftriaxone 1-2 g IV daily) or fluoroquinolone, modified based on urine culture and susceptibility.
- **Alternative Regimens:** 1. Alternative Oral Antimicrobial Regimens for Enteric/Coliform Bacterial Orchitis: Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) 160/800 mg PO twice daily for 14 days (if organism is susceptible). 2. Alternative Parenteral Regimens for Severe / MDR Bacterial Infection: Intravenous Aminoglycosides (Gentamicin), Carbapenems (Ertapenem, Imipenem, Meropenem), or Piperacillin-Tazobactam. 3. Specific Pathogens: For Tuberculous Orchitis: standard multidrug antituberculous therapy (Rifampin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol). For Brucella Orchitis: Doxycycline plus Rifampin or Gentamicin. For Syphilitic Orchitis: Parenteral Penicillin G.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :** Emergency surgical exploration is indicated if testicular torsion cannot be definitively excluded by Doppler ultrasound. Surgical intervention is required for acute local complications of pyogenic bacterial orchitis, including: 1. Percutaneous or open surgical drainage of scrotal or testicular abscess or pyocele. 2. Orchiectomy (surgical removal of the involved testicle) for extensive testicular infarction, complete parenchymal liquefaction/necrosis, severe pyocele refractory to drainage, or uncontrolled suppurative infection. 3. Fine-needle aspiration or testicular biopsy for histological or microbiological diagnosis of suspected granulomatous (tuberculous, fungal) or neoplastic masses.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:** Scrotal abscess / testicular abscess formation; pyocele (accumulation of pus between tunica vaginalis layers); testicular infarction (secondary to inflammatory compression of testicular blood supply or spermatic cord vessel thrombosis); chronic draining scrotal sinus; severe systemic sepsis and septic shock; acute urinary retention.
- **Long-term Sequelae:** 1. Testicular Atrophy: Occurs in approximately 50% of testes affected by mumps orchitis due to ischemic pressure necrosis within the rigid tunica albuginea, and can also follow severe bacterial testicular infarction. 2. Impaired Spermatogenesis and Infertility: Azoospermia, oligospermia, germ cell degeneration, and impaired fertility (though unilateral orchitis rarely causes total permanent infertility if the contralateral testis remains normal and unaffected). 3. Chronic Scrotal / Testicular Pain: Persistent, refractory chronic pain syndrome that is difficult to manage. 4. Hypogonadism: Leydig cell dysfunction or destruction leading to reduced testosterone production in severe bilateral cases.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** Mumps parotitis-orchitis sequence, Color-Flow Doppler ultrasound hyperemia, Reactive hydrocele, Pressure necrosis of seminiferous tubules, Beadlike vas deferens (tuberculous disease), Pyocele, Testicular infarction, Prehn sign (elevation of scrotum relieves pain in epididymo-orchitis but not in testicular torsion), Spermatic cord funiculitis.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:** 1. Orchitis differs fundamentally from other male accessory gland infections in that viruses (mumps) are primary pathogens and bloodborne (hematogenous) spread is the major route of infection. 2. Acute bacterial orchitis distinctly almost NEVER occurs as a primary isolated testicular infection; it almost exclusively arises secondary to contiguous extension from acute bacterial epididymitis (epididymo-orchitis). 3. While mumps orchitis is extremely rare in prepubertal boys, it affects ~20% of postpubertal males with mumps. 4. Mumps orchitis can occur in the complete absence of parotid swelling (parotitis) in a minority of patients. 5. Antibiotics have NO role in mumps viral orchitis and therapy is purely supportive, whereas bacterial orchitis requires prompt antimicrobial treatment. 6. Testicular torsion must ALWAYS be ruled out in any young male presenting with acute unilateral testicular pain; Doppler ultrasound demonstrating increased hyperemic blood flow confirms orchitis/epididymitis, whereas absent blood flow indicates surgical emergency torsion. 7. Testicular atrophy occurs in ~50% of mumps orchitis cases due to high intratesticular pressure within the noncompliant tunica albuginea, but total permanent infertility is rare unless the disease is severe and bilateral.
