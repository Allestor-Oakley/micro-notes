# Asymptomatic Bacteriuria

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Asymptomatic bacteriuria (ABU) is defined as the presence of a quantitative growth of a bacterial pathogen in the urine of an individual without clinical symptoms or signs attributable to a urinary tract infection. Statistically, in asymptomatic women, it is defined as the presence of 10^5 or more colony-forming units (CFU)/mL of the same uropathogen in two consecutive clean-catch voided urine specimens (which carries a 95% probability of true bacteriuria; a single voided specimen carries an 80% probability). In asymptomatic men, a single clean-catch voided urine specimen containing 10^3 or 10^5 or more CFU/mL of a single uropathogen defines bacteriuria. In catheterized individuals (men or women), a single urine specimen obtained via catheterization containing 10^2 CFU/mL or more of a uropathogen defines bacteriuria.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - By Population / Health Status: (1) ABU in Healthy Premenopausal Women; (2) ABU in Pregnant Women; (3) ABU in Postmenopausal and Elderly Women; (4) ABU in Elderly Men; (5) ABU in Diabetic Patients (type 1 and type 2); (6) ABU in Patients with Spinal Cord Injury / Neurogenic Bladder; (7) ABU in Catheterized / Institutionalized Patients (short-term and long-term indwelling catheters); (8) ABU in Renal Transplant Recipients; (9) ABU in Patients Prior to Invasive Urologic Procedures.
  - By Microbiologic Pattern: Single-species ABU vs. Polymicrobial / Contaminated Bacteriuria; Transient / Spontaneous-resolving Bacteriuria vs. Persistent / Recurrent ABU.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Prevalence by Population Group:
    - Preschool Children & Schoolgirls: 1% to 2% prevalence in infants (more common in boys <3 months, then girls); ~1.2% prevalence in school-age girls (about 5% of girls develop ABU at some time before leaving high school); 0.03% in schoolboys.
    - Healthy Premenopausal Women: 2% to 5% prevalence.
    - Pregnant Women: 4% to 7% prevalence (similar to nonpregnant women, but carries a unique 20% to 40% risk of progression to acute pyelonephritis).
    - Diabetic Women: High prevalence (up to 3-fold higher than non-diabetic women, ranging from 9% to 27%).
    - Older Adults & Elderly (>65 years): At least 10% to 15% of men and 20% of women older than 65 years in the community have ABU. In long-term care facilities / nursing homes, prevalence reaches 25% to 50% in elderly women and 15% to 40% in elderly men.
    - Catheterized Individuals: Single catheterization causes infection in 1% of ambulatory patients and >=10% of hospitalized patients; essentially 100% of individuals with long-term indwelling catheters (>30 days) develop ABU.
    - Renal Transplant Recipients: At least 50% develop UTIs or ABU in the early post-transplant period.
  - Specific Risk Factors: Sexual intercourse and new sex partners; diaphragm and spermicide use (nonoxynol-9 increases E. coli adherence and coliform colonization); lack of postcoital urination; history of childhood UTIs or UTIs in female relatives; estrogen deficiency in postmenopausal women (loss of protective vaginal lactobacilli replaced by coliforms); urinary incontinence and bladder prolapse; prostatic hypertrophy and obstructive uropathy in older men; fecal incontinence and perineal soiling in demented/debilitated elderly; neurogenic bladder dysfunction; urinary tract instrumentation and indwelling catheters; diabetes mellitus; and sickle cell trait in pregnancy (historically reported).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Escherichia coli (Uropathogenic E. coli / UPEC): The single most common microorganism causing asymptomatic bacteriuria across all population groups, accounting for 70% to 90% of isolates in healthy community-dwelling women and pregnant women.
  - Other Enterobacteriaceae: Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter species, Citrobacter species.
  - Gram-Positive Cocci: Enterococcus faecalis, Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus / GBS; important in pregnant women), Staphylococcus saprophyticus, and Staphylococcus epidermidis / Coagulase-negative staphylococci.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children & Infants: E. coli, Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterococcus faecalis.
  - Pregnant Women: E. coli (dominant), Group B Streptococcus (S. agalactiae; requires intrapartum penicillin prophylaxis), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis.
  - Elderly & Institutionalized / Complicated Hosts: Decreased proportion of E. coli (~40% to 50%) with a corresponding increase in Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter species, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Providencia stuartii, Morganella morganii, Enterococcus faecalis, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and drug-resistant uropathogens (extended-spectrum beta-lactamase / ESBL-producing E. coli ST131, carbapenemase producers).
  - Catheterized Patients: Polymicrobial infection (in up to 25% to 50%), Proteus, Pseudomonas, Enterobacter, Klebsiella, Serratia, Enterococcus, and Candida species (C. albicans, C. glabrata, C. krusei).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Fastidious & Higher Bacteria: Corynebacterium urealyticum (formerly Corynebacterium group D2; gram-positive, slow-growing, urea-splitting bacillus causing encrusted cystitis and struvite stones in renal transplant recipients and immunocompromised hosts); Actinobaculum schaalii (facultative anaerobic gram-positive bacillus, missed on routine cultures, causing urosepsis in elderly >65 years with renal stones/instrumentation); Gardnerella vaginalis, Ureaplasma urealyticum, and Mycoplasma hominis (isolated with special media, questionable pathogenetic role).
  - Fungi: Candida species (Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, Candida krusei; common candiduria in catheterized, ICU, or diabetic patients receiving broad-spectrum antibiotics).
  - Viruses: Adenovirus (type 11; causes hemorrhagic cystitis or asymptomatic shedding in hematopoietic stem cell transplant and renal transplant recipients).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Ascending Route from Fecal/Vaginal Reservoir: Microorganisms originating from the bowel reservoir colonize the periurethral area and vaginal introitus, ascend through the short female urethra into the bladder, and persist in the urinary lumen.
  - Commensalism & Virulence Attenuation: Strains causing asymptomatic bacteriuria (ABU E. coli) are frequently characterized by an attenuation or loss of traditional urovirulence factors. Compared to pyelonephritic or cystitis UPEC strains, ABU E. coli isolates less frequently express P fimbriae (papG alleles), hemolysin (hlyA), cytotoxic necrotizing factor 1 (cnf1), or S fimbriae. Instead, ABU strains survive in the bladder as "commensals" by carrying genomic deletions, point mutations, or transcriptional downregulation of virulence genes (e.g., downregulated type 1 or P fimbriae), enabling them to inhabit urine without triggering host mucosal inflammation.
  - Bladder Quiescent Intracellular Reservoirs (QIRs): UPEC can invade superficial umbrella cells of the bladder epithelium, forming intracellular bacterial communities (IBCs) and quiescent intracellular reservoirs that evade antimicrobial clearance and host defenses, persisting as a source of recurrent bacteriuria.
  - Reduced Host Innate Inflammatory Response: Host genetic factors play a critical role. Individuals with asymptomatic bacteriuria often exhibit attenuated innate immune responses—specifically reduced Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) expression, reduced CXCR1/CXCR2 IL-8 receptor expression on urothelial cells and neutrophils, and altered interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) transcription. Without robust TLR4-mediated CXCL8/IL-8 release and neutrophil recruitment (pyuria), the bacteria persist in the bladder lumen without causing mucosal inflammation, pain, or systemic febrile responses.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Minimal / Attenuated Inflammatory Response: Unlike acute pyelonephritis or cystitis, which trigger intense neutrophilic inflammation, cytokine release (IL-1beta, IL-6, IL-8), and tissue necrosis, ABU is characterized by lack of urothelial destruction, absence of mucosal ulceration, and low tissue inflammatory response.
  - Protective Inter-Bacterial Competition: Colonization of the bladder by low-virulence ABU strains provides a protective effect by outcompeting and preventing colonization/superinfection by more virulent, symptomatic uropathogenic strains.
  - Physiological Alterations in Pregnancy: In pregnant women, gestational progesterone and hyperestrogenism cause ureteral and renal pelvic dilation, markedly decreased ureteral peristalsis (stasis), and increased bladder capacity/compliance starting at 7 weeks gestation. Additionally, pregnant urine has higher pH and osmolality favoring bacterial growth. These factors allow ABU to easily ascend to the kidney, causing acute pyelonephritis in up to 20% to 40% of untreated pregnant women.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - ABSENT BY DEFINITION: Patients with asymptomatic bacteriuria have NO lower urinary tract symptoms (no dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain, or hematuria) and NO upper urinary tract / systemic symptoms (no fever, chills, rigors, flank pain, or Costovertebral Angle / CVA tenderness).
  - Nonspecific Symptoms in Elderly / Demented Patients: In frail or demented elderly patients, ABU is frequently coincidental; chronic symptoms such as baseline urinary frequency, urgency, incontinence, or stable cognitive impairment are NOT attributable to ABU and do NOT constitute symptomatic UTI.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Normal Physical Examination: Absence of suprapubic tenderness, absence of costovertebral angle (CVA) / flank tenderness, normal vital signs (afebrile, hemodynamically stable).
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Diagnostic Quantitative High-Titer Bacteriuria in a Completely Asymptomatic Host: Presence of >=10^5 CFU/mL of a single uropathogen in 2 consecutive voided urine samples in an asymptomatic female (or 1 sample in a male / catheterized patient) in the complete absence of clinical symptoms.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - CBC & Systemic Inflammatory Biomarkers: Normal WBC count (no leukocytosis or bandemia); normal C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) levels.
  - Urinalysis (Pyuria & Dipstick): Pyuria (>=10 WBCs/mm3 of uncentrifuged midstream urine in a counting chamber, or >=5-10 WBCs/HPF of centrifuged sediment) is present in the VAST MAJORITY (over 90%) of patients with asymptomatic bacteriuria (including pregnant women, diabetics, elderly, and catheterized patients). However, PYURIA ALONE IS NONSPECIFIC and DOES NOT INDICATE SYMPTOMATIC INFECTION or warrant antimicrobial therapy in an asymptomatic patient! Urine dipstick leukocyte esterase and nitrite tests are positive, but a positive dipstick in an asymptomatic patient does NOT justify antibiotic treatment.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Routine Imaging NOT Indicated: Renal ultrasonography, CT, or MRI are NOT indicated for uncomplicated ABU.
  - Selective Indications for Imaging: Postpartum evaluation (ultrasound/CT) in pregnant women whose ABU was difficult to eradicate or relapsed (to rule out pre-existing congenital anomalies or scarring); or pre-procedural imaging prior to urologic surgery.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Urine Culture (Quantitative Colony Count - Core Diagnostic Standard):
    - Collection Methods: Midstream clean-catch voided specimen (after cleansing labia/glans), "in and out" urethral catheterization, or suprapubic bladder aspiration.
    - Quantitative Diagnostic Thresholds for ABU:
      1. Asymptomatic Females (Clean-Catch Voided Urine): >=10^5 CFU/mL (100,000 bacteria/mL) of the same single uropathogen in TWO CONSECUTIVE clean-catch urine specimens (confirming true bacteriuria with 95% probability). (If 10^4 to 10^5 CFU/mL, a repeat specimen yields >=10^5 in only 5% of cases).
      2. Asymptomatic Males (Clean-Catch Voided Urine): >=10^3 to 10^5 CFU/mL of a single uropathogen in a SINGLE clean-catch urine specimen.
      3. Catheterized Patients (Male or Female): >=10^2 CFU/mL of a single uropathogen in a SINGLE catheterized urine specimen.
      4. Suprapubic Bladder Aspiration: Any detectable bacterial growth (>=10^2 CFU/mL) defines bacteriuria.
    - Gram Stain of Uncentrifuged Urine: Presence of >=1 bacterium per oil immersion field correlates with >=10^5 CFU/mL.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Quantitative Urine Culture demonstrating >=10^5 CFU/mL of the same uropathogen in two consecutive clean-catch voided urine specimens in an asymptomatic female (or a single specimen in males or catheterized patients) obtained in the complete absence of urinary or systemic symptoms represents the gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - General Approach — DO NOT SCREEN, DO NOT TREAT (in Most Populations): Routine screening and antimicrobial treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria are STRICTLY NOT RECOMMENDED for the vast majority of patient populations! Treating ABU yields NO CLINICAL BENEFIT, does not prevent symptomatic UTIs or renal failure, increases healthcare costs, causes drug toxicity/side effects, increases risk of C. difficile colitis, and drives emergence of multidrug-resistant uropathogens. Furthermore, in young women with recurrent UTIs, treating ABU actually INCREASES the risk of subsequent symptomatic UTIs by destroying protective low-virulence bacterial colonization!
  - Clear Established Indications for Screening & Antimicrobial Treatment (ONLY 2 MAIN CATEGORIES):
    1. Pregnant Women: Screen all pregnant women at 12 to 16 weeks gestation (or at first prenatal visit). If positive, treat with a short course of antimicrobials and perform monthly follow-up surveillance cultures.
    2. Patients Scheduled for Traumatic Urologic Procedures Associated with Mucosal Bleeding: (e.g., transurethral resection of the prostate / TURP, percutaneous nephrolithotomy, lithotripsy). Obtain urine culture prior to procedure and initiate antimicrobials 12 hours to immediately before the procedure to prevent post-procedure bacteremia and urosepsis.
  - Controversial / Special Exceptions: Prior to renal transplantation (early post-transplant period in some centers, though recent RCTs show no benefit and guidelines increasingly advise against treating ABU in kidney transplant recipients >2 months post-transplant).
  - Populations in Whom Screening & Treatment are EXPLICITLY NOT RECOMMENDED: Premenopausal nonpregnant women; postmenopausal women; diabetic patients; elderly ambulatory or institutionalized nursing home residents; patients with spinal cord injury / neurogenic bladder; patients with short-term or long-term indwelling urinary catheters; and patients undergoing non-urologic surgery.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Regimens for Approved Indications (Pregnancy or Pre-Urologic Procedure):
    - Duration: Short-course therapy (3 to 7 days) based on urine culture susceptibilities.
    - Pregnant Women First-Line Regimens:
      - Nitrofurantoin 100 mg PO bid x 5 to 7 days (avoid in 1st trimester due to potential birth defect risks per ACOG, and avoid at term / 38-42 weeks due to neonatal hemolysis risk).
      - Fosfomycin 3 g PO single dose.
      - Cephalexin 500 mg PO qid (or 500 mg bid) x 3 to 7 days OR Amoxicillin-Clavulanate 500/125 mg PO bid x 3 to 7 days.
      - Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) 160/800 mg PO bid x 3 days (ONLY if susceptible; avoid in 1st trimester and 3rd trimester/near term).
    - Pre-Urologic Procedure Regimen:
      - Antimicrobial targeted to culture susceptibility (e.g., Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV single dose 12 hours or immediately prior to procedure, or Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO). Discontinue immediately after procedure or catheter removal.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Alternative Oral Options in Pregnancy: Amoxicillin 500 mg PO tid x 3 to 7 days (only if organism susceptible, though high E. coli resistance); Cefixime 400 mg PO daily x 3 to 7 days.
  - Asymptomatic Candiduria Management: Treatment is NOT recommended for asymptomatic candiduria in ambulatory or catheterized patients. Removal or replacement of the indwelling catheter alone clears candiduria in 30% to 40% of cases. Treatment is indicated ONLY for patients undergoing traumatic urologic procedures (Oral Fluconazole 200 to 400 mg PO daily x 7 to 14 days prior to procedure) or low-birth-weight neonates.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Removal / Replacement of Indwelling Catheters or Stents: Catheter removal or exchange is the primary non-pharmacologic intervention for catheter-associated ABU or candiduria (clears bacteriuria/candiduria in 30% to 40% without antibiotics).
  - Pre-procedural Antimicrobial Prophylaxis for TURP / Endourology: Pre-procedural administration of targeted IV antibiotics prior to transurethral resection of prostate or lithotripsy in patients with documented ABU.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Acute Pyelonephritis in Pregnancy: Occurs in up to 20% to 40% of untreated pregnant women with ABU (the most common non-obstetric cause of maternal hospitalization during pregnancy), leading to maternal sepsis, preterm labor, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), and low birth weight.
  - Post-Urologic Procedure Bacteremia & Urosepsis: High risk of severe Gram-negative bacteremia, septic shock, and mortality if traumatic urologic procedures (TURP) are performed in the presence of untreated ABU.
  - Ascending Pyelonephritis in Obstructed / Transplanted Kidneys: Ascending infection in patients with ureteral obstruction, renal stents, or neurogenic bladder.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Adverse Effects of Unnecessary Antibiotic Treatment of ABU:
    1. Increased Risk of Symptomatic Recurrent UTIs: Eradicating low-virulence ABU strains eliminates protective microbial competition, allowing colonization by highly virulent uropathogenic E. coli strains and doubling the rate of subsequent symptomatic cystitis/pyelonephritis!
    2. Emergence of Multidrug-Resistant Uropathogens: Drives selection of ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae, fluoroquinolone resistance, and carbapenem resistance.
    3. Clostridioides difficile Infection & Drug Toxicity: Increases risk of C. difficile diarrhea and adverse drug reactions (allergic reactions, renal toxicity, bone marrow suppression).
  - No Long-term Adverse Effects in Nonpregnant Adult/Elderly Populations: In nonpregnant adults, elderly, and diabetics, untreated ABU does NOT lead to hypertension, chronic kidney disease, end-stage renal disease (ESRD), or decreased survival.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Two Consecutive Cultures >=10^5 CFU/mL: Classic Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) diagnostic criteria for ABU in asymptomatic females.
  - Do Not Screen, Do Not Treat: Fundamental antimicrobial stewardship mantra for ABU in nonpregnant adults, elderly, diabetics, and catheterized patients.
  - Commensalization / Virulence Gene Loss: Genetic attenuation of ABU E. coli strains via loss of P fimbriae, hemolysin, and toxins, allowing peaceful bladder coexistence.
  - TLR4 Downregulation: Host genetic trait in ABU patients characterized by reduced urothelial Toll-like receptor 4 expression and suppressed mucosal cytokine release.
  - Quiescent Intracellular Reservoirs (QIRs): Intracellular E. coli bacterial communities inside bladder umbrella cells causing persistent ABU.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - The Pregnancy Exception: Asymptomatic bacteriuria MUST be screened for and treated in pregnant women (12 to 16 weeks gestation) because untreated ABU leads to acute pyelonephritis in up to 20% to 40% of cases and adverse fetal outcomes!
  - The Urologic Procedure Exception: ABU MUST be screened for and treated prior to traumatic urologic procedures with mucosal bleeding (TURP, lithotripsy) to prevent severe post-procedure bacteremia and urosepsis!
  - Treatment Increases Symptomatic UTI Risk: Treating ABU in young nonpregnant women with recurrent UTIs actually INCREASES the frequency of subsequent symptomatic UTIs by destroying protective low-virulence bacterial colonization!
  - Pyuria Does NOT Equal Infection: More than 90% of elderly, diabetic, catheterized, and pregnant patients with ABU have pyuria (leukocytes in urine), but pyuria in an asymptomatic patient is NOT an indication for antibiotic therapy!
  - Asymptomatic Candiduria Rule: Asymptomatic Candida in urine (candiduria) should NOT be treated with antifungals in catheterized or diabetic patients; removing/replacing the catheter clears candiduria in 30% to 40% of cases!
  - Nitrofurantoin & TMP-SMX Avoidance in First Trimester: ACOG guidelines discourage nitrofurantoin and TMP-SMX during the first trimester of pregnancy due to concerns regarding potential birth defect risks, and nitrofurantoin is avoided at term due to neonatal hemolysis risk!
