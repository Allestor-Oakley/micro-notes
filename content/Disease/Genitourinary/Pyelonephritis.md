# Pyelonephritis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Pyelonephritis is an invasive, potentially life-threatening inflammatory bacterial or fungal infection involving the renal parenchyma, renal pelvis, and calyces. Clinically, it is defined by systemic illness and upper urinary tract signs and symptoms including fever, rigors/chills, flank pain or costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness, accompanied by pyuria and quantitative bacteriuria (typically >=10^4 to 10^5 CFU/mL of a uropathogen).
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Acute Uncomplicated Pyelonephritis: Upper urinary tract infection occurring in a healthy, nonpregnant, premenopausal, immunocompetent adult female with a structurally, functionally, and neurologically normal urinary tract.
  - Complicated Pyelonephritis: Infection occurring in individuals with structural or functional urinary tract abnormalities, obstructive uropathy (urolithiasis, strictures, prostatic hypertrophy), indwelling urinary catheters or drainage stents, neurogenic bladder, renal transplantation, diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, male sex, pregnancy, or infection caused by multidrug-resistant uropathogens.
  - Chronic Pyelonephritis: Chronic, tubulointerstitial renal scarring, atrophy, and calyceal deformity resulting from recurrent or persistent renal parenchymal infection, historically associated with vesicoureteral reflux (reflux nephropathy) or chronic urinary obstruction.
  - Special Pathologic & Clinical Forms:
    - Emphysematous Pyelonephritis (EPN): A severe, life-threatening, necrotizing renal parenchymal infection characterized by intrarenal, perirenal, or collecting system gas formation; occurs predominantly in patients with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus (>90% of cases) or urinary tract obstruction.
    - Xanthogranulomatous Pyelonephritis (XGP): A chronic inflammatory destructive pseudotumorous process resulting from chronic renal obstruction and infection (often associated with staghorn calculi), characterized by lipid-laden foamy macrophages (xanthoma cells) replacing renal tissue.
    - Renal & Perinephric Abscess: Suppurative liquefactive necrosis confined within the renal parenchyma (renal abscess / carbuncle) or extending into the Gerota fascia and perinephric space.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Disease Burden & Demographics: In the United States, acute pyelonephritis accounts for approximately 250,000 office visits and over 200,000 hospitalizations annually. Females are affected 4 to 5 times more frequently than males in young adult populations (highest incidence in sexually active women aged 15 to 34 years), whereas the sex ratio equalizes in older adults >65 years due to rising prostatic obstruction and instrumentation in men.
  - Anatomic & Behavioral Risk Factors: Frequency of sexual intercourse ("honeymoon pyelonephritis"); a new sexual partner within the past year; spermicide and diaphragm use; history of urinary tract infection in first-degree female relatives; maternal history of UTIs; personal history of recurrent cystitis or pyelonephritis; vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) in infants and young children; delayed voiding; and urinary incontinence.
  - Pregnancy: Untreated or inadequately treated asymptomatic bacteriuria (ABU) in pregnancy progresses to acute pyelonephritis in 20% to 40% of cases due to gestational progesterone-induced ureteral smooth muscle relaxation, ureteral dilation, hypoperistalsis, and mechanical compression by the gravid uterus (occurring most frequently in the late second and third trimesters, with 80% of cases on the right side).
  - Host Comorbidities & Structural Factors: Diabetes mellitus (increases risk 4-fold to 5-fold, predisposing to papillary necrosis, emphysematous pyelonephritis, and fungal pyelonephritis); urinary tract obstruction (nephrolithiasis, ureteral stricture, prostatic hyperplasia, pelvic tumors); neurogenic bladder (spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis); indwelling urinary catheters and ureteral stents; renal transplantation; autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD); and immunosuppression (chemotherapy, biological agents, systemic steroids).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Escherichia coli (Uropathogenic E. coli / UPEC): The predominant causative organism, accounting for 80% to 90% of acute uncomplicated pyelonephritis cases in outpatients and 40% to 60% of complicated or healthcare-associated pyelonephritis cases.
  - Klebsiella pneumoniae: Second most common gram-negative bacillus, accounting for 5% to 15% of cases.
  - Proteus mirabilis: Accounts for 3% to 10% of cases (strongly associated with urea-splitting struvite staghorn calculi).
  - Enterobacter species, Citrobacter species, Serratia marcescens, and Morganella morganii.
  - Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium: Important causes in complicated, catheterized, post-procedural, or elderly patients.
  - Pseudomonas aeruginosa: Major pathogen in complicated, hospitalized, catheterized, or post-urologic procedure pyelonephritis.
  - Staphylococcus saprophyticus and Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus / GBS; in pregnant women and diabetics).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Infants & Young Children (<5 years): E. coli (75%–80%), Proteus mirabilis (more common in uncircumcised boys), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterococcus faecalis, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (in congenital urologic anomalies).
  - Young Sexually Active Premenopausal Females (15–45 years): Uropathogenic E. coli (85%–90%), Staphylococcus saprophyticus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis.
  - Older Adults (>65 years) & Complicated Hosts: E. coli (frequency decreases to ~40%–50%), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, Enterobacter cloacae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Enterococcus faecalis, Enterococcus faecium, and multidrug-resistant (MDR) uropathogens (extended-spectrum beta-lactamase / ESBL producers such as E. coli ST131, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae / CRE).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Hematogenous / Metastatic Pathogens (causing pyelonephritis or renal abscesses via bacteremic seeding rather than ascending route): Staphylococcus aureus (causes renal cortical abscesses / carbuncles secondary to staphylococcal bacteremia or endocarditis), Salmonella enterica serovars, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida species.
  - Mycobacteria & Fastidious Organisms: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (renal tuberculosis causing sterile pyuria, infundibular strictures, and "putty kidney"); Corynebacterium urealyticum (urea-splitting, gram-positive rod causing encrusted pyelitis and struvite stones in transplant or catheterized hosts); Actinobaculum schaalii.
  - Fungi: Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, Candida tropicalis, Candida krusei (fungal pyelonephritis and fungus balls / bezoars in diabetics, obstructed tracts, or ICU patients); Cryptococcus neoformans; Histoplasma capsulatum; Aspergillus species.
  - Viruses: Cytomegalovirus (CMV; in renal transplant allografts); Adenovirus (types 11 and 35; causing hemorrhagic pyelitis/cystitis in allogeneic HSCT and renal transplants); BK Polyomavirus (BK virus nephropathy and pyelitis in renal transplant recipients).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Ascending Route (Primary Mechanism >95% of Cases): Microorganisms originating from the gut fecal reservoir colonize the perineum, vaginal introitus, and periurethral mucosa. Bacteria ascend through the urethra into the bladder. In susceptible hosts or in the presence of specific bacterial adhesins, bacteria overcome normal bladder flushing and ascend retrogradely against peristalsis up the ureter into the renal pelvis and parenchymal collecting tubules. Ureteral reflux (vesicoureteral reflux / VUR) and impaired ureteral peristalsis (mediated by bacterial endotoxin / LPS) facilitate rapid renal colonization.
  - Uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) Virulence Factors:
    - P Fimbriae / Pyelonephritis-Associated Pili (pap gene cluster, PapG adhesin): Essential virulence determinant for upper tract invasion. The PapG tip adhesin specifically binds to alpha-D-galactopyranosyl-(1->4)-beta-D-galactopyranoside (Gal-Gal) receptor moieties present on glycolipids of human renal collecting duct and tubule epithelial cells, anchoring bacteria to the renal parenchyma.
    - Type 1 Fimbriae (fimH): Mediate bladder colonization and initial ascension.
    - Dr / Afa Adhesins: Bind decay-accelerating factor (DAF) on renal interstitial cells.
    - Toxins & Iron Acquisition Systems: Alpha-hemolysin (hlyA; pore-forming toxin that lyses renal tubular cells, damages cell membranes, and suppresses local cytokine release); Cytotoxic Necrotizing Factor 1 (cnf1); Aerobactin, Enterobactin, Yersiniabactin, and Salmochelin siderophore systems that scavenge ferric iron in iron-limited urine and renal tissue.
  - Hematogenous Route (<5% of Cases): Direct blood-borne seeding of the renal cortex during systemic bacteremia or fungemia (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia, Candida albicans fungemia, Salmonella, or Mycobacterium tuberculosis).
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Suppurative Tubulointerstitial Inflammation: Bacterial attachment to renal tubular epithelial cells triggers an intense innate inflammatory response mediated by Toll-like Receptor 4 (TLR4) and TLR2, inducing massive urothelial and tubular secretion of CXCL8 (IL-8), IL-6, and IL-1beta.
  - Neutrophilic Infiltration & Microabscess Formation: Massive recruitment of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (neutrophils) into the interstitium and collecting tubules produces intratubular neutrophilic casts (white blood cell casts) and suppurative interstitial microabscesses.
  - Ischemic & Suppurative Parenchymal Destruction: Neutrophilic degranulation releases reactive oxygen species, lysozyme, and matrix metalloproteinases, destroying renal tubular cells. Luminal obstruction of tubules and capillary microvascular thrombosis in the renal medulla induce regional ischemia, acute tubular necrosis, and wedge-shaped zones of inflammation extending from the papilla to the renal cortex.
  - Papillary Necrosis: Severe necrotizing ischemic destruction of the renal papillae; occurs predominantly in patients with diabetes mellitus, sickle cell disease or trait, analgesic nephropathy, or severe urinary obstruction.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Systemic & Upper Urinary Tract Symptom Complex: Abrupt onset of high fever (>=38.5°C / 101°F), shaking chills / rigors, flank pain or back pain (typically unilateral, localized over the costovertebral angle, but can be bilateral), nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and constitutional malaise.
  - Concurrent or Antecedent Lower Tract Symptoms: Dysuria, frequency, urgency, and suprapubic discomfort (present in 60% to 70% of cases, though lower tract symptoms may be absent in up to 30% of patients, particularly the elderly or catheterized).
  - Atypical Presentation in Special Populations: Elderly patients may present without classic fever or flank pain, exhibiting instead acute delirium / altered mental status, lethargy, failure to thrive, gastrointestinal distress, or hypothermia and septic shock. Diabetics may present with emphysematous pyelonephritis or severe sepsis. Infants present with fever, poor feeding, irritability, and failure to thrive.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Costovertebral Angle (CVA) Tenderness (Flank Tenderness): Marked, exquisite pain elicited by gentle fist percussion over the costovertebral angle on the affected side (the hallmark physical sign of pyelonephritis).
  - Vital Signs & Systemic Signs: Fever (temperature often 38.5°C to 40°C), tachycardia, tachypnea, and hypotension / septic shock in severe cases.
  - Abdominal Examination: Ipsilateral flank or upper quadrant tenderness; abdominal distention or decreased bowel sounds (ileus secondary to retroperitoneal inflammation); absence of peritoneal signs (unless complicated by perinephric abscess rupture or perforation).
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - White Blood Cell (WBC) Casts in Urine Sediment: Detection of white blood cell casts on urine microscopy pathognomonic for renal parenchymal inflammation / acute pyelonephritis.
  - Flank Pain + CVA Tenderness + High Fever + Pyuria: Classic, highly predictive clinical quadra for acute pyelonephritis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis (typically 12,000 to 20,000/mm3 or higher) with a left shift (bandemia >10%). Severe leukopenia (<4,000/mm3) or marked bandemia (>20%) indicates severe urosepsis and poor host response.
  - Systemic Inflammatory Biomarkers: Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and markedly elevated serum procalcitonin (PCT >0.5 to 2.0 ng/mL; PCT levels >2.0 ng/mL strongly correlate with acute parenchymal pyelonephritis and bacteremia).
  - Serum Chemistry & Renal Function: Elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine indicate acute kidney injury (AKI) or underlying structural obstruction. Serum electrolytes demonstrate dehydration or acidosis.
  - Urinalysis (Dipstick & Microscopy):
    - Leukocyte Esterase & Nitrite: Positive leukocyte esterase (>90% sensitive); positive nitrite in Enterobacteriaceae infections.
    - Urine Microscopy: Pyuria (>=10 WBCs/mm3 in uncentrifuged urine or >=5-10 WBCs/HPF); White Blood Cell (WBC) Casts (diagnostic of renal parenchymal inflammation); microscopic or gross hematuria; mild proteinuria (<1 to 2 g/24 hours).
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Indications for Urgent Renal Imaging (CT / Ultrasound): Routine imaging is NOT required for uncomplicated, non-hospitalized young women with prompt clinical response to antibiotics (<48-72 hours). Urgent imaging (within 24 hours) IS MANDATORY for: (1) Patients remaining febrile or clinically deteriorating after 48 to 72 hours of appropriate antimicrobial therapy; (2) Severely ill patients with septic shock; (3) Known or suspected urinary tract obstruction or urolithiasis; (4) Diabetics (to rule out emphysematous pyelonephritis or papillary necrosis); (5) Immunocompromised hosts; (6) History of renal transplantation, solitary kidney, or prior urologic surgery; (7) Recurrent pyelonephritis episodes.
  - Contrast-Enhanced Computed Tomography (CT) of Abdomen/Pelvis (Diagnostic Imaging Modality of Choice):
    - Acute Pyelonephritis Findings: Striated or wedge-shaped zones of decreased renal parenchymal enhancement (perfusion defects) extending from the papilla to the renal cortex on post-contrast nephrogram phase; delayed excretion into the collecting system; renal enlargement; perinephric fat stranding; and thickening of the Gerota fascia.
    - Complications Detected: Renal abscess (hypodense fluid collection with peripheral ring enhancement); Emphysematous Pyelonephritis (gas within parenchyma or collecting system); Xanthogranulomatous Pyelonephritis (delayed/absent excretion, "bear paw sign" of expanded calyces surrounding a central staghorn calculus); Ureteral obstruction / hydronephrosis; Papillary necrosis (ring shadows or sloughed papillae defects).
  - Renal Ultrasonography: Safe alternative in pregnancy or severe renal failure (avoids iodinated contrast and radiation). Demonstrates renal enlargement, hydronephrosis/obstruction, perinephric fluid collections, or large renal abscesses, but less sensitive than CT for detecting focal pyelonephritis or small microabscesses.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Urine Culture & Susceptibility (MANDATORY IN ALL CASES): Quantitative urine culture MUST be obtained in ALL cases of suspected pyelonephritis prior to initiating antimicrobial therapy!
    - Diagnostic Threshold: Quantitative growth of >=10^4 to 10^5 CFU/mL of a single uropathogen in midstream clean-catch urine (or >=10^2 CFU/mL in catheterized specimens or any growth on suprapubic aspirate) in the presence of upper tract symptoms defines pyelonephritis.
  - Blood Cultures (MANDATORY IN ALL HOSPITALIZED / SEVERE CASES): Two sets of blood cultures should be drawn prior to antibiotics in all hospitalized patients or those presenting with systemic toxicity. Positive in 15% to 30% of uncomplicated acute pyelonephritis cases (bacteremic pyelonephritis); concordance between blood and urine cultures approaches 95%.
  - Gram Stain of Uncentrifuged Urine: Rapid identification of uropathogen morphology (gram-negative rods vs. gram-positive cocci) guides empiric selection.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Quantitative Urine Culture demonstrating >=10^4 to 10^5 CFU/mL of a uropathogen (or positive concordant blood cultures) in a patient with classic upper tract symptoms (fever, flank pain, CVA tenderness) and pyuria/WBC casts represents the clinical gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Decision on Outpatient vs. Inpatient Admission:
    - Outpatient Management: Suitable for mild-to-moderate uncomplicated pyelonephritis in young, nonpregnant, hemodynamically stable, immunocompetent women who can tolerate oral fluids and medications, have reliable home support, and can follow up in 24 to 48 hours.
    - Inpatient Hospital Admission Indications: Severe toxicity, fever >39°C, hemodynamic instability / septic shock, intractable nausea/vomiting (inability to maintain oral intake/medications), metabolic acidosis, acute kidney injury, pregnancy, severe diabetes, immunocompromised state, social issues, or failure of outpatient oral therapy after 48 to 72 hours.
  - Resuscitation & Hemodynamic Support: Aggressive IV fluid resuscitation with balanced crystalloids (Lactated Ringer's or normal saline) to correct dehydration, maintain renal perfusion, and treat sepsis; antipyretics (acetaminophen) for fever control; antiemetics for nausea.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Outpatient Oral Regimens for Uncomplicated Pyelonephritis (IDSA Guidelines):
    1. Oral Fluoroquinolones (First-Line if local resistance <10%): Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO twice daily for 7 days OR Ciprofloxacin ER 1000 mg PO daily for 7 days OR Levofloxacin 750 mg PO daily for 5 days.
    2. Alternative Initial Parenteral Consolidation Dose (if local fluoroquinolone resistance >10% or unknown): Administer a single initial IV dose of Ceftriaxone 1 g IV OR a 24-hour dose of an Aminoglycoside (Gentamicin 5 to 7 mg/kg IV or Tobramycin) BEFORE initiating oral fluoroquinolone, TMP-SMX, or oral beta-lactam therapy.
    3. Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX): 160/800 mg (double-strength) PO twice daily for 14 days (ONLY if organism is known to be susceptible or after initial IV ceftriaxone consolidation).
  - Inpatient Parenteral Regimens for Severe / Hospitalized / Complicated Pyelonephritis:
    1. Third-Generation Cephalosporins: Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h OR Cefotaxime 2 g IV q8h.
    2. Antipseudomonal Beta-Lactams (for complicated, catheterized, healthcare-associated, or ICU cases): Cefepime 2 g IV q8-12h OR Piperacillin-Tazobactam 3.375 to 4.5 g IV q6h OR Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h.
    3. Carbapenems (for high risk of ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae or severe uroseptic shock): Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Imipenem-Cilastatin 500 mg IV q6h OR Ertapenem 1 g IV q24h.
    4. Aminoglycosides: Gentamicin or Tobramycin 5 to 7 mg/kg IV q24h (once-daily dosing; excellent renal parenchymal tissue concentrations).
    5. Plazomicin: 15 mg/kg IV q24h x 7 to 14 days (next-generation aminoglycoside indicated for complicated UTIs including pyelonephritis caused by MDR Enterobacteriaceae).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Oral Beta-Lactam Options (Inferior efficacy, require 10-14 days duration + initial IV ceftriaxone dose):
    - Cefpodoxime proxetil 200 mg PO bid x 10 to 14 days OR Ceftibuten 400 mg PO daily x 10 to 14 days OR Amoxicillin-Clavulanate 875/125 mg PO bid x 14 days. (Note: Nitrofurantoin, Fosfomycin, and Oral Pivmecillinam DO NOT achieve adequate renal parenchymal tissue concentrations and are STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED for pyelonephritis!).
  - Pathogen-Directed Targeted Regimens:
    - Enterococcal Pyelonephritis: Ampicillin 2 g IV q4h PLUS Gentamicin OR Amoxicillin 800-1000 mg PO tid x 14 days (Vancomycin or Linezolid for VRE).
    - Pseudomonas aeruginosa: Cefepime 2 g IV q8h OR Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h OR Piperacillin-Tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h.
    - Candida Pyelonephritis: Fluconazole 200 to 400 mg PO/IV daily for 14 days (Echinocandins do not achieve adequate active urinary concentrations and are NOT recommended for lower tract or renal parenchymal fungal infections, except when renal tissue invasion occurs without bladder excretion dependence).
  - Regimens for Acute Pyelonephritis in Pregnancy:
    - Inpatient admission MANDATORY. Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h OR Ampicillin 2 g IV q6h PLUS Gentamicin 1.5 mg/kg IV q8h. Step-down to oral cephalosporins or amoxicillin-clavulanate to complete 14 days. Daily suppressive prophylaxis (nitrofurantoin 50-100 mg PO at bedtime) continued through delivery.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Emergency Decompression of Obstructed Kidneys: Immediate urologic intervention with percutaneous nephrostomy tube placement or retrograde ureteral stent insertion is MANDATORY for infected hydronephrosis / pyonephrosis secondary to obstructing ureteral calculi or strictures (medical therapy alone fails and leads to fatal urosepsis without drainage!).
  - Drainage of Renal & Perinephric Abscesses: Image-guided (CT or ultrasound) percutaneous catheter drainage or needle aspiration for renal abscesses >=3 cm or perinephric abscesses. Surgical open drainage indicated for multiloculated or refractory abscesses.
  - Emergency Nephrectomy: Emergency life-saving nephrectomy indicated for severe, extensive Emphysematous Pyelonephritis (EPN class 3/4) failing medical/percutaneous management, or for advanced Xanthogranulomatous Pyelonephritis (XGP) with a non-functioning kidney.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Urosepsis and Septic Shock (Gram-negative bacteremic shock carrying 20% to 40% mortality).
  - Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) and Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN).
  - Suppurative Complications: Intrarenal abscess (renal carbuncle), Perinephric abscess (extension into Gerota fascia), Pyonephrosis (pus-filled obstructed collecting system).
  - Necrotizing Complications: Renal Papillary Necrosis (sloughing of papillae causing acute ureteral colic) and Emphysematous Pyelonephritis (intrarenal gas formation with high mortality up to 20%–50%).
  - Obstetric Complications: Maternal sepsis, adult respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), preterm labor, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), and low birth weight.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Chronic Renal Parenchymal Scarring (focal cortical scars over blunted calyces, occurring in up to 10%–20% of children and adults following acute pyelonephritis).
  - Secondary Hypertension (renovascular/parenchymal hypertension developing secondary to renal scarring).
  - Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) and End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) (especially in children with vesicoureteral reflux / reflux nephropathy or recurrent pyelonephritis).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - WBC Casts: Diagnostic urinary microscopy finding pathognomonic for acute renal parenchymal pyelonephritis.
  - Costovertebral Angle (CVA) Tenderness: Exquisite flank pain elicited by fist percussion over the 12th rib/spine junction.
  - PapG / P Fimbriae: Gal-Gal binding tip adhesin of UPEC, essential for upper tract tissue invasion.
  - Bear Paw Sign: Pathognomonic CT appearance of expanded, fluid-filled calyces surrounding a central staghorn calculus in Xanthogranulomatous Pyelonephritis.
  - Striated Nephrogram: Characteristic CT finding of alternating hyperdense and hypodense parenchymal bands in acute pyelonephritis.
  - Emphysematous Pyelonephritis: Life-threatening necrotizing gas-forming renal infection in uncontrolled diabetics.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Strict Contraindication of Nitrofurantoin & Fosfomycin: Nitrofurantoin, Fosfomycin, and Pivmecillinam achieve high urinary concentration but POOR RENAL PARENCHYMAL TISSUE CONCENTRATIONS and are STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED for the treatment of pyelonephritis!
  - Absence of Lower Tract Symptoms: Up to 30% of patients with true acute pyelonephritis (especially elderly, catheterized, or diabetic hosts) have NO lower urinary tract symptoms (no dysuria, frequency, or urgency)!
  - Staphylococcal Hematogenous Seeding: Isolation of Staphylococcus aureus in urine culture indicates hematogenous bacteremic seeding to the kidney (renal abscess) rather than an ascending urinary tract infection, requiring immediate blood cultures and echocardiogram!
  - Mandated Urine Culture Rule: Unlike uncomplicated cystitis (where culture is optional), a urine culture and susceptibility test MUST BE OBTAINED IN 100% OF PYELONEPHRITIS CASES!
  - Echinocandin Ineffectiveness in Fungal Pyelonephritis: Echinocandins (caspofungin, micafungin) do not achieve active concentrations in urine and are NOT recommended for lower or upper urinary tract fungal infections (oral fluconazole is the drug of choice for Candida pyelonephritis)!
  - Single-Dose IV Ceftriaxone Consolidation: When local fluoroquinolone resistance exceeds 10%, an initial single IV dose of 1 g ceftriaxone or an aminoglycoside MUST be given prior to starting oral fluoroquinolones!
