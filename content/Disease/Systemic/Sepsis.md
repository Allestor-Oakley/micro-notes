# Sepsis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Sepsis is defined by Sepsis-3 (Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock, 2016) as a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to an infection. Organ dysfunction is clinically represented by an acute increase in the Sequential (Sepsis-related) Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of 2 points or more attributable to infection (presuming baseline SOFA score is zero unless preexisting organ dysfunction is known). Septic shock is defined as a subset of sepsis in which underlying circulatory, cellular, and metabolic abnormalities are profound enough to substantially increase mortality; clinically identified by a persistent vasopressor requirement to maintain a mean arterial pressure (MAP) of 65 mm Hg or greater AND a serum lactate level greater than 2 mmol/L (18 mg/dL) despite adequate volume resuscitation. (Historically, Sepsis-1 and Sepsis-2 defined sepsis as Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) resulting from infection, severe sepsis as sepsis with organ dysfunction, and septic shock as sepsis-induced hypotension refractory to fluid resuscitation; Sepsis-3 eliminated the terms SIRS and severe sepsis).
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Clinical Severity / Continuum (Sepsis-3 Framework): (1) Uncomplicated Localized Infection (infection without acute host organ dysfunction); (2) Sepsis (infection with acute life-threatening organ dysfunction, SOFA change >=2 points); (3) Septic Shock (sepsis with persistent vasopressor-dependent hypotension MAP >=65 mm Hg and hyperlactatemia >2 mmol/L).
  - Bedside Screening Tool (qSOFA): Quick SOFA (qSOFA) incorporates three bedside criteria: respiratory rate >=22 breaths/min, altered mentation (Glasgow Coma Scale <15), and systolic blood pressure <=100 mm Hg. Presence of >=2 qSOFA criteria rapidly identifies patients with suspected infection at higher risk for poor outcomes or ICU stay, though qSOFA is a prognostic screening tool rather than a formal definition of sepsis.
  - Acquisition Setting / Subtypes: (1) Community-Acquired Sepsis (frequently stemming from community-acquired pneumonia, pyelonephritis, intra-abdominal infection); (2) Healthcare-Associated / Nosocomial / ICU-Acquired Sepsis (stemming from ventilator-associated pneumonia, catheter-related bloodstream infections, surgical site infections, or multi-drug resistant hospital flora); (3) Neutropenic / Immunocompromised Sepsis (high-risk rapid deterioration requiring immediate broad-spectrum antipseudomonal empiric therapy); (4) Special Subclasses of Fever/Sepsis (Classic, Health Care-Associated, Immune-Deficient, and HIV-Related).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Epidemiology: Globally estimated at ~31.5 million episodes of hospital-treated sepsis annually, accounting for >5.3 million deaths per year. Sepsis represents ~6% of adult hospitalizations and 10% to 37% of ICU admissions in high-income nations. Cost of treating sepsis in US hospitals exceeded $24 billion annually (making it the single most expensive condition treated in US hospitals). In-hospital mortality for sepsis ranges from 15% to 20%, rising to 22% to 40%+ in septic shock (and up to 65% in patients with 4 or more organ failures).
  - Host & Demographic Risk Factors: Bimodal age distribution peaking in infants and elderly adults (patients >=65 years account for nearly 65% of sepsis cases due to immunosenescence and comorbidity burden); male sex (higher susceptibility in males); Black race (higher sepsis incidence and mortality compared to White patients due to higher rates of infection, acute organ dysfunction, healthcare disparities, and genetic factors); poor socioeconomic status / high community poverty rate; and malnutrition or lack of vaccination.
  - Comorbidities & Clinical Risk Factors: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), active malignancy / cancer (3- to 4-fold increased risk of sepsis, up to 10-fold in hematologic malignancies), diabetes mellitus (predisposes particularly to urinary and skin/soft tissue sepsis), chronic kidney disease / hemodialysis, chronic liver disease / cirrhosis, HIV/AIDS, systemic immunosuppressive or chemotherapy use, duration of hospitalization, presence of indwelling devices (central venous line, urinary catheter, endotracheal tube), and recent major surgery or trauma.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Gram-Positive Bacteria: Account for 40% to 52% of culture-positive sepsis cases in modern ICUs. Leading species: Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA and MRSA; prominent cause of bacteremia, pneumonia, skin/soft tissue sepsis), Streptococcus pneumoniae (leading cause of community-acquired pulmonary sepsis), Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium (VRE), Staphylococcus epidermidis and other coagulase-negative staphylococci (indwelling device infections), and Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus, causing invasive necrotizing fasciitis and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome).
  - Gram-Negative Bacteria: Account for 38% to 62% of positive isolates in ICU surveys (predominant in Eastern Europe, Asia, and Latin America). Leading species: Escherichia coli (most common cause of urinary tract sepsis and intra-abdominal sepsis), Klebsiella pneumoniae (frequently ESBL or carbapenem-resistant), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (major nosocomial and neutropenic pathogen), Acinetobacter baumannii, Enterobacter species, Proteus species, Serratia marcescens, and Burkholderia pseudomallei (cause of melioidosis, a leading cause of community-acquired sepsis in Southeast Asia/Thailand).
  - Fungi: Account for 5% to 19% of sepsis cases. Leading species: Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, Candida parapsilosis, Candida tropicalis, Candida krusei, and Aspergillus species (in severely immunocompromised hosts).
  - Viruses: Account for 1% to 7% of sepsis cases (Influenza A/B, Coronaviruses including SARS-CoV-2, Dengue virus, Ebola/Marburg, Cytomegalovirus, Herpes Simplex Virus, Enteroviruses in neonates).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Neonates & Infants (<3 months): Group B Streptococcus (Streptococcus agalactiae), Escherichia coli, Listeria monocytogenes, Herpes Simplex Virus, Enteroviruses, and Parechoviruses. Early-onset neonatal sepsis is dominated by maternal vaginal flora (GBS, E. coli); late-onset by nosocomial/skin flora (CoNS, S. aureus, Klebsiella, Candida).
  - Children & Adolescents: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis (meningococcemia presenting with petechial/purpuric rash and purpura fulminans), Staphylococcus aureus, Group A Streptococcus, Haemophilus influenzae type b (in unimmunized populations), and Salmonella species (non-typhoidal Salmonella is the most common non-malarial cause of bloodstream infection in Sub-Saharan Africa).
  - Adults & Elderly (>65 years): Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus (high MRSA prevalence), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococcus species, Proteus mirabilis, and Clostridioides difficile (causing toxic megacolon/sepsis).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Zoonotic, Environmental & Fastidious Bacteria: Capnocytophaga canimorsus (fulminant sepsis with purpura fulminans and symmetrical peripheral gangrene following dog/cat bites, especially in asplenic hosts), Vibrio vulnificus (bullous lesions, necrotizing wound infection, and rapid septic shock following raw oyster ingestion or marine water exposure in cirrhotic/hemochromatosis hosts), Bacillus anthracis (inhalational anthrax sepsis with hemorrhagic mediastinitis), Yersinia pestis (plague), Francisella tularensis, Brucella species, Leptospira interrogans, Borrelia recurrentis, Streptobacillus moniliformis (rat-bite fever), Neisseria gonorrhoeae (disseminated gonococcal sepsis), Legionella pneumophila, Rickettsia rickettsii (Rocky Mountain spotted fever), Orientia tsutsugamushi (scrub typhus), and Coxiella burnetii.
  - Opportunistic Fungi & Parasites: Cryptococcus neoformans, Histoplasma capsulatum, Coccidioides immitis, Fusarium species, Trichosporon beigelii, Mucorales (Rhizopus), Prototheca, Plasmodium falciparum (severe falciparum malaria mimicking septic shock), Babesia microti (asplenic hosts), and Strongyloides stercoralis (hyperinfection syndrome with Gram-negative enteric bacteremia/sepsis).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Anatomic Portals of Entry: Sepsis originates from a primary focal infection that overrides local mucosal and innate immune defenses. Most common anatomical sources (in descending order): (1) Respiratory tract (pneumonia/lung infection, accounting for ~45% to 50% of sepsis cases); (2) Intra-abdominal infections (peritonitis, cholangitis, abscesses, ~20%); (3) Bloodstream infections / intravascular catheters (~10% to 12%); (4) Genitourinary tract (pyelonephritis, complicated UTI, ~8% to 10%); (5) Skin and soft tissue infections (~6% to 8%); (6) Central nervous system; and (7) Unknown / unidentifiable primary site (~30% of blood culture-negative cases).
  - Microbial Virulence & Quorum Sensing: Pathogens breach mucosal/epithelial barriers via adhesins, invasins, capsules, and proteases. Bacteria coordinate gene expression and virulence factor production via density-dependent cell-to-cell signaling (quorum sensing), forming protective biofilms on tissue and medical devices. Toxin-producing bacteria secrete exotoxins (e.g., S. aureus TSST-1, S. pyogenes SPE-A/C superantigens that directly cross-link MHC class II to T-cell receptor beta-chains, activating up to 20% of T-lymphocytes) or pore-forming hemolysins.
  - PAMP & DAMP Recognition: Microorganisms release conserved Pathogen-Associated Molecular Patterns (PAMPs)—such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS / endotoxin) from Gram-negative outer membranes, peptidoglycan, lipoteichoic acid from Gram-positive cell walls, lipopeptides, flagellin, and unmethylated CpG bacterial DNA. PAMPs bind germline-encoded Pattern Recognition Receptors (PRRs)—including Toll-like Receptors (TLR4 for LPS, TLR2 for lipoteichoic acid, TLR5 for flagellin, TLR9 for DNA), C-type lectin receptors, and cytosolic NLRs/inflammasomes—expressed on monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells, and endothelial cells. Tissue injury and necrotic cells release endogenous Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns (DAMPs / alarmins)—such as High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1), heat shock proteins, extracellular S100A8/S100A9 (calprotectin), nuclear histones, and cell-free DNA—creating a self-amplifying loop of inflammatory activation.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Hyperinflammation & Cytokine Cascade: NF-kappaB activation downstream of PRR signaling drives massive transcriptional release of early pro-inflammatory cytokines: Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-alpha), Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1beta), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), Interleukin-12, Interleukin-18, and High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1). Cytokines induce fever, activate complement, induce endothelial adhesion molecules (E-selectin, ICAM-1, VCAM-1), recruit neutrophils, and trigger systemic microvascular leak.
  - Endothelial Dysfunction & Microvascular Leak: Disruption of the endothelial glycocalyx (the protective luminal glycoprotein-polysaccharide layer) and degradation of adherens junctions (VE-cadherin, occludins) lead to profound vascular permeability, extravasation of protein-rich fluid into interstitial spaces, systemic edema, severe intravascular hypovolemia, and loss of vascular tone (refractory vasodilation mediated by inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and overproduction of NO).
  - Coagulopathy & Immunothrombosis: Sepsis triggers a severe procoagulant state. Monocytes and activated endothelial cells express surface Tissue Factor (TF), initiating the extrinsic coagulation cascade (Factor VIIa -> Factor Xa -> Thrombin -> Fibrin deposition). Simultaneously, natural anticoagulant pathways are severely blunted: antithrombin and TFPI are depleted and inactivated by broken glycocalyx, and the Protein C pathway is impaired (loss of endothelial thrombomodulin and EPCR). Fibrinolysis is shutdown by high circulating levels of Plasminogen Activator Inhibitor-1 (PAI-1). Neutrophils release Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs—composed of DNA, histones, and granular proteases), providing a scaffold for platelet aggregation and microvascular thrombosis. Severe consumption of clotting factors and platelets results in Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC), characterized by widespread microvascular thrombosis (causing organ ischemia) and simultaneous oozing hemorrhage (purpura fulminans).
  - Sepsis-Induced Immunosuppression / Immunoparalysis: Concurrently with hyperinflammation, compensatory anti-inflammatory mechanisms induce profound immune dysfunction: apoptotic depletion of CD4+ and CD8+ T-lymphocytes, B-cells, and dendritic cells; monocyte reprogramming with downregulated HLA-DR expression (monocyte deactivation/anergy); elevated expression of immune checkpoint molecules (PD-1 on T-cells, PD-L1 on monocytes/endothelium); upregulation of IL-10 and TGF-beta; and expansion of regulatory T-cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells. This state predisposes to secondary opportunistic infections (Stenotrophomonas, Acinetobacter, Enterococcus, Candida) and viral reactivations (CMV, HSV).
  - Cellular Dysoxia & Mitochondrial Injury: Sepsis-induced organ failure is NOT primarily caused by cell death or overt structural necrosis; postmortem tissue shows minimal cell necrosis. Rather, organ dysfunction is driven by functional cellular hibernation, microvascular shunting, and severe mitochondrial injury (mitochondrial dysoxia and ATP depletion caused by reactive oxygen/nitrogen species), leading to impaired oxidative phosphorylation and cellular metabolic shutdown.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Systemic Manifestations: Acute onset of high fever with shaking chills and rigors, or alternatively hypothermia (spontaneous hypothermia <36.0°C occurs in 9% to 35% of septic patients and is associated with persistent lymphopenia and higher mortality); profound fatigue, weakness, malaise, nausea, vomiting, and diaphoresis.
  - Site-Specific Symptoms: Cough, purulent sputum, pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea (pulmonary source); abdominal pain, distention, diarrhea, jaundice (intra-abdominal/biliary source); dysuria, flank pain, costovertebral tenderness (urinary source); severe localized pain, swelling, erythema, or bullae (skin/soft tissue source).
  - Neurological & Cerebral Symptoms: Acute confusion, lethargy, disorientation, agitation, stupor, or delirium (sepsis-associated encephalopathy, occurring early in >50% of cases).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Hemodynamic & Vital Sign Abnormalities: Tachycardia (heart rate >90 beats/min), tachypnea (respiratory rate >=22 breaths/min), fever (core temp >=38.0°C) or hypothermia (<36.0°C), hypotension (systolic BP <=100 mm Hg or MAP <65 mm Hg), and narrow pulse pressure.
  - Cutaneous & Perfusion Signs: Cool, clammy, mottled extremities with delayed capillary refill (>3 seconds), or early "warm shock" with bounding pulses and flushed extremities due to peripheral vasodilation; acrocyanosis; petechial or purpuric skin eruptions, ecchymoses, or diffuse erythroderma; purpura fulminans (extensive non-blanching purpura with tissue necrosis); ecthyma gangrenosum (punched-out indurated ulcers with central black necrotic eschars and surrounding erythematous halos, characteristic of Pseudomonas aeruginosa sepsis); and symmetrical peripheral gangrene (ischemic necrosis involving distal fingers, toes, lips, or nose without large artery occlusion).
  - Organ Dysfunction Signs: Acute oliguria (urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h); jaundice and scleral icterus (cholestatic liver injury); abdominal distention and absent bowel sounds (septic ileus); and diffuse pulmonary rales/crackles (acute lung injury / ARDS).
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Ecthyma Gangrenosum: Painless or painful erythematous macules/vesicles evolving rapidly into central black eschars with surrounding erythematous halos, highly characteristic of Pseudomonas aeruginosa bacteremic sepsis.
  - Purpura Fulminans with Acrocyanosis: Widespread non-blanching purpuric skin necrosis paired with symmetrical acrocyanosis pathognomonic for severe DIC and septic shock (classic in Neisseria meningitidis and Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis).
  - Symmetrical Peripheral Gangrene: Simultaneous ischemic dry gangrene of distal extremities (digits of hands and feet) in the absence of major vascular occlusion, pathognomonic for severe septic shock, DIC, and vasopressor-induced microvascular thrombosis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis (WBC >12,000/mm3), leukopenia (WBC <4,000/mm3), or normal WBC count with >10% immature band forms (left shift); severe bandemia (>20%) strongly correlates with bacteremia. Thrombocytopenia (platelet count <100,000/mm3) occurs in up to 50% of septic patients and correlates with disease severity and DIC. Normocytic anemia due to hemolysis or microvascular consumption.
  - Inflammatory & Host Biomarkers: Serum Procalcitonin (PCT) is markedly elevated (>0.5 ng/mL, often >2 to 10+ ng/mL in bacterial sepsis); procalcitonin has higher sensitivity (0.77) and specificity (0.79) than C-reactive protein (CRP) for bacterial infection and is uniquely valuable to guide antibiotic discontinuation/stewardship. Serum CRP is elevated (>2 SD above normal). Novel biomarkers include monocyte HLA-DR expression (flow cytometry demonstrating decreased monocyte HLA-DR expression indicates monocyte anergy/immunosuppression) and transcriptomic gene expression signatures (e.g., SeptiCyte, blood leukocyte RNA signatures discriminating bacterial from viral sepsis).
  - Metabolic & Perfusional Indicators: Serum Lactate is elevated (>1 mmol/L; lactate >2 mmol/L is required for septic shock definition; lactate >4 mmol/L indicates severe tissue hypoperfusion). Arterial blood gas (ABG) shows metabolic acidosis (low pH, low HCO3-) with compensatory respiratory alkalosis (low PaCO2), elevated anion gap, and hypoxemia (PaO2/FiO2 ratio <300 in ARDS). Serum Glucose: Hyperglycemia (>140 mg/dL or 7.7 mmol/L) in non-diabetic patients due to stress-induced gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance.
  - Organ Dysfunction Panel: Serum Creatinine elevated (>1.2 to 2.0+ mg/dL or acute fold increase reflecting AKI); BUN elevated; Total Bilirubin elevated (>2.0 to 4.0+ mg/dL or 70 umol/L, reflecting cholestatic liver injury); Alkaline phosphatase and transaminases (ALT/AST) elevated; Serum Albumin decreased (<3.0 g/dL due to vascular leak and altered hepatic synthesis). Coagulation parameters: Prolonged Prothrombin Time (PT / INR >1.5) and Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT >60 seconds), low Fibrinogen, and markedly elevated D-dimer / Fibrin Degradation Products (FDPs) in DIC.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Chest Radiography (Plain Film): Initial test for pulmonary source; demonstrates lobar consolidation, patchy interstitial/alveolar opacities, or bilateral diffuse infiltrates characteristic of Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) with normal cardiac silhouette size.
  - Computed Tomography (CT): Modality of choice for occult sepsis sources. Chest CT evaluates complicated pneumonia, lung abscess, or empyema. Abdominal/Pelvic CT with contrast detects intra-abdominal abscesses, peritonitis, appendicitis, diverticulitis, ischemic bowel, cholangitis, or pyelonephritis. Head CT evaluates central nervous system focus or septic embolic strokes.
  - Ultrasonography: Rapid bedside test (Point-of-Care Ultrasound / POCUS) evaluating gallbladder (acute cholecystitis), renal collecting system (hydronephrosis/pyonephrosis), deep vein thrombosis, cardiac function/ejection fraction, hyperdynamic vs. septic cardiomyopathy, IVC collapse/fluid responsiveness, and lung sliding/B-lines.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Blood Cultures (Essential Requirement): At least two sets of blood cultures (both aerobic and anaerobic bottles per set, drawn from separate venipuncture sites) MUST be obtained prior to initiating antimicrobial therapy (provided culture collection does not delay antibiotic start by >45 minutes). If an indwelling intravascular catheter has been in place >48 hours, at least one blood culture set must be drawn directly from the catheter simultaneously with peripheral blood cultures to calculate Differential Time to Positivity (DTP, catheter culture turning positive >=2 hours earlier than peripheral culture confirms CRBSI).
  - Targeted Site Cultures & Stains: Gram stain, KOH/fungal stain, and aerobic/anaerobic cultures of urine, sputum/endotracheal aspirate, surgical wound exudate, abdominal fluid, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), or skin lesion aspirates/biopsies (e.g., needle aspiration or biopsy of petechial/purpuric skin lesions or ecthyma gangrenosum).
  - Rapid Culture-Independent Molecular Diagnostics: Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization Time-of-Flight (MALDI-TOF) Mass Spectrometry on positive blood culture broth allows rapid organism identification within hours. Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) multiplex panels (e.g., BioFire FilmArray blood culture identification panel) detect common bacterial/fungal species and resistance genes (meCA, vanA/B, KPC, NDM) directly from blood.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** There is NO single gold standard diagnostic test for sepsis. Sepsis is a complex clinical syndrome diagnosed by combining clinical findings of acute life-threatening organ failure (SOFA score increase >=2 points) caused by a documented or strongly suspected infection, backed by microbiological cultures or molecular pathogen identification.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) 1-Hour / 3-Hour Resuscitation Bundle:
    1. Measure serum lactate immediately; remeasure within 2 to 4 hours if initial lactate is elevated (>2 mmol/L).
    2. Obtain blood cultures prior to administration of antimicrobials.
    3. Administer broad-spectrum intravenous antimicrobials as soon as possible and WITHIN 1 HOUR of recognition for both sepsis and septic shock.
    4. Rapid Fluid Resuscitation: Begin rapid administration of at least 30 mL/kg of IV crystalloids (balanced crystalloids such as Lactated Ringer's or Plasma-Lyte preferred over 0.9% Normal Saline) for hypotension (MAP <65 mm Hg) or serum lactate >=4 mmol/L, initiated within the first 3 hours.
    5. Vasopressor Therapy: Apply vasopressors during or after fluid resuscitation to maintain a Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) >=65 mm Hg. Norepinephrine is the FIRST-CHOICE vasopressor. If MAP remains inadequate on norepinephrine, add Vasopressin (at a fixed dose of 0.03 units/min) or Epinephrine. Avoid dopamine except in select bradycardic patients.
    6. Inotropic Support: Add Dobutamine (or switch to epinephrine) if myocardial dysfunction / septic cardiomyopathy is present or persistent hypoperfusion occurs despite adequate MAP and intravascular volume.
    7. Hemodynamic Reassessment: Frequently reassess volume status and tissue perfusion using dynamic physiologic parameters (passive leg raise test, stroke volume variation, pulse pressure variation, bedside ultrasound of IVC) rather than relying solely on static CVP targets.
  - Respiratory & Supportive Care: Supplemental oxygen; early endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation for severe acute lung injury / ARDS (using lung-protective ventilation strategy: low tidal volumes (6 mL/kg predicted body weight), plateau pressure <30 cmH2O, and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP)). Glycemic Control: Initiate IV insulin protocol when blood glucose levels exceed 180 mg/dL (targeting glucose 144 to 180 mg/dL). Stress Ulcer Prophylaxis: H2-receptor antagonists or PPIs in patients with risk factors for GI bleeding. VTE Prophylaxis: Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) daily.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Empiric Broad-Spectrum Regimen (Adult Sepsis / Septic Shock): Must cover Gram-positive (including MRSA) and Gram-negative (including Pseudomonas aeruginosa) organisms:
    - Gram-Positive / MRSA Coverage: Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h (targeting AUC/MIC 400-600 or trough 15-20 mcg/mL) OR Daptomycin 6 to 10 mg/kg IV q24h.
    - PLUS Antipseudomonal Beta-Lactam Coverage: Cefepime 2 g IV q8h OR Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h (or Imipenem 500 mg IV q6h).
  - Empiric Combination Therapy for Septic Shock: SSC guidelines suggest initial empirical combination therapy (using at least two antimicrobials of different classes targeting Gram-negative pathogens, e.g., antipseudomonal beta-lactam PLUS ciprofloxacin/levofloxacin OR an aminoglycoside like tobramycin/gentamicin) for initial management of septic shock to ensure spectrum coverage; combination therapy should be de-escalated to monotherapy within 2 to 3 days once sensitivities are known.
  - Site-Specific Empiric Regimens:
    - Community-Acquired Pulmonary Sepsis: Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h PLUS Azithromycin 500 mg IV q24h (or Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h) PLUS Vancomycin if CA-MRSA suspected.
    - Intra-Abdominal Sepsis: Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h + Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h.
    - Urosepsis: Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h OR Cefepime 2 g IV q8h OR Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h.
    - Empiric Antifungal Coverage: Limited to patients at high risk for invasive candidiasis (recurrent intra-abdominal perforations, total parenteral nutrition, broad-spectrum antibiotics >7 days, Candida colonization at multiple sites, neutropenia). Preferred agent: Echinocandin (Micafungin 100 mg IV q24h, Caspofungin 70 mg load then 50 mg IV q24h, or Anidulafungin 200 mg load then 100 mg IV q24h).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Severe Beta-Lactam / Penicillin Allergy Regimens: Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h PLUS Aztreonam 2 g IV q8h PLUS Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q8-12h (or Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h) OR Gentamicin/Tobramycin 5 to 7 mg/kg IV q24h.
  - Adjunctive Glucocorticoid Therapy: Intravenous Hydrocortisone (200 mg/day IV given as 50 mg IV q6h or continuous infusion) is recommended ONLY for patients with septic shock whose blood pressure is refractory to adequate fluid resuscitation and vasopressors (hydrocortisone accelerates shock reversal, though it does not consistently reduce 28-day overall mortality). Do NOT give corticosteroids in sepsis without shock!
  - Targeted Antimicrobial Duration & De-escalation: Total treatment duration of 7 to 10 days is adequate for most serious sepsis infections. Daily assessment for de-escalation; serum procalcitonin levels can be used to support shortening antibiotic duration or discontinuing empiric coverage if infection is ruled out.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Emergency Source Control: Rapid identification and anatomical control of the source of infection is a mandatory cornerstone of sepsis management. Source control interventions MUST be implemented as soon as medically and logistically possible (ideally within 6 to 12 hours of diagnosis).
  - Source Control Modalities: (1) Surgical debridement or amputation of infected/necrotic tissue (necrotizing fasciitis, gas gangrene, infected pancreatic necrosis, decubitus ulcer); (2) Percutaneous or open surgical drainage of abscesses (intra-abdominal, pelvic, liver, subphrenic, lung, psoas abscesses, or empyema); (3) Surgical repair or diversion of perforated abdominal viscera (perforated ulcer, diverticulitis, appendicitis, bowel ischemia); (4) Operative decompression of obstructed urinary or biliary tracts (percutaneous nephrostomy / ureteral stenting for pyonephrosis, ERCP / biliary drainage for ascending cholangitis); (5) Immediate removal of infected foreign bodies, intravascular catheters, central lines, Foley catheters, or orthopedic implants implicated as the infection source.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Septic Shock & Refractory Vasoplegic Cardiovascular Collapse.
  - Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS / Acute Lung Injury) with severe refractory hypoxemia and pulmonary edema (occurring in 30% to 50% of severe sepsis cases).
  - Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) requiring continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) or hemodialysis.
  - Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) with widespread microvascular thrombosis, purpura fulminans, symmetrical peripheral gangrene, and catastrophic bleeding.
  - Sepsis-Associated Encephalopathy (SAE) with acute delirium, confusion, stupor, or coma.
  - Critical Illness Polyneuropathy and Myopathy (CIP/CIM) causing profound flaccid muscle weakness and difficulty weaning from mechanical ventilation.
  - Septic Cardiomyopathy / Acute Myocardial Depression (reversible LV/RV systolic dysfunction mediated by circulating cardiosuppressant cytokines and mitochondrial dysfunction).
  - Sepsis-Induced Gastrointestinal Dysfunction: Septic ileus, acalculous cholecystitis, stress ulceration with GI hemorrhage, and intestinal mucosal barrier breakdown / dysbiosis.
  - Acute Hepatic Dysfunction: Cholestatic jaundice, hyperbilirubinemia, and ischemic hepatitis ("shock liver").
- **Long-Term Sequelae:**
  - Post-Sepsis Syndrome (PSS) and Persistent Inflammation, Immunosuppression, and Catabolism Syndrome (PICS).
  - Chronic Physical Disability and Functional Impairment (persistent restrictive lung defects after ARDS, inability to return to work, reduced exercise capacity).
  - Long-term Cognitive Impairment and Dementia-like executive dysfunction (occurring in >25% to 50% of severe sepsis survivors).
  - High Readmission Rates: 43% of severe sepsis survivors are rehospitalized within 90 days (most commonly for recurrent sepsis, heart failure, pneumonia, COPD exacerbations, or acute kidney injury).
  - Increased Long-term Mortality: Sepsis survivors exhibit elevated all-cause mortality, cardiovascular events (myocardial infarction, stroke in 30% of survivors over 1 year), and end-stage renal disease for years following the acute episode.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Sepsis-3: The 2016 consensus definition redefining sepsis as life-threatening organ dysfunction (SOFA >=2) caused by a dysregulated host response.
  - qSOFA: Bedside screening tool (Respiratory rate >=22, Altered mentation, Systolic BP <=100) identifying patients at high risk for poor outcomes.
  - Ecthyma Gangrenosum: Punched-out necrotic ulcers with central black eschars and red halos pathognomonic for Pseudomonas aeruginosa sepsis.
  - Purpura Fulminans: Non-blanching purpuric skin necrosis caused by microvascular DIC in severe sepsis (classic in meningococcemia).
  - Immunoparalysis / Monocyte Anergy: Sepsis-induced immunosuppression marked by decreased monocyte HLA-DR expression.
  - Immunothrombosis: Physiological innate immune host defense mechanism where platelets and coagulation form microvascular blood clots to trap pathogens, which becomes destructive and generalized in sepsis/DIC.
  - Symmetrical Peripheral Gangrene: Distal ischemic necrosis of digits caused by DIC and microvascular thrombosis during septic shock.
  - Trained Immunity: Epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming of innate immune cells following microbial exposure.
  - Warburg Effect in Sepsis: Metabolic shift in septic monocytes from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Hypothermia Is WORSE Than Fever in Sepsis: Spontaneous hypothermia (<36.0°C) in sepsis is NOT a blunted response but a regulated, evolutionarily conserved hypometabolic response to severe inflammation; clinically, hypothermia correlates with persistent lymphopenia, higher organ failure, and significantly HIGHER mortality than fever!
  - Antipyretic Therapy Does NOT Improve Sepsis Survival: Randomized clinical trials and meta-analyses show that treating fever in septic patients with antipyretics (acetaminophen, ibuprofen, or external cooling) does NOT reduce 28-day mortality or improve clinical outcomes!
  - Normal WBC Count Does NOT Exclude Sepsis: WBC count is completely normal in a substantial percentage of septic patients; presence of >10% immature band forms (left shift) or severe bandemia (>20%) is a far more reliable indicator of serious infection.
  - Early Goal-Directed Therapy (EGDT) Is NO LONGER Superior: Large multicenter randomized trials (ProCESS, ARISE, ProMISe) proved that protocolized EGDT with central venous catheterization (CVP/ScvO2 monitoring) offers NO survival benefit over usual good clinical resuscitation and increases healthcare costs!
  - Drotrecogin Alfa (Recombinant Activated Protein C) Withdrawn: Once heralded as a targeted sepsis breakthrough in 2001 (PROWESS), recombinant human activated protein C was proven INEFFECTIVE in the PROWESS-SHOCK trial (2012) and withdrawn globally from the market due to lack of efficacy and bleeding risks!
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Contraindicated in Sterile Inflammation: Systemic antimicrobial prophylaxis is EXPLICITLY NOT RECOMMENDED for severe inflammatory states of noninfectious origin (e.g., severe acute pancreatitis, sterile burn injury) as it increases drug toxicity and selection of resistant superinfections!
  - Corticosteroid Restraint: High-dose methylprednisolone worsens mortality in sepsis. Hydrocortisone (200 mg/day) is indicated ONLY for vasopressor-refractory septic shock, NOT for sepsis without shock!
