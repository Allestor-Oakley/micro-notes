# Erysipelas

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Erysipelas is a distinctive, rapidly progressive form of superficial cellulitis of the skin characterized by prominent lymphatic involvement. It involves primarily the superficial dermis and dermal lymphatics, presenting as a painful, bright red, edematous, indurated plaque with a characteristic peau d'orange (orange peel) texture and an advancing, raised border that is sharply demarcated from adjacent uninvolved skin. Unlike deeper cellulitis, uncomplicated erysipelas remains confined to the superficial dermis and lymphatics without invading deep subcutaneous tissues.
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Facial Erysipelas: Classically involves the face, commonly extending across the nasal bridge with bilateral involvement of the cheeks in a butterfly distribution (historically predominated, currently accounts for 5% to 20% of cases); often preceded by antecedent streptococcal upper respiratory tract infection.
  2. Lower Extremity Erysipelas: Currently the most frequent anatomical site (accounting for 70% to 80% of cases), typically occurring in regions of chronic dependent edema, venous stasis, or impaired lymphatic drainage.
  3. Bullous Erysipelas: A severe clinical complication occurring in approximately 5% of cases, characterized by intraepidermal flaccid bullae and blister formation over the erythematous lesion. Fluid is initially sterile but may yield S. pyogenes, S. aureus, or MRSA after erosion.
  4. Neonatal Erysipelas: Distinctive presentation in neonates, typically originating from infection of the umbilical stump or maternal vaginal carriage of Group B streptococci.
  5. Relapsing / Recurrent Erysipelas: Recurrent episodes occurring in up to 30% of patients over a 3-year period, predominantly in individuals with persistent lymphedema, venous insufficiency, or fungal intertrigo.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Epidemiology: Erysipelas occurs across the age spectrum but is most common in infants, young children, and older adults. An anatomical shift has occurred over time: formerly the face was the most common site, but currently 70% to 80% of lesions occur on the lower extremities and only 5% to 20% on the face.
  2. Portals of Entry: Disruption of the skin barrier via minor local trauma, abrasions, skin ulcers, psoriatic or eczematous lesions, fungal intertrigo, or tinea pedis, though the skin often remains grossly intact. In neonates, the umbilical stump serves as the entry site.
  3. Systemic & Local Risk Factors: Preexisting subclinical or extensive lymphedema (e.g., post-radical mastectomy, radiation therapy, or pelvic lymph node dissection), venous stasis, chronic dependent edema, obesity, paraparesis, diabetes mellitus, alcohol abuse, nephrotic syndrome, and hypogammaglobulinemia.
  4. Chronic Bacterial Reservoirs: Asymptomatic anal colonization with Group A or G streptococci or vaginal carriage of Group B streptococci serve as persistent reservoirs in individuals with relapsing infection.
  5. Vicious Cycle of Recurrence: Preexisting lymphatic obstruction creates protein-rich interstitial fluid that impairs innate clearance; the inflammatory damage from an episode of erysipelas further damages and obstructs lymphatic channels, predisposing to recurrent infections (30% recurrence rate over 3 years).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes) is the predominant causative organism, responsible for 30% to 50% of bacteremic cases and the vast majority of nonbacteremic cases.
  - Other Beta-Hemolytic Streptococci: Group C streptococci and Group G streptococci are major causative agents, accounting for up to 40% of bacteremic cases.
  - Staphylococcus aureus: Uncommonly or rarely implicated (accounting for roughly 10% of bacteremic cases or isolated in bullous erysipelas, including MRSA strains).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Neonates: Group B streptococci (Streptococcus agalactiae, acquired from maternal vaginal carriage or umbilical stump infection) and Group A streptococci.
  - Infants and Young Children: Group A streptococci.
  - Older Adults: Group A streptococci, Group C streptococci, Group G streptococci, and Group B streptococci (especially in patients with lymphedema secondary to radical pelvic surgery, radiation, or malignancy).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Campylobacter jejuni (causes erysipelas-like skin lesions associated with bacteremia in patients with hypogammaglobulinemia).
  - Gram-negative bacilli (recovered in roughly 10% of bacteremic cases in hospitalized or compromised hosts).
  - Non-infectious or secondary mimics: Familial Mediterranean Fever (FMF, presenting with recurrent non-streptococcal erysipelas-like erythema), Carcinoma Erysipeloides (diffuse inflammatory breast carcinoma mimicking low-grade erysipelas), and Borrelia burgdorferi (erythema chronicum migrans mimicking erysipelas).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Inoculation & Portal: Bacteria enter through minor skin breaks (abrasions, ulcers, tinea pedis, eczematous lesions, umbilical stump) or seed from antecedent upper respiratory tract infections or asymptomatic mucosal reservoirs (anal or vaginal carriage).
  - Tissue Localization: Infection rapidly lodges in the upper dermis and cutaneous lymphatics. Unlike deeper cellulitis, uncomplicated erysipelas remains confined to the superficial dermis and lymphatic network without deeper subcutaneous or muscular involvement.
  - Lymphatic Diffusion: Extracellular streptococcal enzymes promote rapid diffusion along dermal lymphatic channels, producing a characteristic advancing, raised red border.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Inflammatory Response: Intense vascular congestion, endothelial swelling, dermal edema, and marked polymorphonuclear leukocytic infiltration lead to local tissue induration, swelling, warmth, and the characteristic peau d'orange appearance.
  - Intraepidermal Blistering: In severe disease (bullous erysipelas), extensive dermal edema causes intraepidermal cleavage and flaccid bulla formation.
  - Lymphatic Obstruction & Fibrosis: Acute inflammation damages and obstructs lymphatic channels. Repeated episodes lead to permanent lymphatic scarring, worsening lymphedema, and chronic tissue fibrosis (which can progress to elephantiasis nostras verrucosa).

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Sudden, acute onset of localized skin pain, swelling, and rapidly spreading redness.
  - Prominent constitutional symptoms including high fever, chills, rigors, headache, malaise, and systemic toxicity, which frequently accompany or precede the appearance of cutaneous lesions.
  - History of antecedent streptococcal upper respiratory tract infection (present in about one-third of facial erysipelas cases).
  - History of predisposing chronic leg edema, lymphedema, venous insufficiency, prior surgical lymph node dissection, or previous bouts of erysipelas.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Bright red, highly erythematous, warm, edematous, and indurated plaque with a distinctive peau d'orange (orange peel) texture.
  - Advancing, raised border that is sharply demarcated from adjacent normal, uninvolved skin.
  - Facial Distribution: Classically extends across the bridge of the nose with bilateral involvement of both cheeks (butterfly distribution).
  - Lower Extremity Distribution: Involves the legs or feet (70% to 80% of cases), frequently accompanied by regional lymphadenopathy and red linear lymphangitic streaking.
  - Bullous Manifestations: Flaccid intraepidermal bullae/blisters containing clear or serous fluid overlying the red plaque in approximately 5% of severe cases.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Bright red, edematous, indurated plaque with a sharply demarcated, raised advancing border and peau d'orange appearance.
  - Bilateral facial involvement extending across the nasal bridge in a classic butterfly pattern.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Polymorphonuclear leukocytosis is common, reflecting acute bacterial inflammation.
  - Inflammatory Markers: Elevated Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) and C-Reactive Protein (CRP).
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Radiography and cross-sectional imaging are generally unnecessary for typical cases; however, quantitative lymphoscintigraphy can demonstrate underlying subclinical or clinical primary/secondary lymphedema in patients with lower extremity erysipelas.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Surface Skin Cultures: Group A streptococci usually CANNOT be cultured from the intact surface of the skin lesion.
  - Needle Aspiration & Punch Biopsy: Pathogens are only rarely isolated from punch biopsy specimens or tissue fluid aspirated from the advancing edge of the lesion.
  - Blister Fluid Cultures: In bullous erysipelas, blister fluid is often sterile before erosion; after erosion, S. pyogenes, S. aureus, or MRSA may be isolated.
  - Ulcer Cultures: In cases complicating contiguous infected ulcers, Group A and other beta-hemolytic streptococci are isolated from the ulcerated area in up to 30% of patients.
  - Blood Cultures: Positive in 5% to 9% of cases (recovering Group A streptococci in 30% to 50%, other beta-hemolytic streptococci in up to 40%, and S. aureus or gram-negative pathogens in ~10%).
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - Clinical Diagnosis: Diagnosis is made primarily on the basis of the characteristic clinical appearance of the lesion (sharp, raised border, bright red peau d'orange induration) and the overall clinical setting.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Hospitalization for patients with severe disease, high fever, marked systemic toxicity, or bullous lesions requiring parenteral antimicrobial therapy.
  - Limb Elevation: Elevation of the affected extremity above heart level to promote lymphatic and venous drainage and reduce swelling.
  - Treatment of Portals of Entry: Aggressive management of underlying tinea pedis, skin hygiene, and care of contiguous skin ulcers.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Mild Early Outpatient Disease (Oral Therapy): Oral Penicillin V (500 mg every 6 hours) or initial intramuscular procaine penicillin (600,000 units once or twice daily).
  - Moderately Severe / Hospitalized Disease (Parenteral Therapy): Parenteral aqueous Penicillin G (2 million units IV every 6 hours), a first-generation cephalosporin (e.g., IV Cefazolin), or IV Ampicillin-sulbactam.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Penicillin-Allergic Patients: Oral Erythromycin (250-500 mg every 6 hours) or other macrolides (noting macrolide-resistant S. pyogenes in some areas). Oral or IV Clindamycin is an alternative (noting ~15% clindamycin resistance among Group A streptococci).
  - Suspected Staphylococcal Involvement / Diagnostic Ambiguity: When differentiation between erysipelas and staphylococcal cellulitis is not clear-cut, antistaphylococcal therapy with Nafcillin, Oxacillin, Ampicillin-sulbactam, or a first-generation cephalosporin should be administered.
  - Acutely Ill / Severe Bullous Erysipelas / Suspected MRSA: Intravenous Vancomycin is indicated to treat possible MRSA or non-susceptible streptococci pending blood culture results.
  - Chronic Suppressive Prophylaxis (for Recurrent Erysipelas): Long-term antibiotic suppression with oral Penicillin V (250-500 mg twice daily), monthly parenteral Benzathine Penicillin G (1.2 million units IM), or oral Erythromycin (250 mg once or twice daily for penicillin-allergic patients) significantly reduces the recurrence rate.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Surgical intervention is generally not indicated for uncomplicated erysipelas, and incision/débridement should be avoided unless complicated by deep subcutaneous abscesses, necrotizing fasciitis, or necrotic ulcers. Pneumatic compression devices, support stockings, or microsurgical lymphovenous anastomosis may be used to manage chronic lymphedema after acute infection resolves.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Bullous erysipelas (intraepidermal blister formation in ~5% of severe cases).
  - Deep subcutaneous tissue extension leading to cellulitis, subcutaneous abscess formation, or necrotizing fasciitis.
  - Bacteremia, septicemia, and systemic shock.
  - Secondary infection or superinfection with Staphylococcus aureus or MRSA.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Permanent lymphatic obstruction and chronic lymphedema.
  - Relapsing / Recurrent Erysipelas (recurrence rate of ~30% over 3 years).
  - Elephantiasis Nostras Verrucosa: Severe temperate-zone nonfilarial lymphatic obstruction following recurrent streptococcal erysipelas or lymphangitis, characterized by grotesque limb enlargement, dermal fibrosis, and verrucous pachydermia.
  - Poststreptococcal Glomerulonephritis: Potential nonsuppurative immune-mediated renal sequela following Group A streptococcal skin infection.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Peau d'orange (orange peel appearance of indurated edematous dermis).
  - Raised advancing border with sharp demarcation from normal skin.
  - Butterfly rash (extension across the nasal bridge with bilateral cheek involvement).
  - Elephantiasis nostras verrucosa (grotesque chronic lymphatic enlargement and verrucous skin changes following recurrent streptococcal erysipelas).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Sharp border exception: Typical erysipelas is distinguished from cellulitis by its raised, sharply demarcated border, but clinical differentiation occasionally may not be clear-cut (pseudoerysipelas features in venectomy cellulitis or staphylococcal skin infections).
  - Low culture yield exception: Group A streptococci usually cannot be cultured from the intact surface of the skin lesion and are only rarely isolated from punch biopsy or tissue fluid aspirates at the advancing edge.
  - Familial Mediterranean Fever (FMF): Recurrent erysipelas-like erythema occurs in FMF patients without streptococcal infection.
  - Carcinoma Erysipeloides: Diffuse inflammatory breast carcinoma mimics low-grade erysipelas without infectious etiology.
  - Campylobacter jejuni: Erysipelas-like skin lesions associated with C. jejuni bacteremia occur in patients with hypogammaglobulinemia.
  - Erythema Chronicum Migrans (Lyme Disease): Resembles erysipelas but is painless, progresses much more slowly, may demonstrate central clearing, and lacks high fever.
  - Early Facial Herpes Zoster: Zoster involving cranial nerve V (division 2) before vesiculation mimics unilateral facial erysipelas, but is distinguished by preceding severe pain and hyperesthesia.
  - Contact Dermatitis and Giant Urticaria: Visually resemble erysipelas but are distinguished by intense pruritus and absence of fever.
