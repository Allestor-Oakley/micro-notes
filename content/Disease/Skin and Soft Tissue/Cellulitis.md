# Cellulitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Cellulitis is an acute, spreading bacterial infection of the skin that extends deeper than erysipelas, involving the deep dermis and subcutaneous tissues. It is clinically characterized by localized pain, tenderness, swelling, warmth, and marked erythema. Unlike superficial pyodermas such as impetigo or erysipelas, cellulitis involves deeper soft tissue strata and typically lacks raised, sharply demarcated borders.
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Purulent vs. Nonpurulent Cellulitis: Nonpurulent cellulitis is most commonly caused by beta-hemolytic streptococci or MSSA and presents as spreading erythema without focal purulence or abscess. Purulent cellulitis is associated with a focal nidus of suppuration (abscess, pustule, furuncle, or eschar) and is predominantly caused by Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA).
  2. Clinical and Anatomical Variants:
     - Erysipelas: A distinctive superficial form of cellulitis with prominent lymphatic involvement, sharply demarcated raised borders, and a bright red, edematous, indurated (peau d'orange) appearance.
     - Saphenous Venectomy / Post-CABG Cellulitis: Recurrent lower extremity cellulitis extending along the harvest site of the saphenous vein in coronary artery bypass graft patients, frequently caused by non-group A beta-hemolytic streptococci (Groups B, C, G).
     - Perianal Streptococcal Cellulitis: Distinctive Group A streptococcal infection occurring principally in children, characterized by intense perianal erythema, pruritus, pain on defecation, and blood-streaked stools.
     - Buccal Cellulitis: Historically caused by Haemophilus influenzae type b in infants aged 3 to 24 months, presenting with an indurated cheek lesion with a characteristic bluish (blue dome) or purple-red hue and high rates of bacteremia.
     - Periorbital / Orbital Cellulitis: Infection involving the tissues around the orbit, caused by S. aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, or Group A streptococci.
     - Dissecting Cellulitis of the Scalp (Perifolliculitis Capitis Abscedens et Suffodiens): A chronic, progressive inflammatory condition featuring recurrent, painful, fluctuant dermal and subcutaneous nodules with interconnecting burrowing abscesses, purulent drainage, scarring, and alopecia.
     - Crepitant / Anaerobic Cellulitis: Cellulitis characterized by gas formation in devitalized subcutaneous tissue, caused by Clostridium perfringens, C. septicum, or nonclostridial anaerobes and facultative bacilli (E. coli, Klebsiella, Bacteroides, Peptostreptococcus).
     - Infectious Gangrene / Gangrenous Cellulitis: Rapidly progressive cellulitis with extensive necrosis of subcutaneous tissue and skin (e.g., Type I polymicrobial necrotizing fasciitis, Type II Group A streptococcal gangrene, Type III marine Vibrio/Aeromonas infection, progressive bacterial synergistic gangrene, or synergistic necrotizing cellulitis).
     - Erysipeloid: An indolent cellulitis caused by Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae occurring principally in individuals who handle saltwater fish, shellfish, poultry, meat, or hides.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Epidemiologic Factors: Cellulitis occurs across the entire age spectrum. Minor local skin trauma (lacerations, abrasions, puncture wounds, shaving abrasions, athletic turf burns, insect or animal bites) is the most common initiating pathogenic event. Specific environmental exposures confer unique infection risks (e.g., marine or saltwater exposure for Vibrio vulnificus, freshwater exposure for Aeromonas hydrophila, handling raw fish, poultry, or meat for Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae or Streptococcus iniae).
  2. Systemic and Local Risk Factors:
     - Chronic dependent edema, lymphedema (which impairs lymphatic drainage and immune cell trafficking), venous stasis, deep venous thrombosis, and peripheral vascular disease.
     - Obesity, diabetes mellitus, alcohol abuse, cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome (predisposing to spontaneous Escherichia coli cellulitis).
     - Prior Surgical Procedures: Saphenous vein harvesting, axillary lymph node dissection (post-mastectomy breast or arm cellulitis), lumpectomy with breast irradiation, and radical pelvic surgery or radiation therapy (predisposing to Group B and G streptococcal cellulitis of the vulva, groin, and legs, occasionally occurring postcoitally).
     - Immunocompromise: Neutropenia, hematologic malignancies, corticosteroid therapy, organ transplantation, hypogammaglobulinemia (predisposing to Campylobacter jejuni cellulitis), and HIV infection (predisposing to Helicobacter cinaedi, Cryptococcus, Fusarium, or Pseudomonas gangrenous cellulitis).
     - Injection Drug Use ("skin popping"): Predisposes to severe polymicrobial, S. aureus, or streptococcal cellulitis, subcutaneous abscesses, and necrotizing soft tissue infections.
     - Preexisting Cutaneous Breakdown: Tinea pedis (a major portal of entry for lower extremity cellulitis), furuncles, ulcers, eczema, and varicella lesions.
     - High Recurrence Rate: Recurrent cellulitis occurs in 22% to 49% of patients, particularly those with persistent lymphedema, venous insufficiency, or tinea pedis.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes): Principal cause of nonpurulent cellulitis, erysipelas, rapidly spreading early postoperative wound cellulitis, and Type II necrotizing fasciitis.
  - Other Beta-Hemolytic Streptococci: Groups B (Streptococcus agalactiae), C, and G streptococci (major causes of recurrent lower extremity cellulitis in lymphedema, saphenous venectomy site cellulitis, and post-pelvic surgery cellulitis).
  - Staphylococcus aureus: Major cause of purulent cellulitis, furuncles/carbuncles with surrounding cellulitis, and abscess-associated soft tissue infections. Both Methicillin-Susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) and Methicillin-Resistant S. aureus (MRSA) are common.
  - Combined direct tissue aspirate sampling recovers S. aureus in approximately 50% and Group A streptococci in approximately 25% of positive cultures.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Infants and Young Children: Historically, Haemophilus influenzae type b was a prominent cause of buccal/facial cellulitis in children aged 3 to 24 months (now rare due to Hib conjugate vaccine). Group A streptococci cause perianal cellulitis in young children and superinfect varicella lesions. Group B streptococci cause cellulitis/erysipelas in neonates (originating from umbilical stump infection or maternal vaginal carriage).
  - Adults and Older Adults: Group A streptococci, MSSA/MRSA, non-group A beta-hemolytic streptococci (Groups C, G, B), and Enterococcus faecalis. Lower extremity cellulitis in elderly patients is frequently complicated by thrombophlebitis and underlying venous stasis or lymphedema.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Marine and Saltwater Exposures: Vibrio vulnificus, Vibrio alginolyticus, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, and non-serogroup O1 Vibrio cholerae (cause severe cellulitis with hemorrhagic bullae, rapid necrosis, and bacteremia, especially in patients with alcoholic cirrhosis, hemochromatosis, or thalassemia).
  - Freshwater Exposures: Aeromonas hydrophila (rapidly progressive crepitant cellulitis or myonecrosis after freshwater trauma or catfish spine punctures).
  - Occupational/Animal Exposures: Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae (erysipeloid in meat, fish, or poultry handlers), Streptococcus iniae (rapid cellulitis within 6 to 24 hours after fish bone or knife punctures in tilapia handlers), Pasteurella multocida (animal bites or lacerations), and Corynebacterium diphtheriae (cutaneous diphtheria presenting as chronic nonhealing ulcers or impetiginoid lesions).
  - Diabetic Foot & Decubitus Ulcer Cellulitis: Polymicrobial mixtures including Enterobacteriaceae (E. coli, Klebsiella, Proteus, Enterobacter), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter species, Enterococcus species, and anaerobes (Bacteroides fragilis group, Peptostreptococcus, Clostridium species).
  - Immunocompromised / Neutropenic Hosts: Helicobacter cinaedi (causes salmon-pink, red-brown, or copper-colored indolent cellulitis with bacteremia), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ecthyma gangrenosum and gangrenous cellulitis), Escherichia coli (spontaneous cellulitis in nephrotic syndrome or hematologic malignancies), Legionella pneumophila and Legionella micdadei, Serratia marcescens, Campylobacter jejuni and C. fetus, Cryptococcus neoformans, Fusarium species, Aspergillus species, and agents of Mucormycosis (Rhizopus, Mucor, Lichtheimia).
  - Crepitant / Gas-Forming Pathogens: Clostridium perfringens, Clostridium septicum, Clostridium sordellii, as well as nonclostridial facultative and anaerobic bacteria (E. coli, Klebsiella, Bacteroides, Peptostreptococcus).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Entry Portal: Infection typically originates from primary cutaneous inoculation following disruption of the epidermal barrier via minor or major trauma (abrasions, lacerations, puncture wounds, surgical incisions, venectomy sites, needle injections, or insect bites) or preexisting skin lesions (tinea pedis, ulcers, dermatoses). Less commonly, cellulitis results from direct extension of a contiguous deep focus (subcutaneous abscess, osteomyelitis fistula, perirectal abscess) or hematogenous seeding from distant bacteremia (e.g., pneumococcal, meningococcal, or Helicobacter cinaedi bacteremia).
  - Tissue Localization & Tissue Spread: Inoculated bacteria multiply within the deep dermis and subcutaneous tissue layers. Streptococci produce extracellular enzymes (hyaluronidase, streptokinase, DNases) that facilitate rapid, widespread diffusion through tissue spaces and lymphatic channels without forming focal purulent collections, leading to ill-defined advancing borders. Staphylococci produce coagulase and toxins, promoting localized suppuration, tissue destruction, and focal abscess formation. Infection readily spreads along lymphatic channels, causing regional lymphangitis and lymphadenitis.
  - Impaired Lymphatic Drainage & Edema: Preexisting lymphedema or venous stasis creates a protein-rich interstitial fluid environment that impairs immune cell trafficking and innate clearance mechanisms. Recurrent bouts of cellulitis further damage and occlude lymphatic vessels, establishing a vicious cycle of worsening lymphedema and recurrent infections.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Inflammatory Cascade: Bacterial proliferation triggers a dense polymorphonuclear leukocytic infiltration in the dermis and subcutaneous fat, accompanied by marked vascular dilation, increased capillary permeability, and tissue edema.
  - Vascular and Microvascular Complications: Local inflammatory mediators cause prominent tissue hyperemia (erythema) and warmth. Fibrin thrombi may form in small dermal and subcutaneous blood vessels, which in severe or necrotizing cases leads to local ischemia, patch necrosis, bulla formation, or gangrene. Lower extremity cellulitis can induce secondary deep venous thrombophlebitis.
  - Systemic Toxin Effects: Exotoxin-producing strains (e.g., Group A streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins acting as superantigens, or S. aureus Panton-Valentine leukocidin / TSST-1) provoke profound systemic inflammatory responses, resulting in high fever, marked leukocytosis, hypotension, capillary leak, and potentially toxic shock syndrome and multiorgan failure.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Acute or subacute onset of localized skin pain, tenderness, swelling, and spreading redness, usually developing within several days after minor or major local cutaneous trauma.
  - Systemic constitutional symptoms including fever, chills, rigors, malaise, headache, and generalized toxicity.
  - History of predisposing environmental or occupational exposures (marine or freshwater trauma, handling raw fish or poultry, animal bites, recent surgery, saphenous venectomy, or injection drug use).
  - History of recurrent leg swelling, lymphedema, venous stasis, or tinea pedis in lower extremity cases.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Erythema, Warmth, and Edema: Affected skin is intensely erythematous, warm to touch, swollen, and tender. In contrast to erysipelas, cellulitis borders are ill-defined, non-elevated, and lack sharp demarcation, often showing patchy involvement with skip areas.
  - Lymphatic Involvement: Regional lymphadenopathy is common; red linear streaks (lymphangitic streaking) extending proximally from the cellulitic area toward regional lymph node basins are frequently present.
  - Purulence vs. Nonpurulence: Presence of focal suppuration, pustules, fluctuant abscesses, furuncles, or central eschars indicates staphylococcal involvement.
  - Advanced Cutaneous Changes: Petechiae, ecchymoses, flaccid bullae (serous or hemorrhagic), and small patches of cutaneous necrosis or skin sloughing may develop in severe cases.
  - Special Anatomical/Clinical Signs:
    - Saphenous Venectomy Cellulitis: Erythema and marked edema extending along the surgical course of the harvested leg vein.
    - Perianal Streptococcal Cellulitis: Intense perianal erythema, anal fissures, pain on defecation, purulent perianal discharge.
    - Buccal Cellulitis: Indurated swelling of the cheek with a characteristic bluish or purple-red (violaceous) discoloration.
    - Erysipeloid: Well-demarcated, violaceous or purplish cellulitic lesion lacking suppuration, typically on the hand.
    - Marine Vibrio Cellulitis: Rapidly spreading erythema with hemorrhagic bullae and necrotic skin ulcers.
- **Pathognomonic Features:** Cellulitis lacks a single pathognomonic sign; however, specific clinical presentations provide strong diagnostic clues:
  - Nidus of focal suppuration (abscess/pustule) with surrounding erythema is a hallmark of staphylococcal cellulitis.
  - Rapidly spreading erythema without sharp borders and associated lymphangitic streaking is characteristic of streptococcal cellulitis.
  - Coral-red fluorescence under Wood lamp in C. minutissimum infection (erythrasma/pitted keratolysis).
  - Bluish/violaceous cheek swelling in H. influenzae type b buccal cellulitis in an unimmunized infant.
  - Red linear streaks directed toward regional lymph nodes in acute lymphangitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Polymorphonuclear leukocytosis with a left shift (increased immature band forms) is typically present regardless of bacterial etiology. Thrombocytopenia may be observed if complicated by sepsis, DIC, or toxic shock syndrome.
  - Inflammatory Markers: Elevated Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) and C-Reactive Protein (CRP).
  - Serum Chemistry & Biomarkers: Elevated serum Creatine Phosphokinase (CPK) levels indicate deep muscle involvement and progression to necrotizing fasciitis or myositis. Azotemia, elevated creatinine, or metabolic acidosis indicate systemic toxicity or severe sepsis.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Plain Radiography (X-rays): Useful to rule out underlying osteomyelitis, radiopaque foreign bodies, or soft tissue gas (subcutaneous crepitus/emphysema in clostridial or nonclostridial crepitant cellulitis).
  - Ultrasonography (US): Bedside point-of-care US effectively differentiates cellulitis (showing cobblestoning appearance of subcutaneous edema) from subcutaneous abscesses (demonstrating fluid collections) and deep pyomyositis/necrotizing fasciitis.
  - Computed Tomography (CT) and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): CT and MRI demonstrate subcutaneous fat stranding, fascial thickening, deep tissue edema, and gas collections. MRI is highly sensitive for distinguishing cellulitis from necrotizing fasciitis or deep compartment infections, though imaging should never delay urgent surgical exploration when necrotizing infection is suspected.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Needle Aspiration & Tissue Biopsy: Routine needle aspiration of intact cellulitic skin is NOT indicated due to low yield (pathogen identified in only ~16% of cases). Diagnostic aspiration or punch biopsy is reserved for:
    1. Immunocompromised hosts with atypical presentations.
    2. Presence of fluctuant areas, purulent bullae, or focal abscesses.
    3. Failure of initial empiric antimicrobial therapy.
    4. Specific environmental exposures (e.g., marine or freshwater trauma).
  - Gram Stain and Culture: Gram-stained smears of pus from unroofed bullae, drainage from contiguous ulcers, or surgical aspirates reveal gram-positive cocci in pairs/chains (streptococci) or clusters (staphylococci), or gram-negative bacilli.
  - Blood Cultures: Positive in only 2% to 8% of uncomplicated community-acquired cellulitis cases, but yield increases to 18% in hospitalized patients and is higher in cellulitis superimposed on lymphedema, severe sepsis, or marine Vibrio/Aeromonas exposures.
  - Molecular & Serologic Tests: Real-time PCR testing of tissue or blood for specific pathogens (e.g., B. anthracis, N. meningitidis, Group A Streptococcus). Anti-streptolysin O (ASO) and anti-DNase B titers confirm antecedent streptococcal infection.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - Clinical Diagnosis: Uncomplicated cellulitis is primarily a clinical diagnosis based on history, physical examination, and characteristic soft tissue findings.
  - Surgical Exploration & Frozen-Section Biopsy: For suspected deep or necrotizing soft tissue infection (necrotizing fasciitis/gangrenous cellulitis), direct surgical inspection of deep subcutaneous tissue and fascia (showing "dishwater" fluid, lack of bleeding, and easy blunt dissociation of fascial planes) with frozen-section histopathology is the definitive gold standard.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Hemodynamic Resuscitation: Intravenous fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support for patients presenting with hypotension, severe sepsis, or toxic shock syndrome.
  - Local Supportive Measures: Elevation of the affected extremity above heart level to promote venous and lymphatic drainage and reduce edema. Cool saline compresses or moist heat applications. Immobilization of the limb during acute inflammation.
  - Management of Underlying Factors: Aggressive control of underlying tinea pedis, skin hygiene, treatment of venous stasis/lymphedema (elastic support stockings, pneumatic compression devices once acute infection subsides).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Mild, Uncomplicated Nonpurulent Cellulitis (Outpatient Oral Therapy):
    - Target: Group A Streptococcus and MSSA.
    - Regimens: Oral Cephalexin (500 mg every 6 hours), Dicloxacillin (500 mg every 6 hours), or Cefadroxil (0.5-1.0 g once or twice daily) for 5 to 10 days.
  - Moderately Severe Nonpurulent Cellulitis (Inpatient Parenteral Therapy):
    - Regimens: IV Cefazolin (1.0-1.5 g every 8 hours) or IV Nafcillin / Oxacillin (2.0 g every 4-6 hours).
  - Purulent Cellulitis or Suspected MRSA Infection (Outpatient / Inpatient Therapy):
    - Target: Community-Acquired MRSA and MSSA.
    - Oral Regimens: Oral Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX, 1 to 2 double-strength tablets twice daily) or oral Clindamycin (300-450 mg every 6-8 hours, where local resistance is low). Note: TMP-SMX is widely used off-label for MRSA skin infections.
    - Parenteral Regimens: IV Vancomycin (1.0-1.5 g every 12 hours, target trough 15-20 mcg/mL) or IV Daptomycin (4-6 mg/kg once daily).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Penicillin-Allergic Patients:
    - Mild/Moderate: Oral Clindamycin (300-450 mg every 6-8 hours) or oral macrolides (Azithromycin, Erythromycin - note rising streptococcal/staphylococcal resistance).
    - Severe: IV Vancomycin (1.0 g every 12 hours), IV Linezolid (600 mg every 12 hours), or IV Daptomycin.
  - Severe MRSA & Advanced Gram-Positive Agents:
    - IV Linezolid (600 mg every 12 hours), IV Ceftaroline (5th generation cephalosporin with MRSA activity), IV Tedizolid, or long-acting lipoglycopeptides (Dalbavancin, Oritavancin, Telavancin).
  - Specific Environmental / Clinical Scenarios:
    - Saltwater / Marine Injury (Vibrio vulnificus): Doxycycline (100 mg IV/PO twice daily) PLUS Ceftazidime (or Cefotaxime or Ciprofloxacin).
    - Freshwater Injury (Aeromonas hydrophila): Ciprofloxacin (400 mg IV / 500 mg PO twice daily) or Ceftazidime / Gentamicin.
    - Animal / Human Bites (Pasteurella, Eikenella, anaerobes): Oral Amoxicillin-Clavulanate (875/125 mg twice daily) or IV Ampicillin-Sulbactam (1.5-3.0 g every 6 hours).
    - Diabetic Foot / Decubitus Ulcer Cellulitis (Broad Polymicrobial): IV Ampicillin-Sulbactam (3.0 g every 6 hours), Piperacillin-Tazobactam (3.375-4.5 g every 6 hours), or Meropenem / Imipenem PLUS Vancomycin (for MRSA).
    - Group A Streptococcal Toxic Shock / Streptococcal Gangrene: IV Penicillin G (3-4 million units every 4 hours) PLUS IV Clindamycin (600-900 mg every 6-8 hours to inhibit exotoxin production via the Eagle effect).
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Incision and Drainage: Mandatory for cellulitis associated with focal fluctuant abscesses, carbuncles, or infected sebaceous cysts.
  - Emergency Surgical Débridement: Urgent, aggressive operative exploration and extensive surgical débridement of necrotic fat, fascia, and devitalized tissue are required for gangrenous cellulitis, necrotizing fasciitis, or clostridial myonecrosis. Second-look re-exploration is performed in 24 hours.
  - Excision and Grafting: Wide surgical excision of sinus tracts and skin grafting for chronic refractory dissecting cellulitis of the scalp or progressive bacterial synergistic gangrene.
  - Prophylaxis for Recurrent Cellulitis: Chronic suppressive antibiotic therapy with oral Penicillin V (250-500 mg twice daily), monthly IM Benzathine Penicillin G (1.2 million units), or oral Erythromycin (for penicillin-allergic) significantly reduces recurrence in patients with lymphedema or venous stasis.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Bacteremia and Septicemia: Dissemination of bacteria into the bloodstream, occurring in 2% to 8% of community cellulitis cases and higher in lymphedema or compromised hosts.
  - Septic Shock and Toxic Shock Syndrome: Mediated by pyrogenic exotoxins or superantigens, leading to capillary leak, refractory hypotension, multiorgan failure, and DIC (purpura fulminans).
  - Local Abscess Formation & Cutaneous Necrosis: Focal tissue breakdown, bulla rupture, and skin gangrene requiring drainage or débridement.
  - Deep Tissue Extension: Progression of superficial cellulitis into necrotizing fasciitis, infectious tenosynovitis, suppurative arthritis, pyomyositis, or osteomyelitis.
  - Thrombophlebitis: Secondary deep or superficial venous thrombosis complicating lower extremity cellulitis or axillary lymphadenitis.
  - Compartment Syndrome: Increased tissue pressure within closed fascial compartments (e.g., leg or forearm) secondary to severe edema and exudate, requiring urgent decompressive fasciotomy.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Permanent Lymphatic Destruction & Chronic Lymphedema: Inflammatory damage to lymphatic vessels during acute cellulitis obstructs lymphatic drainage, causing persistent swelling and predisposing to recurrent cellulitis.
  - Elephantiasis Nostras Verrucosa: A severe, progressive temperate-zone form of nonfilarial lymphatic obstruction following recurrent streptococcal cellulitis/lymphangitis, characterized by grotesque limb enlargement, dermal fibrosis, and verrucous pachydermia.
  - Poststreptococcal Glomerulonephritis: Nonsuppurative immune-mediated renal complication following Group A streptococcal skin infection (associated with elevated anti-DNase B titers).
  - Tissue Scarring, Fibrosis, and Alopecia: Scarring and permanent hair loss following severe necrotizing skin sloughing or dissecting cellulitis of the scalp.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Peau d'orange" (indurated, orange-peel cutaneous appearance in erysipelas and severe edematous cellulitis).
  - "Blue dome" or violaceous / purple-red hue (classic visual appearance of Haemophilus influenzae type b buccal cellulitis in infants).
  - "Red linear streaks" (pathognomonic visual manifestation of acute ascending lymphangitis).
  - "Dishwater pus" (thin, foul or serosanguineous exudate characteristic of necrotizing fasciitis and anaerobic cellulitis).
  - "Cobblestoning" (ultrasound descriptor for subcutaneous tissue edema in cellulitis).
  - "Malignant pustule" and "gelatinous nonpitting edema" (descriptors for cutaneous anthrax mimicking severe cellulitis).
  - "Rose spots" (pink papular skin lesions in Salmonella Typhi bacteremia).
  - "Pastia lines" and "Nikolsky sign" (associated with scarlet fever and staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome / SSSS).
  - "Eagle effect" (reduced efficacy of penicillin against large stationary-phase bacterial populations, overcome by adding clindamycin).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Erysipelas vs. Cellulitis Boundary: Erysipelas is classically distinguished from cellulitis by its raised, sharply demarcated borders; however, clinical overlap occurs, and staphylococcal cellulitis can occasionally present with pseudoerysipelas features.
  - Direct Sampling Yield: Unlike abscesses or skin ulcers, culturing or aspirating intact cellulitic skin has an extremely low diagnostic yield (~16%), making routine diagnostic needle aspiration unnecessary and unrewarding.
  - Co-Trimoxazole Activity against S. pyogenes: While TMP-SMX is FDA-approved primarily for MRSA, in vitro and emerging clinical data demonstrate efficacy against Group A Streptococcus as well, making it useful for empirical management of uncomplicated cellulitis and skin abscesses.
  - Absence of Skin Lesions in Disseminated Gonococcemia: Disseminated gonococcal infection typically features classic petechial/pustular skin lesions, but patients receiving monoclonal complement inhibitors (e.g., eculizumab) paradoxically present without skin lesions.
  - Eosinophilic Cellulitis (Wells Syndrome): Mimics bacterial cellulitis with edematous erythematous plaques and fever, but lacks local heat and tenderness, fails to respond to antibiotics, displays dermal eosinophilic infiltration on biopsy, and responds to systemic corticosteroids.
  - Carcinoma Erysipeloides: Inflammatory breast carcinoma mimicking low-grade erysipelas or cellulitis without true bacterial infection.
  - Dermatitis Artefacta / Factitial Cellulitis: Self-induced subcutaneous lesions or polymicrobial cellulitis resulting from deliberate injection of foreign substances (e.g., saliva, contaminated fluids); identified by birefringent foreign bodies under polarizing microscopy.
  - Pain Out of Proportion: Presence of severe, excruciating pain out of proportion to physical skin findings is a major red flag that infection has progressed past simple cellulitis into deep necrotizing fasciitis or pyomyositis.
