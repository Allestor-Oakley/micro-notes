# Myonecrosis and Myositis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Infectious myositis is an inflammatory and generally necrotizing process of skeletal muscle resulting primarily from hematogenous seeding of muscle during transient bacteremia or direct bacterial invasion following penetrating trauma, surgical contamination, or contiguous extension from adjacent deep tissue foci (such as osteomyelitis or decubitus ulcers). Myonecrosis specifically denotes ischemic or toxin-mediated destructive liquefaction or gas-producing gangrene of muscle tissue. Generalized muscle inflammation and degeneration (rhabdomyolysis) can also accompany systemic viral, parasitic, or bacterial illnesses.
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Pyogenic and Predominantly Localized Infections:
     - Pyomyositis (Primary Muscle Abscess / Tropical Pyomyositis): Acute primary bacterial infection of skeletal muscle leading to localized pus accumulation within muscle bellies, occurring without a primary predisposing site in adjacent skin or bone.
     - Clostridial Myonecrosis (Gas Gangrene): Rapidly progressive, life-threatening histotoxic clostridial invasion of skeletal muscle following dirty penetrating trauma (traumatic gas gangrene, primarily C. perfringens) or hematogenous dissemination from GI mucosal lesions (spontaneous or nontraumatic gas gangrene, primarily C. septicum).
     - Group A Streptococcal Necrotizing Myositis (Peracute Streptococcal Pyomyositis / Streptococcal Myonecrosis / Spontaneous Streptococcal Gangrenous Myositis): Fulminant, peracute monomicrobial necrotizing streptococcal infection of skeletal muscle, frequently accompanied by streptococcal toxic shock syndrome.
     - Nonclostridial (Crepitant) Myositis & Synergistic Nonclostridial Anaerobic Myonecrosis: Mixed anaerobic and facultative bacterial myonecrosis involving muscle, fascia, and subcutaneous fat (e.g., Peptostreptococcus, Bacteroides, E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter).
     - Aeromonas hydrophila Myonecrosis: Rapidly progressive myonecrosis accompanied by soft tissue gas following freshwater penetrating trauma.
     - Infected Vascular Gangrene: Secondary bacterial infection of muscle groups devitalized by arterial insufficiency or diabetic vascular disease.
     - Psoas & Deep Pelvic Muscle Abscesses: Anatomically distinct deep retroperitoneal or pelvic muscle abscesses (psoas, iliacus, obturator internus/externus, piriformis) originating from hematogenous spread or contiguous gastrointestinal/urologic lesions.
  2. Nonpyogenic and Predominantly Generalized Processes:
     - Epidemic Pleurodynia (Bornholm Disease): Acute febrile viral myositis of intercostal and diaphragmatic muscle caused by Coxsackievirus B.
     - Viral Myalgias & Acute Benign Childhood Myositis: Transient muscle pain, stiffness, and gastrocnemius-soleus tenderness following influenza A, influenza B, or dengue virus infections.
     - Parasitic Myositis: Muscle invasion accompanied by marked eosinophilia caused by Trichinella spiralis / T. pseudospiralis (trichinosis), Taenia solium cysticerci (cysticercosis), Sarcocystis, or Haycocknema perplexum.
     - Infection-Associated Rhabdomyolysis: Severe generalized muscle breakdown with extreme serum CPK elevation and myoglobinuria triggered by influenza, dengue, EBV, enteroviruses, or Legionella.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Epidemiology & Geographic Variations: Pyomyositis occurs worldwide; in warm tropical climates (tropical pyomyositis), it predominantly affects children (accounting for up to 1% to 4% of pediatric hospital admissions historically in endemic tropical regions like Uganda), whereas in temperate regions (North America, Europe, Australia), it occurs less commonly and predominated in adults and elderly individuals (annual incidence ranging from 0.5 to 13.5 per 100,000). Clostridial myonecrosis occurs worldwide following dirty penetrating trauma, combat injuries, natural disasters, or surgical procedures.
  2. Portals of Entry & Initiating Factors:
     - Penetrating trauma (compound fractures, war wounds, motor vehicle accidents, dirty lacerations), surgical wounds (bowel or biliary tract surgery, septic abortion, vasectomy), arterial insufficiency, and parenteral injections (subcutaneous epinephrine, insulin, black-tar heroin or methamphetamine skin popping, fluoroscopically guided joint injections, or venipuncture / platelet transfusions in neutropenic patients).
     - Blunt muscle trauma or vigorous exercise precedes 20% to 50% of pyomyositis cases (locus minoris resistentiae).
     - Gastrointestinal mucosal lesions (colon carcinoma, diverticulitis, bowel infarction, necrotizing enterocolitis, volvulus) serve as the portal of entry for spontaneous C. septicum gas gangrene.
  3. Systemic Risk Factors & Host Susceptibility: Diabetes mellitus, chronic alcoholism, alcoholic liver disease / cirrhosis, HIV infection / AIDS (predisposing to S. aureus, Salmonella, MAC, and Cryptococcus pyomyositis), intravenous drug abuse, corticosteroid therapy, hematologic malignancies (leukemia, lymphoma, multiple myeloma, myelodysplasia, cyclic neutropenia, neutropenic colitis), chronic granulomatous disease (CGD), rheumatologic disorders (rheumatoid arthritis, SLE), post-transplant immunosuppression, end-stage renal disease / hemodialysis, and recent varicella infection in children.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Pyomyositis: Staphylococcus aureus is the single most common cause overall (responsible for 95% of tropical pyomyositis cases and 66% to 70% of temperate-region pyomyositis cases, including community-acquired MRSA carrying Panton-Valentine leukocidin / PVL genes). Group A Streptococcus accounts for 1% to 5% of pyomyositis cases.
  - Clostridial Myonecrosis (Gas Gangrene): Clostridium perfringens is isolated in 80% to 95% of traumatic gas gangrene cases. Clostridium novyi is involved in 10% to 40%, Clostridium septicum in 5% to 20% (and the primary cause of spontaneous nontraumatic gas gangrene), and Clostridium sordellii (associated with home abortions or black-tar heroin injection).
  - Group A Streptococcal Necrotizing Myositis: Monomicrobial Group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes, predominantly M-protein types M1, M3, M12, M28 carrying speA/speC exotoxin genes).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children: Tropical pyomyositis caused by S. aureus, S. pyogenes, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and viral myositis (influenza B, influenza A, Coxsackievirus B). Superinfected varicella skin lesions predispose children to Group A streptococcal necrotizing myositis.
  - Adults & Elderly: S. aureus (MSSA and MRSA), S. pyogenes, C. perfringens, C. septicum, Groups B, C, G streptococci (including S. dysgalactiae subsp. equisimilis), Enterobacteriaceae, anaerobes, and vascular gangrene organisms.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Gram-Negative Bacilli: Escherichia coli (emerging cause in neutropenic patients with hematologic malignancy), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Klebsiella oxytoca, Serratia marcescens, Proteus, Enterobacter cloacae, Morganella morganii, Citrobacter freundii, Salmonella species, Yersinia enterocolitica, Francisella novicida, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Burkholderia cenocepacia, Burkholderia mallei (glanders), Burkholderia pseudomallei (melioidosis), and Aeromonas hydrophila (freshwater trauma).
  - Anaerobes: Fusobacterium necrophorum, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Veillonella, Peptostreptococcus, Capnocytophaga sputigena, Actinomyces, and Clostridium species.
  - Mycobacteria & Fungi: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC, as an IRIS manifestation in AIDS), Mycobacterium haemophilum, Cryptococcus neoformans, Histoplasma capsulatum, Candida albicans / Candida tropicalis, Aspergillus fumigatus, Fusarium species, Scedosporium inflatum, and Mucormycosis agents.
  - Parasites & Viruses: Trichinella spiralis, Trichinella pseudospiralis, Taenia solium (cysticercosis), Toxoplasma gondii, Sarcocystis, Haycocknema perplexum, Microsporidia (Trachipleistophora hominis), Dracunculus medinensis, Toxocara canis, Influenza A and B, Dengue virus, EBV, CMV, Echoviruses, Coxsackieviruses A and B, HTLV-1, and HIV.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Bacteremic Seeding vs. Direct Inoculation: Skeletal muscle is normally highly resistant to bacterial infection due to rich blood supply and local tissue defenses. In pyomyositis, transient bacteremia seeds muscle tissue, particularly when localized minor blunt trauma or strenuous exercise causes microscopic hematoma formation or local ischemia (locus minoris resistentiae). In clostridial myonecrosis, direct inoculation of spores into devitalized, hypoxic muscle containing sterile dirt or foreign debris lowers the oxidation-reduction potential, enabling rapid clostridial germination and proliferation (generation time as short as 8 minutes).
  - Toxin Production & Microvascular Thrombosis: In gas gangrene, C. perfringens alpha-toxin (a lecithinase / phospholipase C) and theta-toxin (perfringolysin O) cause myocyte cytolysis, destruction of cell membranes, and profound intravascular platelet-leukocyte aggregation. Microvascular thrombosis of nutrient vessels induces widespread muscular ischemia, intense herald pain, and rapid propagation of myonecrosis.
  - Gas Generation & Compartment Pressure: Bacterial metabolic fermentation of muscle glycogen and tissue proteins produces gas (carbon dioxide and hydrogen) that dissects along fascial planes and muscle bundles. Rapid fluid exudation and gas accumulation within rigid muscle compartments dramatically increase intramuscular pressure, causing secondary compartment syndrome, further muscle necrosis, and nerve compression.
  - Systemic Toxin Dissemination: Streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins (SpeA, SpeC) acting as superantigens or clostridial alpha-toxin directly suppress myocardial contractility, provoke systemic capillary leak, and trigger massive endogenous inflammatory mediator release, culminating in septic shock and multiorgan failure.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Histopathology of Pyomyositis: Early invasive stage shows focal interfascicular muscle edema, PMN leukocytic infiltration, and myocyte degeneration, progressing to suppurative central liquefaction necrosis with mature intramuscular abscess formation surrounded by granulation tissue.
  - Histopathology of Gas Gangrene: Grossly, involved muscle initially appears pale and edematous, then turns reddish-purple, greenish-purple, and dark gangrenous, becoming friable, non-contractile on electrical/mechanical stimulation, and non-bleeding when cut. Histologically, muscle fibers show extensive coagulation necrosis, empty gas cavities between fiber bundles, loss of supporting stroma, abundant blunt-ended gram-positive bacilli, and a striking absence of inflammatory leukocytes (due to toxin-induced leukocidins and vascular leukostasis).
  - Viral & Parasitic Histopathology: Viral myositis shows patchy myocyte necrosis with sparse or variable lymphoplasmacytic infiltration. Trichinosis features intracellular encysted larvae within nurse cells in skeletal muscle, surrounded by intense eosinophilic and granulomatous inflammation.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Pyomyositis: Subacute onset of variable fever, localized muscle pain, stiffness, and swelling evolving over several weeks, or acute onset with high fever, severe focal muscle pain, and systemic toxicity. History of recent minor blunt muscle trauma or vigorous exercise in 20% to 50% of cases.
  - Clostridial Myonecrosis (Gas Gangrene): Abrupt onset of severe, excruciating local pain in an injured extremity or post-surgical wound (the earliest and most reliable symptom), rapidly progressing over hours. The patient appears severely ill, pale, sweating, anxious, or apathetic, rapidly developing hypotension and shock.
  - Group A Streptococcal Necrotizing Myositis: Peracute, fulminant onset of excruciating muscle pain, boardlike muscle swelling, high fever, chills, and rapid collapse into toxic shock syndrome within 24 to 48 hours.
  - Viral Myositis & Epidemic Pleurodynia: Bilateral calf pain, tenderness, and difficulty walking in children following influenza B (acute benign childhood myositis), or paroxysms of severe, sharp, knifelike lower chest / epigastric pain precipitated by breathing or movement in epidemic pleurodynia (Coxsackievirus B).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Stages of Pyomyositis:
    - Stage 1 (Invasive Stage, Days 1-10): Subacute onset, mild pain, local muscle swelling with firm or wooden consistency, minimal tenderness, intact overlying skin without warmth or erythema. Aspiration yields no pus.
    - Stage 2 (Suppurative Stage, Days 10-21): Fever, distinct muscle tenderness and localized swelling conforming to the involved muscle belly. Overlying skin is warm and intact, but erythema is often absent because deep muscle fascia contains the pus. Aspiration yields frank purulent material.
    - Stage 3 (Late / Septic Stage): Severe systemic toxicity, high fever, exquisite local tenderness, skin erythema, warmth, and fluctuance. Metastatic septic emboli, shock, and multiorgan failure may supervene.
  - Gas Gangrene Physical Findings: Tense, marked edema and local tenderness around a wound. Serosanguineous, dirty-appearing, brownish or pale discharge with a peculiar sweet or foul odor escaping from the wound, sometimes containing visible gas bubbles. Overlying skin transforms rapidly from pale/swollen to a characteristic bronze or yellow-brown discoloration, followed by tense hemorrhagic bullae containing dark fluid and patches of green-black skin necrosis. Subcutaneous crepitus is usually present but may be obscured by severe edema.
  - Specific Anatomical Presentations:
    - Iliopsoas / Iliacus Pyomyositis: Pain on hip extension, flexed hip posture, antalgic limp, pain radiating to the groin or thigh, mimicking appendicitis or septic hip arthritis.
    - Obturator Internus / Piriformis Pyomyositis: Deep pelvic or buttock pain, fever, sciatica-like radiation, pain on internal/external hip rotation.
    - Quadriceps / Calf Pyomyositis: Swelling, induration, and marked tenderness of the thigh or calf muscles, potentially complicating into anterior tibial or forearm compartment syndrome.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Bronze or yellow-brown skin discoloration with tense serosanguineous bullae and crepitus overlying a rapidly progressive, excruciatingly painful wound (classic sign of clostridial gas gangrene).
  - Severe local muscle pain distinctly out of proportion to early physical findings or skin changes.
  - Contractile failure and lack of bleeding from discolored muscle on surgical incision (pathognomonic operative finding of myonecrosis).
  - Bilateral severe calf pain and gastrocnemius tenderness with difficulty walking in a child recovering from influenza B (acute benign childhood myositis).

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Marked leukocytosis with a prominent left shift (bandemia) in pyomyositis and gas gangrene; leukopenia or hypothermia indicates severe septic shock. Decreased hematocrit/hemoglobin in gas gangrene due to alpha-toxin lecithinase intravascular hemolysis. Eosinophilia is common in tropical pyomyositis (reflecting underlying helminthiasis) and marked in trichinosis.
  - Inflammatory Markers: Markedly elevated C-Reactive Protein (CRP) and Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR).
  - Serum Muscle Enzymes & Chemistry: Creatine Phosphokinase (CPK) is markedly elevated in Group A streptococcal necrotizing myositis, rhabdomyolysis, viral myositis, and clostridial gas gangrene. Interestingly, serum CPK levels may be surprisingly normal or only mildly elevated in uncomplicated staphylococcal pyomyositis despite gross muscle destruction. Elevated blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine (azotemia / acute kidney injury), myoglobinuria, and metabolic acidosis with elevated lactate.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Plain Radiography (X-rays): Demonstrates focal soft tissue swelling, skeletal abnormalities (osteomyelitis, fractures), and tissue gas (extensive gaseous dissection tracking along muscle and fascial planes in clostridial gas gangrene or Aeromonas myonecrosis).
  - Ultrasonography (US): Excellent bedside tool; demonstrates muscle enlargement and hypoechoic areas in early pyomyositis, and confirms focal fluid collections / abscesses with internal echoes in the suppurative stage. Also assesses hip joint effusions.
  - Computed Tomography (CT) with Contrast: Highly accessible; reveals muscle enlargement, loss of normal muscle architecture, low-density central fluid collections with rim enhancement, and subcutaneous/fascial fat stranding. Ideal for guiding percutaneous needle catheter drainage of deep pelvic/psoas abscesses.
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): The single most sensitive and detailed modality for muscle infections; demonstrates focal muscle enlargement, slight T1-signal increase with gadolinium rim enhancement, diffuse high T2-signal intensity of involved muscle, and central hyperintense fluid collections. Essential for detecting early non-suppurative myositis and distinguishing myositis from necrotizing fasciitis or cellulitis.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Gram Stain: Direct Gram stain of purulent muscle aspirate or wound exudate:
    - Pyomyositis: Gram-positive cocci in clusters (S. aureus) or chains (streptococci).
    - Gas Gangrene: Large, thick, gram-positive bacilli with blunt ends and a striking paucity of PMN leukocytes. Subterminal spores suggest C. septicum.
  - Cultures: Aerobic and anaerobic cultures of pus, tissue biopsy, and blood. Liquid anaerobic cultures showing "stormy fermentation" (rapid gas production) within 6 hours suggest C. perfringens. Blood cultures are positive in 5% to 35% of pyomyositis cases, up to 15% of C. perfringens gas gangrene, and nearly 100% of C. septicum and Group A streptococcal myositis.
  - Histopathology & Biopsy: Frozen-section or routine biopsy of muscle demonstrates coagulation necrosis, gas cavities, myocyte destruction, and presence/absence of leukocytic infiltration. Skin punch biopsy in neutropenic candidal myositis demonstrates yeasts in blood vessels and pseudohyphae in muscle. Muscle biopsy in trichinosis reveals encysted larvae.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - Direct Surgical Exploration & Muscle Inspection: Direct operative exploration with incision of the muscle fascia and visual/mechanical inspection of skeletal muscle is the definitive gold standard for myonecrosis. Non-viable muscle appears pale/discolored, fails to contract upon mechanical or electrical stimulation, and does not bleed when cut.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Hemodynamic & Critical Care Resuscitation: Immediate, aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation, blood transfusions (to maintain hematocrit against intravascular hemolysis), and vasopressor support in an Intensive Care Unit (ICU) setting for septic shock and toxic shock syndrome.
  - Renal Protection: Aggressive hydration and urinary alkalinization for myoglobinuric acute kidney injury secondary to severe rhabdomyolysis or myonecrosis; short-term hemodialysis if acute renal failure supervenes.
  - Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy (HBO): Controversial adjunct in gas gangrene; elevated partial pressures of oxygen reduce clostridial replication and suppress alpha-toxin expression. Most applicable for extensive truncal or paraspinal gas gangrene where complete surgical excision is impossible. Must NEVER delay immediate surgical exploration.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Empiric Pyomyositis Regimen:
    - Outpatient / Mild Stage 1: Oral Clindamycin, Cephalexin, Dicloxacillin, or TMP-SMX.
    - Hospitalized / Severe / Immunocompromised: IV Vancomycin (15-20 mg/kg IV every 8-12 hours, targeting S. aureus including MRSA) PLUS IV Piperacillin-Tazobactam (3.375-4.5 g IV every 6 hours) or IV Meropenem (1 g IV every 8 hours) to cover gram-negative bacilli and anaerobes.
  - Empiric Gas Gangrene / Clostridial Myonecrosis Regimen:
    - High-dose IV Penicillin G (2-3 million units IV every 2-3 hours, or 24 million units/day) PLUS IV Clindamycin (600-900 mg IV every 6-8 hours). Clindamycin suppresses toxin production and overcomes the Eagle effect.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Penicillin-Allergic Patients:
    - Gas Gangrene: IV Clindamycin (600-900 mg IV every 6-8 hours), IV Metronidazole (500 mg IV every 6 hours), or IV Carbapenem (Meropenem / Imipenem).
    - Pyomyositis: IV Linezolid (600 mg IV every 12 hours) or IV Daptomycin (6-8 mg/kg IV once daily) as alternatives to Vancomycin.
  - Pathogen-Specific Regimens:
    - Confirmed Group A Streptococcal Necrotizing Myositis: High-dose IV Penicillin G PLUS IV Clindamycin.
    - Aeromonas hydrophila Myonecrosis: IV Ciprofloxacin (400 mg IV every 12 hours) or IV Ceftazidime / Cefepime PLUS IV Gentamicin.
    - Parasitic Myositis (Trichinosis): Oral Albendazole (400 mg twice daily) or Mebendazole PLUS short-course systemic corticosteroids (Prednisone) for severe inflammatory symptoms.
    - Fungal Myositis: IV Amphotericin B or Voriconazole / Isavuconazole for Aspergillus, Fusarium, or Mucormycosis.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Operative Drainage: Drainage of all established muscle abscesses is mandatory. Selection between CT/ultrasound-guided percutaneous catheter drainage versus open surgical drainage depends on abscess size, multiloculation, and location.
  - Surgical Débridement & Fasciotomy: Emergency surgical exploration with extensive longitudinal fasciotomy, complete excision of nonviable discolored muscle, and wound débridement for gas gangrene, Group A streptococcal myositis, or Aeromonas myonecrosis. Wounds are left open; serial re-exploration (second-look surgery) at 24 hours is standard.
  - Decompressive Fasciotomy for Compartment Syndrome: Urgent emergency fasciotomy when pyomyositis or streptococcal myositis causes acute compartment syndrome (e.g., anterior tibial or forearm compartments). High-level limb amputation may be required for unsalvageable extremity destruction.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Septic Shock & Toxic Shock Syndrome: Mediated by staphylococcal or streptococcal superantigens or clostridial alpha-toxin, leading to capillary leak, refractory hypotension, ARDS, and multiorgan failure.
  - Acute Kidney Injury & Myoglobinuria: Secondary to massive rhabdomyolysis or clostridial intravascular hemolysis.
  - Compartment Syndrome: Increased tissue pressure within rigid muscle compartments secondary to edema and suppuration, requiring urgent decompressive fasciotomy.
  - Metastatic Abscesses & Septic Emboli: Deep vein thrombosis, septic pulmonary emboli, metastatic brain/visceral abscesses, or secondary endocarditis.
  - High Mortality: Gas gangrene carries a 20% to 30% mortality in traumatic cases and 67% to 100% in spontaneous C. septicum cases; Group A streptococcal myositis carries an 80% to 100% mortality.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Muscle Fibrotic Scarring & Functional Disability: Extensive muscle loss or delayed surgical drainage leads to severe tissue fibrosis, joint contractures, limb weakness, and permanent gait or upper extremity disability.
  - Amputation Disability: Permanent physical impairment following therapeutic limb amputation.
  - Recurrent Pyomyositis: Frequently observed in patients with advanced HIV/AIDS or persistent primary immunodeficiencies.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Stormy fermentation (rapid gas production in liquid anaerobic cultures of C. perfringens within 6 hours).
  - Eagle effect (reduced bactericidal efficacy of penicillin against high-density stationary-phase bacteria, overcome by adding protein-synthesis-inhibiting clindamycin).
  - Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL) (staphylococcal cytotoxin strongly linked to invasive MRSA pyomyositis and osteomyelitis).
  - Locus minoris resistentiae (area of lowered local tissue resistance created by minor blunt muscle trauma, predisposing to bacteremic seeding).
  - Bronze discoloration (classic skin hue preceding bulla formation in clostridial gas gangrene).
  - Acute benign childhood myositis (post-influenzal B calf pain and gait difficulty in young children).
  - Nurse cells (modified skeletal muscle myocytes harboring encysted Trichinella larvae).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Normal CPK in Gross Pyomyositis: Unlike viral myositis, rhabdomyolysis, or streptococcal myositis where serum CPK is markedly elevated, serum CPK levels are frequently NORMAL in staphylococcal pyomyositis despite massive intramuscular abscess formation.
  - Absence of Inflammatory Leukocytes in Gas Gangrene: Microscopic examination of exudate or muscle biopsy in C. perfringens gas gangrene shows abundant thick bacilli but virtually NO polymorphonuclear leukocytes due to toxin-mediated leukocidins and vascular leukostasis.
  - Absence of Obvious Wounds in C. septicum Gangrene: Spontaneous gas gangrene occurs without any external trauma, originating instead from occult GI mucosal lesions (particularly colon adenocarcinoma) or neutropenic colitis.
  - Absence of Erythema in Stage 2 Pyomyositis: Overlying skin is warm and intact but typically lacks erythema or redness because the thick overlying deep fascia confines the intramuscular pus.
  - Diabetic Muscle Infarction Mimic: Aseptic ischemic necrosis of thigh muscles in poorly controlled diabetics presents with acute thigh pain and swelling, mimicking pyomyositis or necrotizing fasciitis; MRI demonstrates muscle edema without fluid collection, and surgical intervention should be avoided as it worsens outcomes.
