# Lymphadenitis and Lymphangitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:**
  - Lymphadenitis is an acute (suppurative) or chronic (often granulomatous) inflammation of lymph nodes. It may be restricted to a solitary node or a localized group of nodes draining a specific anatomical area (regional lymphadenitis), or become generalized during systemic bloodstream or lymphatic infections. Gross pathological features range from nonsuppurative and suppurative to necrotizing or caseous inflammation.
  - Lymphangitis is an inflammation of lymphatic channels, primarily within subcutaneous tissues. It manifests either as an acute process of bacterial origin spreading rapidly along lymphatic pathways, or as a chronic, indolent process of mycotic, mycobacterial, or filarial etiology.
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Anatomical / Regional Syndromes of Lymphadenitis:
     - Cervical Lymphadenitis: Unilateral or bilateral involvement of submandibular, anterior, or posterior cervical nodes.
     - Deep Neck Space & Retropharyngeal / Parapharyngeal Lymphadenitis: Focal nodal inflammation in deep neck compartments that may complicate oral/pharyngeal infections or mimic deep space abscesses.
     - Axillary Lymphadenitis: Suppurative adenitis draining arm/hand pyodermas or adverse localized reaction to BCG vaccination.
     - Subpectoral Lymphadenitis: Suppurative infection originating from thumb or interdigital web infections that drains to subpectoral nodes beneath the pectoralis major.
     - Epitrochlear Lymphadenitis: Nodal inflammation draining forearm or hand lesions, often mimicking olecranon bursitis or septic arthritis.
     - Suppurative Iliac Lymphadenitis: Infection of external and common iliac nodes in the anterior retroperitoneal space between the posterior peritoneum and psoas/iliacus fascia, draining the lower leg, lower abdominal wall, or perineum.
     - Acute Mediastinal Lymphadenitis: Thoracic nodal inflammation complicating bacterial mediastinitis, esophageal perforation, odontogenic spread, or inhalation anthrax.
     - Inguinal Buboes: Sexually transmitted (syphilis, lymphogranuloma venereum / LGV, chancroid) and non-sexually transmitted (plague, tularemia, cat-scratch disease, melioidosis, Group A streptococcal infection in homosexual men) regional node enlargement.
  2. Histopathological & Etiologic Categories:
     - Acute Suppurative Lymphadenitis: Characterized by PMN leukocytic infiltration, sinusoidal lining cell hyperplasia, microvascular congestion, and tissue liquefaction/abscess formation (predominantly S. aureus, S. pyogenes, oral anaerobes).
     - Chronic Granulomatous Lymphadenitis: Characterized by epithelioid histiocytes, Langhans giant cells, stellate abscesses, or caseation necrosis (e.g., M. tuberculosis, nontuberculous mycobacteria, Bartonella henselae, Francisella tularensis, Yersinia, fungi).
     - Generalized Lymphadenitis: Diffuse, non-suppurative enlargement of multiple lymph node chains associated with systemic infectious illnesses (HIV, secondary syphilis, infectious mononucleosis, toxoplasmosis, miliary TB, brucellosis, leptospirosis, leishmaniasis).
  3. Clinical Forms of Lymphangitis:
     - Acute Ascending Lymphangitis: Rapidly progressive red linear streaking tracing subcutaneous lymphatic channels toward regional nodes (principally S. pyogenes, S. aureus, Pasteurella multocida, Streptobacillus moniliformis).
     - Chronic Granulomatous / Nodular Lymphangitis (Lymphocutaneous Syndrome / Sporotrichoid Lymphangitis): Indolent, sequential formation of subcutaneous nodules along lymphatic channels (Sporothrix schenckii, Mycobacterium marinum, Mycobacterium kansasii, Nocardia brasiliensis/asteroides, Leishmania, Francisella tularensis, S. aureus botryomycosis).
     - Filarial Lymphangitis: Recurrent lymphangitis and lymphadenitis caused by adult Wuchereria bancrofti or Brugia malayi infestation within lymphatic vessels.
     - Pseudolymphangitis: Non-infectious linear inflammatory reactions produced by exogenous phytophotodermatitis (e.g., coumarins in lime rinds/juice) or chemical irritants draining along skin planes.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Age Distribution & Demographics:
     - Acute suppurative lymphadenitis is most common in young children (preschool age), whereas granulomatous lymphadenitis occurs across all age groups. Suppurative iliac lymphadenitis affects children, young adults, and older individuals.
     - Nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) cervicofacial lymphadenitis occurs predominantly in otherwise healthy children aged 1 to 5 years (annual incidence 1 to 5 per 100,000 children).
     - Scrofula (tuberculous lymphadenitis) is common in resource-limited countries and in immigrants, Native Americans, Latinos, and HIV-infected individuals in industrialized nations.
  2. Portals of Entry:
     - Disruption of epidermal or mucosal barriers via minor cutaneous trauma, lacerations, abrasions, insect or flea/tick/mite bites, animal scratches or bites (cats, dogs, rats), fish bone or knife puncture wounds (tilapia handling), dermatophytosis / tinea pedis (interdigital web infection), primary pyodermas (impetigo, furuncles, ecthyma), or mucosal breakdown (tonsillitis, periodontitis, proctitis). Intravesical BCG instillation for bladder cancer predisposes to BCG iliac lymphadenitis.
  3. Systemic Comorbidities & Immunocompromise:
     - Primary Phagocyte & Immunological Defects: Chronic Granulomatous Disease (CGD, predisposing to severe suppurative lymphadenitis caused by catalase-positive pathogens like S. aureus, Serratia, Klebsiella, Salmonella, Pseudomonas, B. cepacia, Aspergillus, Nocardia, BCG, Granulobacter bethesdensis, Acidomonas methanolica), Hyper-IgE (Job) syndrome, early complement (C1r, C3) deficiencies.
     - Acquired Immunodeficiency: HIV infection/AIDS (predisposing to mycobacterial lymphadenitis, Cryptococcus, Histoplasma, Bartonella, CMV, Kaposi sarcoma, Castleman disease, lymphoma, leishmaniasis, P. jirovecii), anti-interferon-gamma autoantibodies (predisposing to severe NTM and fungal lymphadenitis in Asian/African adults), neutropenia following oral mucositis in leukemia patients, organ transplantation (predisposing to post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder / PTLD driven by EBV).
  4. Environmental, Occupational, and Animal Exposures:
     - Contact with cats/kittens (Bartonella henselae), sheep/livestock handling (Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis), swimming pools/fish tanks/brackish water (Mycobacterium marinum), gardening/sphagnum moss/barberry thorns (Sporothrix schenckii), outdoor activities/fleas/wildlife in endemic regions (Yersinia pestis, Francisella tularensis, Coxiella burnetii), and mosquito exposure in tropical regions (filarial lymphangitis).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Acute Suppurative Lymphadenitis & Acute Lymphangitis:
    - Staphylococcus aureus (both MSSA and MRSA; responsible for 40% to 50% of acute suppurative cervical adenitis cases in contemporary pediatric and adult series).
    - Group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes; historically the primary cause of acute suppurative adenitis, now accounting for 15% to 20% of cases, but remaining the principal cause of acute ascending lymphangitis).
    - Oral Anaerobes and Streptococci: Bacteroides species, Prevotella melaninogenica, Peptostreptococcus, Fusobacterium, and alpha-hemolytic streptococci (including Streptococcus intermedius and the S. anginosus group) in lymphadenitis originating from dental, oral, or pharyngeal foci (isolated in up to 40% of aspirates).
  - Subacute / Chronic Granulomatous Lymphadenitis & Chronic Lymphangitis:
    - Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (NTM): Mycobacterium avium-intracellulare complex (MAC, the predominant cause of cervicofacial lymphadenitis in healthy children in industrialized societies), M. scrofulaceum, M. bovis, M. interjectum, M. malmoense, M. haemophilum, M. xenopi, M. szulgai, M. lentiflavum, M. bohemicum.
    - Mycobacterium tuberculosis (the primary cause of scrofula in resource-limited nations, endemic regions, recent immigrants, and HIV-infected hosts).
    - Bartonella henselae (the causative agent of Cat-Scratch Disease).
    - Sporothrix schenckii (the primary cause of chronic nodular / sporotrichoid lymphangitis).
    - Mycobacterium marinum (swimming pool / fish tank granuloma).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Infants and Children: S. aureus (MSSA and MRSA), S. pyogenes, NTM (MAC in children aged 1 to 5 years), Bartonella henselae, oral anaerobes, BCG vaccine bacillus (axillary or iliac adenitis following BCG vaccination), and exanthematous/respiratory viruses (EBV, CMV, Adenovirus, HHV-6, HHV-7, Parvovirus B19, Measles, Rubella).
  - Adults and Older Adults: S. aureus, S. pyogenes, Groups B, C, and G streptococci, M. tuberculosis, Treponema pallidum (syphilis), Chlamydia trachomatis serovars L1-L3 (LGV), Haemophilus ducreyi (chancroid), Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis (in sheep handlers), Francisella tularensis, Yersinia pestis, Coxiella burnetii (Q fever), and filarial helminths (Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Immunocompromised / HIV / Neutropenic Hosts:
    - Fungi: Candida albicans, Aspergillus fumigatus, Cryptococcus neoformans, Histoplasma capsulatum, Pneumocystis jirovecii, Fusarium, Scedosporium species.
    - Bacteria & Mycobacteria: Disseminated MAC, M. tuberculosis, Nocardia species (N. brasiliensis, N. asteroides), Rhodococcus equi, Helicobacter cinaedi, Yersinia enterocolitica, Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, Granulobacter bethesdensis, Acidomonas methanolica, Rothia aeria, Chromobacterium violaceum.
    - Parasites & Protozoa: Leishmania donovani, Leishmania brasiliensis, Leishmania mexicana, Toxoplasma gondii, Trypanosoma brucei, Trypanosoma cruzi, Microsporidia (Trachipleistophora hominis).
  - Aquatic, Animal, and Vector-Borne Pathogens:
    - Pasteurella multocida (cat/dog bites causing lymphangitis or adenitis).
    - Streptobacillus moniliformis (rat-bite fever causing lymphangitis).
    - Vibrio vulnificus, Aeromonas hydrophila, Burkholderia pseudomallei (melioidosis), Bacillus anthracis (inhalation or cutaneous anthrax).
    - Cowpox virus.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Lymphatic Filtration and Tissue Inoculation: Lymph nodes serve as regional anatomical filters that trap bacteria, fungi, parasites, and cellular debris carried in lymph draining from inflamed or infected cutaneous, mucosal, or deep tissue sites. Pathogens enter lymphatics via direct traumatic inoculation (punctures, bites, scratches), spread from contiguous pyodermas (impetigo, furuncles, cellulitis), or extend from mucosal sites (pharyngitis, periodontitis, proctitis).
  - Ascending Lymphangitis: When a superficial dermal or subcutaneous infection is not locally contained, microorganisms invade lymphatic vessel walls. Inflammation spreads rapidly along the lymphatic vessel walls and surrounding tissue spaces toward regional lymph nodes, producing visible red linear streaks in overlying skin.
  - Retroperitoneal and Deep Space Extension: Infection draining from the lower extremities or pelvic organs into iliac, axillary, or subpectoral nodes can erode through lymph node capsules into adjacent fascial planes (e.g., the retroperitoneal space between the posterior peritoneum and psoas/iliacus fascia, subpectoral space, or retropharyngeal/parapharyngeal neck spaces), forming deep retroperitoneal or deep neck space abscesses.
  - Hematogenous Seeding: Less commonly, lymph nodes are infected via hematogenous seeding during systemic bloodstream infections (e.g., miliary TB, secondary syphilis, disseminated histoplasmosis or cryptococcosis, typhoid fever).
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Acute Suppurative Histopathology: Initial tissue response consists of sinusoidal lining cell swelling and hyperplasia, microvascular congestion, and dense infiltration of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs). Bacterial multiplication and neutrophilic lysosomal enzyme release cause parenchymal necrosis, tissue liquefaction, and purulent abscess formation within the node.
  - Granulomatous Histopathology:
    - Stellate Abscesses surrounded by Palisading Epithelioid Cells: Characteristic of Cat-Scratch Disease (Bartonella henselae), LGV (Chlamydia trachomatis), Tularemia (Francisella tularensis), Yersinia mesenteric lymphadenitis, and fungal lymphadenitis.
    - Caseation Necrosis: Typical of Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Histoplasma capsulatum, featuring central acellular caseous debris surrounded by epithelioid histiocytes, Langhans giant cells, and lymphocytes.
    - Noncaseating Granulomas: Observed in Sarcoidosis, NTM in immunocompetent hosts, and foreign body reactions (silicone breast/joint prostheses, metal debris).
    - Reactive Follicular Hyperplasia with Monocytoid B Cells: Characteristic of Toxoplasmosis (blurred germinal center margins, epithelioid histiocyte clusters, subcapsular/trabecular sinus distention by monocytoid B cells).
    - Syphilitic Histopathology: "Starry sky" pattern, prominent plasma cell infiltration, occlusive capillaritis, and endothelial swelling.
  - Vascular and Lymphatic Obstruction: Severe inflammation within lymph nodes and lymphatics causes lymphatic thrombosis, vessel obliteration, and perilymphatic edema. Healing can lead to permanent lymphatic scarring, lymphedema, and in severe recurrent cases, elephantiasis nostras verrucosa. Thrombosis of adjacent major veins (e.g., axillary, subclavian, or superior vena cava compression) can complicate severe regional lymphadenitis.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Acute Suppurative Lymphadenitis: Rapid onset of localized, painful, tender swelling in a regional lymph node basin (cervical, axillary, inguinal, epitrochlear), accompanied by high fever, chills, rigors, malaise, and limited motion of the involved body part (e.g., torticollis, painful arm elevation, or antalgic hip flexion/limp).
  - Acute Lymphangitis: Rapid development (within 24 to 48 hours) of painful red linear streaks extending proximally along an extremity from an inciting skin wound or bite toward regional lymph nodes, associated with high fever, chills, and systemic toxicity.
  - Chronic Granulomatous Lymphadenitis & Lymphangitis: Indolent, subacute, or painless enlargement of regional lymph nodes evolving over weeks to months (e.g., scrofula, NTM, cat-scratch disease), often with minimal fever or systemic symptoms, followed by skin discoloration, fluctuance, and spontaneous sinus tract drainage. In chronic nodular lymphangitis (sporotrichoid spread), painless or mildly tender subcutaneous nodules appear sequentially along lymphatic pathways.
  - Inguinal Buboes: Painful or hard, matted inguinal swellings following sexual exposure (syphilis, LGV, chancroid) or animal/flea exposure in endemic regions (plague, tularemia).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Acute Suppurative Lymphadenitis: Enlarged lymph node(s) (typically >= 3 cm in diameter), exquisitely tender, firm or frankly fluctuant mass with overlying erythema, warmth, and soft tissue edema. Overlying skin may break down and drain purulent material.
  - Specific Anatomical Findings:
    - Cervical Adenitis: Unilateral neck mass, walnut-sized or larger matted nodes, trismus/limited mouth opening, torticollis (head tilted away from affected side).
    - Axillary Adenitis: Prominent edema of shoulder, arm, axilla, supraclavicular fossa, and pectoral area; ipsilateral pleural effusion.
    - Subpectoral Adenitis: Swelling obscured by pectoralis major muscle; arm elevation causes muscle contraction and movement of mass (mimicking inferior muscle avulsion); downward dissection manifests as chest or abdominal wall cellulitis.
    - Suppurative Iliac Adenitis: Tightly flexed hip, ipsilateral rectus muscle spasm, tender posterolateral pelvic or inguinal mass, pain on hip extension (minimal pain on abduction/adduction), antalgic limp.
    - Inguinal Buboes: LGV presents with a matted, fixed, lobulated inguinal mass adherent to purplish overlying skin, multiple fistulous tracts, and the classic "groove sign" (linear depression created by nodes above and below the inguinal ligament). Chancroid buboes are exquisitely painful and rupture spontaneously. Syphilitic buboes are firm, painless, discrete, and non-suppurative. Plague buboes are massive, matted, extremely tender nodal clusters.
  - Acute Lymphangitis: Bright red linear streaks (a few millimeters to centimeters wide) tracing subcutaneous lymphatic channels proximal to an inoculation site, accompanied by tender regional lymphadenopathy and extremity edema.
  - Chronic Granulomatous / Nodular Lymphangitis:
    - Sporotrichoid Spread: Primary chancriform ulcer or erythematous papule at inoculation site (hand/finger) with a chain of firm or fluctuant, nodular subcutaneous lesions extending proximally along thickened, cord-like lymphatic channels.
    - NTM Cervicofacial Adenitis: Submandibular or anterior cervical enlargement with overlying violaceous/purplish skin discoloration ("thinning" of skin) and sinus tract formation.
    - Oculoglandular (Parinaud) Syndrome: Granulomatous nodular conjunctivitis with ipsilateral preauricular lymphadenopathy (Bartonella henselae, F. tularensis, Listeria, Sporothrix, Adenovirus).
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Red linear streaks extending proximally along an extremity toward regional lymph node basins (pathognomonic sign of acute ascending lymphangitis).
  - "Groove sign" of LGV: Linear depression separating matted inguinal lymph nodes above and below the inguinal ligament.
  - Chain of subcutaneous nodules extending proximally along lymphatic drainage pathways from a primary distal lesion (pathognomonic sporotrichoid / nodular lymphangitis pattern).
  - Parinaud Oculoglandular Syndrome: Unilateral granulomatous conjunctivitis with ipsilateral preauricular lymphadenitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Marked leukocytosis (WBC 12,000 to 25,000/mm3) with neutrophilia and bandemia (left shift) in acute suppurative lymphadenitis and acute lymphangitis. Mild leukopenia or monocytosis in Kikuchi-Fujimoto disease and viral lymphadenitis. Eosinophilia in filarial lymphangitis, parasitic infections (Toxocara, Trichinella), and Kimura disease.
  - Inflammatory Markers: Markedly elevated Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) and C-Reactive Protein (CRP) in pyogenic, tuberculous, and inflammatory (Kawasaki, Kikuchi, PFAPA) adenitis.
  - Immunological & Serologic Testing:
    - Serology for Bartonella henselae (IFA / ELISA), Toxoplasma gondii (IgG/IgM ELISA, Sabin-Feldman dye test, IgG avidity), Treponema pallidum (VDRL/RPR, FTA-ABS, TP-PA), Chlamydia trachomatis (LGV serology), Francisella tularensis, Coxiella burnetii, EBV, CMV, HIV antibodies/p24 antigen.
    - Interferon-Gamma Release Assays (IGRA) or Tuberculin Skin Test (PPD) for M. tuberculosis. Check for anti-IFN-gamma autoantibodies in adult Asian/African hosts with severe NTM or fungal lymphadenitis.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Ultrasonography (US): Excellent initial modality to differentiate firm nonsuppurative lymphadenopathy from drainable suppurative abscess collections, evaluate node size (> 3 cm), detect intranodal necrosis/fluctuance, and distinguish epitrochlear or popliteal lymphadenitis from olecranon bursitis, osteomyelitis, or septic arthritis.
  - Computed Tomography (CT) with Contrast: Essential for evaluating deep neck space (retropharyngeal, parapharyngeal), mediastinal, subpectoral, iliac retroperitoneal, and pelvic lymphadenitis. CT localizes fluid collections, detects fascial plane displacement, identifies rim-enhancing necrotic lymph nodes (characteristic of TB and NTM), and differentiates drainable abscesses from solid cellulitis/phlegmon.
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): Highly detailed evaluation of soft tissue, fascial, and psoas/iliacus muscle involvement in iliac lymphadenitis and deep soft tissue infections.
  - Positron Emission Tomography (PET / PET-CT): 18F-FDG PET-CT identifies focal or deep parenchymal lymphadenitis in chronic Q fever (Coxiella burnetii) and localizes hypermetabolic nodes in TB, sarcoidosis, or prosthetic-wear reactions.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Percutaneous Fine-Needle Aspiration (FNA) / Nodal Aspirate:
    - Gram Stain & Aerobic/Anaerobic Culture: Direct Gram stain and culture of purulent aspirates yield pyogenic pathogens (S. aureus, S. pyogenes, oral anaerobes).
    - Acid-Fast Bacilli (AFB) Stain & Mycobacterial Culture: Ziehl-Neelsen / Kinyoun stain and mycobacterial culture (Löwenstein-Jensen / liquid media at 37°C for M. tuberculosis, 30°C for M. marinum) recover mycobacteria in 60% to 90% of cases.
    - Fungal Stains & Cultures: Calcofluor white, PAS, or silver stains for Sporothrix, Cryptococcus, Histoplasma, Aspergillus, Candida.
  - Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) & Molecular Assays: Highly sensitive and specific on aspirates or tissue biopsies for Bartonella henselae, M. tuberculosis (GeneXpert), NTM species identification (16S rRNA gene sequencing), Chlamydia trachomatis (LGV), Neisseria meningitidis, Yersinia, Francisella tularensis, Leishmania.
  - Histopathology (Excisional / Incisional Biopsy):
    - Warthin-Starry silver stain for Bartonella henselae and Treponema pallidum.
    - Demonstrates stellate granulomas (cat-scratch, LGV, tularemia), caseating granulomas (TB), noncaseating granulomas (sarcoidosis, NTM), necrotizing crescentic histiocytic lesions without neutrophils (Kikuchi disease), or "starry sky" plasma cell infiltration (syphilis).
  - Blood Cultures: Positive in acute pyogenic lymphangitis, bacteremic streptococcal/staphylococcal lymphadenitis, plague, tularemia, Brucella, Salmonella typhi, and disseminated mycobacterial/fungal infections in immunocompromised hosts.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - Microbiological & Histopathological Examination of Excised Lymph Node / Tissue Biopsy: Excisional lymph node biopsy with combined histopathological analysis and comprehensive microbiological culture/PCR testing represents the definitive gold standard for establishing etiology in subacute, chronic, or refractory lymphadenitis.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Systemic Stabilization: Hospitalization, intravenous fluid resuscitation, and parenteral antimicrobials for toxic patients with acute suppurative lymphadenitis (axillary, deep neck space, iliac, mediastinal) or acute ascending lymphangitis complicated by bacteremia or septic shock.
  - Local Measures: Application of warm, moist compresses to affected node basins or lymphangitic pathways to promote localization and ease pain. Immobilization and elevation of affected extremities above heart level to facilitate lymphatic drainage.
  - Supportive Care for Specific Syndromes: Single-dose systemic corticosteroids (Prednisone 1 to 2 mg/kg) promptly abort febrile attacks in PFAPA syndrome. IVIG and high-dose aspirin for Kawasaki disease.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Mild-to-Moderate Acute Pyogenic Lymphadenitis & Lymphangitis (Outpatient Oral Therapy):
    - When S. aureus (MSSA) and Streptococci are suspected: Oral Cephalexin (500 mg every 6 hours or 25-50 mg/kg/day in children), Dicloxacillin (500 mg every 6 hours), or Amoxicillin-Clavulanic acid (875/125 mg twice daily).
    - When Odontogenic / Pharyngeal Source is suspected: Oral Penicillin V (500 mg every 6 hours) or Amoxicillin (500 mg every 8 hours) or Amoxicillin-Clavulanic acid.
    - Community-Acquired MRSA Coverage: Oral Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX, 1 to 2 double-strength tablets twice daily) or Doxycycline (100 mg twice daily).
  - Moderately Severe to Severe Acute Lymphadenitis / Lymphangitis (Inpatient Parenteral Therapy):
    - Parenteral Cefazolin (1.0 to 1.5 g IV every 8 hours), IV Ampicillin-Sulbactam (1.5 to 3.0 g IV every 6 hours), or IV Ceftriaxone (1 to 2 g IV daily).
    - High-Risk MRSA / Acutely Ill / Hospitalized: IV Vancomycin (15 to 20 mg/kg IV every 8 to 12 hours) or IV Linezolid (600 mg IV every 12 hours). High-dose IV Penicillin G (2 to 4 million units IV every 4 hours) for confirmed streptococcal lymphadenitis or subpectoral/axillary lymphadenitis.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Penicillin-Allergic Patients:
    - Mild/Moderate: Oral Clindamycin (300 to 450 mg orally every 6 to 8 hours) or oral Macrolides (Azithromycin 500 mg day 1, then 250 mg daily; or Clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily - noting macrolide resistance among streptococci/staphylococci).
    - Severe: IV Clindamycin (600 to 900 mg IV every 6 to 8 hours) or IV Vancomycin.
  - Pathogen-Specific Directed Regimens:
    - Cat-Scratch Disease (Bartonella henselae): Azithromycin (500 mg PO day 1, then 250 mg daily for 4 days), Ciprofloxacin, Gentamicin, Rifampin, or Doxycycline. Prolonged therapy (3 months) with Erythromycin or Doxycycline +/- Rifampin for Bacillary Angiomatosis / Peliosis in AIDS.
    - Lymphogranuloma Venereum (LGV): Oral Doxycycline (100 mg twice daily for 21 days) or oral Erythromycin (500 mg four times daily for 21 days).
    - Tuberculous Lymphadenitis (M. tuberculosis): Standard 6-month antituberculous regimen (Rifampin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol for 2 months, followed by Rifampin and Isoniazid for 4 months).
    - Nontuberculous Mycobacterial (NTM) Adenitis: Complete surgical excision is primary therapy. Adjunctive multidrug therapy with Clarithromycin/Azithromycin plus Rifampin or Ethambutol for extensive deep disease or compromised hosts.
    - Sporotrichosis (Sporothrix schenckii): Oral Itraconazole (200 mg daily) for 3 to 6 months; saturated solution of potassium iodide (SSKI) as alternative.
    - Swimming Pool Granuloma (Mycobacterium marinum): Oral Doxycycline, Clarithromycin, or TMP-SMX monotherapy for mild cases; Combination Rifampin plus Ethambutol for severe/sporotrichoid disease.
    - Animal / Human Bites (Pasteurella multocida, Eikenella): Amoxicillin-Clavulanic acid (875/125 mg PO twice daily) or IV Ampicillin-Sulbactam.
    - Plague (Yersinia pestis) & Tularemia (Francisella tularensis): IV Streptomycin, Gentamicin, or oral Doxycycline / Ciprofloxacin.
    - Filarial Lymphangitis (W. bancrofti): Diethylcarbamazine (DEC) or Ivermectin plus Albendazole (targeting Wolbachia endosymbionts with Doxycycline).
    - Chronic Prophylaxis for Recurrent Lymphangitis / Cellulitis: Chronic suppressive Penicillin V (250 to 500 mg PO twice daily), monthly IM Benzathine Penicillin G (1.2 million units), or oral Erythromycin (250 mg once or twice daily).
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Percutaneous Needle Aspiration: Favored initial diagnostic and therapeutic intervention for fluctuant acute pyogenic lymphadenitis (cervical, epitrochlear) and fluctuant Cat-Scratch Disease nodes (relieves pain and avoids sinus tract formation). Ultrasound-guided percutaneous catheter drainage for deep retroperitoneal iliac or psoas abscesses.
  - Incision and Drainage: Indicated for frank, fluctuant, superficial pyogenic nodal abscesses when needle aspiration is inadequate. Note: Simple incision and drainage is SATISFACTORY for bacterial or B. henselae adenitis, but SHOULD BE AVOIDED in NTM or tuberculous lymphadenitis due to high risk of chronic sinus tract formation, non-healing wounds, and unsightly scarring.
  - Complete Excisional Biopsy: Preferred surgical approach for Nontuberculous Mycobacterial (NTM) cervicofacial lymphadenitis (achieves > 90% cure rate and avoids chronic drainage). Also indicated for diagnostic evaluation of persistent non-suppurative lymphadenopathy suspected to be lymphoma, fungal, or atypical granulomatous infection.
  - Surgical Management of Deep Space Infections: Operative incision, drainage, and débridement for suppurative mediastinal lymphadenitis, deep neck space abscesses, or subpectoral abscesses.
  - Tonsillectomy for PFAPA Syndrome: Induces permanent symptom remission in > 95% of children with periodic fever, aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis, and cervical adenitis syndrome.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Suppuration & Abscess Formation: Progression of acute adenitis to frank intra-nodal and perilymphatic abscesses (occurs in up to 20% of acute pediatric cervical adenitis).
  - Deep Space Extension & Rupture: Breakdown of nodal capsules leading to retropharyngeal or parapharyngeal space abscesses, subpectoral abscesses, psoas/iliacus retroperitoneal abscesses, or suppurative mediastinitis.
  - Vascular Compression & Thrombosis: Thrombosis of adjacent major veins (axillary, subclavian, internal jugular vein / Lemierre-like syndrome) or Superior Vena Cava (SVC) obstruction secondary to massive mediastinal lymphadenitis.
  - Spontaneous Sinus Tract Formation: Chronic purulent or caseous skin drainage and fistulae formation, particularly in untreated NTM, scrofula, LGV, and actinomycosis.
  - Systemic Dissemination & Septic Shock: Bacteremia, septicemia, metastatic abscesses, and septic shock complicating severe pyogenic, plague, tularemia, or anthrax lymphadenitis.
  - Extranodal Organ Involvement in Cat-Scratch Disease: Encephalitis, neuroretinitis, aseptic meningitis, osteomyelitis, hepatosplenic granulomas, and thrombocytopenia.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Chronic Lymphedema & Elephantiasis: Permanent lymphatic vessel destruction and fibrosis following acute or recurrent lymphangitis/lymphadenitis, leading to persistent extremity edema. In severe cases, this progresses to Elephantiasis Nostras Verrucosa (temperate-zone non-filarial elephantiasis) or filarial elephantiasis of limbs and scrotum.
  - Cosmetic Deformity & Tissue Scarring: Unsightly hypertrophic scarring, skin tethering, and discoloration following spontaneous sinus tract drainage or improper incision of mycobacterial lymphadenitis.
  - Chronic Relapsing Lymphadenitis: Recurrent febrile nodal flares over years (e.g., PFAPA syndrome lasting 4 to 5 years, relapsing C. pseudotuberculosis adenitis).
  - Joint Contractures & Functional Impairment: Persistent hip flexion contractures or arm motion restriction following iliac, subpectoral, or epitrochlear suppurative lymphadenitis.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Groove sign" (linear depression dividing matted inguinal nodes above and below the inguinal ligament in Lymphogranuloma Venereum / LGV).
  - "Peau d'orange" and "peau violacée" (purplish skin thinning and discoloration over NTM lymphadenitis).
  - "Scrofula" (classic historical term for tuberculous cervical lymphadenitis).
  - "Peanut butter / caseous pus" (thick material aspirated from tuberculous lymph nodes).
  - "Starry sky" histopathology (prominent plasma cells, capillaritis, and endothelial swelling in syphilitic lymphadenitis).
  - "Stellate abscesses" (central neutrophilic necrosis surrounded by palisading epithelioid cells in Cat-Scratch Disease, LGV, Tularemia, and Yersinia).
  - "Sporotrichoid spread" (sequential subcutaneous nodules along lymphatic drainage lines).
  - "Parinaud oculoglandular syndrome" (granulomatous conjunctivitis with ipsilateral preauricular lymphadenopathy).
  - "Elephantiasis nostras verrucosa" (grotesque chronic non-filarial lymphatic enlargement and verrucous pachydermia following recurrent lymphangitis).
  - "Phytophotodermatitis" (linear coumarin photo-reaction mimicking superficial lymphangitis).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Incision & Drainage Exception: Simple incision and drainage is standard for pyogenic bacterial abscesses, but is CONTRAINDICATED / DISCOURAGED in Nontuberculous Mycobacterial (NTM) lymphadenitis due to high rates of chronic sinus tract formation and non-healing wounds; complete excisional biopsy is preferred.
  - Testicular / Scrotal Sparing vs. Filarial Involvement: Unlike Fournier gangrene where testes are spared, filarial lymphangitis/lymphadenitis directly involves the spermatic cord, epididymis, and scrotum, leading to hydrocele and massive scrotal elephantiasis.
  - Absence of Primary Skin Lesions in Plague / Tularemia: Classic plague buboes typically present as massive, exquisitely tender nodal clusters WITHOUT an obvious primary insect bite lesion at onset.
  - Sterile / Non-infectious Lymphadenitis Mimics:
    - Kikuchi-Fujimoto Disease (histiocytic necrotizing lymphadenitis): Self-limited cervical adenopathy with fever in young women, showing crescentic histiocytes and karyorrhectic debris WITHOUT neutrophils; does NOT respond to antibiotics.
    - Rosai-Dorfman Disease (sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy).
    - Kawasaki Disease: Acute febrile mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome in children presenting with non-suppurative unilateral cervical adenopathy that can precede exanthem/conjunctivitis and mimic bacterial adenitis.
    - PFAPA Syndrome: Periodic fever, aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis, and cervical adenitis in children; aborted by single-dose prednisone or cured by tonsillectomy (> 95%).
    - Prosthetic-Wear Lymphadenopathy: Enlarged regional lymph nodes showing noncaseating granulomas or histiocytic necrotizing reaction filled with metal, polyethylene, or silicone particles from joint or breast implants; 18F-FDG PET-CT avid, mimicking lymphoma or metastatic cancer.
    - Anti-IFN-Gamma Autoantibodies: Adult-onset immunodeficiency in Asian/African individuals manifesting as severe, recurrent NTM, fungal, or Histoplasma lymphadenitis in previously healthy hosts.
