# Pharyngitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Pharyngitis is defined as the clinical triad of sore throat, fever, and pharyngeal inflammation characterized by pharyngeal erythema and edema, with or without exudates, vesicles, or ulcerations. Although commonly presenting as a primary infectious disease of the tonsillopharynx, pharyngitis and sore throat may also manifest as part of a generalized upper respiratory tract infection or as a prominent feature of systemic disorders such as primary acute retroviral syndrome (HIV infection).
- **Major Classifications:** Classifications include Viral Pharyngitis (the most prevalent, benign, self-limited form associated with common upper respiratory viruses, enteroviruses, or herpesviruses); Bacterial Pharyngitis (led by Group A Beta-Hemolytic Streptococcal (GAS) pharyngitis/tonsillitis, Non-Group A Streptococcal pharyngitis (Group C and Group G Streptococci), and anaerobe-mediated Vincent angina); Specific Clinical Syndromes (Pharyngoconjunctival Fever caused by adenovirus, Herpangina and Hand-Foot-Mouth Disease (HFM) caused by enteroviruses, Infectious Mononucleosis (IM) caused by Epstein-Barr virus or cytomegalovirus, Diphtheria caused by Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Gonococcal Pharyngitis caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and Endemic Adolescent Pharyngitis caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum or Arcanobacterium haemolyticum); and Exudative versus Non-Exudative Pharyngitis.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:** Pharyngitis is one of the most frequent outpatient complaints in both children and adults, accounting for 6.2 to 9.7 million annual visits to primary care practices, clinics, and emergency departments for children and over 5 million annual visits for adults in the United States, with family studies documenting an annual incidence of sore throat in 16% of adults and 41% of children. The highest disease burden occurs in children and young adults, with approximately 50% of cases identified between 5 and 24 years of age and the highest overall number of cases occurring in school-aged children (5 to 18 years). In temperate climates, overall pharyngitis and GAS pharyngitis peak during winter and early spring, corresponding to seasonal activity of respiratory viruses. Group A Streptococcus accounts for 25% to 40% of pharyngitis in children and 5% to 17% in adults (occurring at a rate of 0.14 cases per child-year in developed nations). Risk factors include crowded living or assembly settings (schools, daycare centers, military barracks, college dormitories, summer camps), exposure to infected household or classroom contacts, consumption of contaminated food or unpasteurized milk products (associated with Group C and G streptococcal epidemics), cigarette smoking (identified as a major risk factor for symptomatic Mycoplasma pneumoniae respiratory outbreaks), and high-risk sexual exposures (for Neisseria gonorrhoeae pharyngitis).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):** Respiratory viruses are the single most common cause of acute pharyngitis, accounting for 25% to 45% (and up to 60%) of overall cases. Primary viral etiologies include Rhinovirus and Adenovirus (the most prevalent viral drivers, identified in 12% to 27% of overall cases), Coronaviruses, Enteroviruses (Coxsackieviruses A and B, Echoviruses, Enterovirus 71, Enterovirus D68), Influenza A and B viruses, Parainfluenza viruses, Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), Human Metapneumovirus, Human Bocavirus, Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Herpes Simplex Virus types 1 and 2 (HSV-1, HSV-2), and Primary Human Immunodeficiency Virus Type 1 (HIV-1). The single most important bacterial pathogen is Group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes). Non-GAS bacterial pathogens include Non-Group A Streptococci (Group C and Group G Streptococci, specifically Streptococcus dysgalactiae subsp. equisimilis and Streptococcus equi subsp. zooepidemicus), Fusobacterium necrophorum (identified in up to 20.5% of young adults with pharyngitis), Arcanobacterium haemolyticum, Mixed anaerobes (Vincent angina), Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Yersinia pestis, Francisella tularensis, and Treponema pallidum. Atypical bacterial agents include Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and Chlamydia psittaci.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:** In infants and young children (under 5 years of age), viral pathogens heavily dominate (Adenovirus, Enteroviruses causing herpangina or Hand-Foot-Mouth disease, Rhinovirus, RSV, Parainfluenza), while GAS pharyngitis is relatively uncommon in children under 3 years. In school-aged children (5 to 15 years), Group A Streptococcus reaches its peak prevalence (accounting for 30% to 40% of pharyngitis cases), occurring alongside Adenovirus, EBV, and Enteroviruses. In adolescents and young adults (15 to 24 years), key pathogens shift to include Fusobacterium necrophorum (13.5% to 20.5% prevalence), Epstein-Barr Virus (Infectious Mononucleosis), Group A, C, and G Streptococci, Arcanobacterium haemolyticum, Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1 and HSV-2 pharyngitis), Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Primary HIV infection. In older adults (over 65 years), viral upper respiratory tract pathogens (Rhinovirus, Coronavirus, Influenza) predominate, while deep space suppurative infections (peritonsillar and parapharyngeal space abscesses) may present with subtle or atypical signs, frequently in the setting of underlying immunocompromising conditions.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:** Atypical bacterial pathogens include Mycoplasma pneumoniae and Chlamydia pneumoniae (both linked to recurrent pharyngitis in pediatric populations), Chlamydia psittaci, and Arcanobacterium haemolyticum (characteristically causing pharyngitis accompanied by a scarlatiniform rash). Opportunistic, rare, or non-bacterial pathogens causing pharyngitis or laryngeal/pharyngeal ulceration include Candida species, Cytomegalovirus, Herpes Simplex Virus in immunocompromised hosts, Histoplasma capsulatum, Coccidioides immitis, Blastomyces dermatitidis, Cryptococcus neoformans, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Nontuberculous Mycobacteria, and Parvovirus B19.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:** Respiratory viruses and bacterial pathogens enter the host via inhaled aerosolized respiratory droplets, direct contact with infectious oral/nasal secretions, or oral ingestion of contaminated food and milk products (specifically Group C and Group G streptococci). Pathogens adhere to non-ciliated and ciliated epithelial cells of the oropharynx and tonsillar crypts using specialized surface adhesins (such as streptococcal pili, fibronectin-binding proteins, and lipoteichoic acid). In viral pharyngitis, viral replication or the host mucosal immune response triggers local induction and release of inflammatory peptide mediators, specifically bradykinin and prostaglandins. Bradykinin acts directly on pharyngeal sensory nerve endings to produce the sensation of sore throat. Group A Streptococcus utilizes virulence factors including M protein, hyaluronic acid capsule, C5a peptidase, streptolysins O and S, and pyrogenic exotoxins to evade host phagocytosis, hydrolyze extracellular matrix components (via hyaluronidase, streptokinase, and DNases), and induce intense mucosal inflammation. Fusobacterium necrophorum invades the mucosal barrier of tonsillar crypts, leading to mucosal necrosis, peritonsillar suppuration, and contiguous invasion into the parapharyngeal space with septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:** The host tissue response is characterized by intense microvascular engorgement, marked mucosal edema, dense polymorphonuclear neutrophil infiltration, and mucosal epithelial desquamation, resulting in visible pharyngeal erythema, tonsillar hypertrophy, and purulent exudates. GAS pyrogenic exotoxins function as superantigens, inducing systemic capillary dilation and the characteristic scarlatiniform exanthem. Molecular mimicry between streptococcal M protein or capsular antigens and human host tissues (cardiac myosin, tropomyosin, and basal ganglia neuronal antigens) triggers autoimmune-mediated tissue destruction in susceptible hosts, resulting in Acute Rheumatic Fever (ARF). Deposition of streptococcal immune complexes within renal glomeruli triggers poststreptococcal acute glomerulonephritis.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):** Sudden onset of severe sore throat (odynophagia), pain or difficulty with swallowing (dysphagia), fever, headache, chills, malaise, and gastrointestinal symptoms including nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain (frequently observed in pediatric GAS pharyngitis). In viral pharyngitis, sore throat is characteristically accompanied by conjunctivitis, coryza, nasal congestion, cough, hoarseness, diarrhea, or discrete oral ulcerative lesions. In Infectious Mononucleosis (EBV), symptoms are subacute in onset and feature severe sore throat, prominent fatigue (noted in 65% to 75% of patients), fever, headache, myalgia, arthralgia, and abdominal fullness. In Fusobacterium necrophorum infection or Lemierre syndrome, initial pharyngeal symptoms improve after approximately 4 days, followed by the sudden onset of severe rigors, high spiking fevers, ipsilateral neck pain, and profound prostration.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):** Pharyngeal vascular engorgement and mucosal edema; tonsillar enlargement; patchy, discrete, or confluent gray-white or yellow-white exudates covering the posterior pharynx and tonsillar pillars; soft palate petechiae; uvular erythema and edema; tender, enlarged anterior cervical lymph nodes (particularly at the angle of the jaw in GAS pharyngitis); diffuse scarlatiniform "sandpaper-like" rash sparing the palms and soles (in GAS scarlet fever or Arcanobacterium haemolyticum); bilateral conjunctival injection (in Adenovirus pharyngoconjunctival fever); discrete 1-to-4 mm erythematous-based vesicles or ulcerations on the tonsillar pillars, uvula, soft palate, or posterior pharynx (in herpangina); erythematous-based vesicles in the pharynx accompanied by vesicular lesions on the hands, feet, and buttocks (in Hand-Foot-Mouth disease); posterior cervical or generalized lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly (35%), and hepatomegaly (in EBV mononucleosis); and a dark gray, leathery pseudomembrane covering the tonsils or pharynx that bleeds upon attempts to dislodge it (in Corynebacterium diphtheriae).
- **Pathognomonic Features:** The classic clinical constellation of tonsillar or pharyngeal exudates, tender anterior cervical lymphadenopathy, fever or history of fever, and absence of cough (highly predictive of Group A Streptococcal pharyngitis); concurrent acute purulent conjunctivitis and acute pharyngitis (pharyngoconjunctival fever, highly specific for Adenovirus); scarlatiniform macular or maculopapular rash beginning on distal extensor extremities, sparing palms and soles, and spreading centripetally in an adolescent (characteristic of Arcanobacterium haemolyticum); a dark gray, leathery, adherent pseudomembrane that bleeds when scraped, accompanied by massive soft tissue edema of the neck ("bull neck", pathognomonic for diphtheria); and pharyngitis followed by septic rigors and marked tenderness along the sternocleidomastoid muscle (signifying Lemierre syndrome).

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):** Systemic laboratory testing is non-specific and NOT routinely indicated for uncomplicated ambulatory pharyngitis. Complete blood count (CBC) showing leukocytosis with a left shift may be present in severe bacterial pharyngitis. In mononucleosis-like illnesses (EBV or CMV), CBC reveals lymphocytosis with greater than 10% atypical lymphocytes. Heterophil antibody testing (Monospot or monoslide test) or specific serum IgM antibodies to EBV viral capsid antigen (VCA) confirms primary EBV mononucleosis (though heterophil antibodies may be negative in children under 4 years). In suspected Lemierre syndrome or severe systemic infection, CBC, C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and blood cultures are obtained immediately.
- **Imaging Modalities:** Diagnostic imaging (radiographs, CT, MRI) is NOT indicated for routine uncomplicated acute pharyngitis. Lateral soft tissue neck radiographs or contrast-enhanced neck CT is indicated if deep space fascial neck infections (peritonsillar, retropharyngeal, or parapharyngeal space abscesses) or epiglottitis are suspected, revealing soft tissue swelling, fluid collections, or gas. Contrast-enhanced CT of the neck or Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA) is indicated for suspected Lemierre syndrome, revealing internal jugular vein wall enhancement, intraluminal thrombosis, and soft tissue edema.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):** Microbiologic confirmation of GAS pharyngitis is performed using Rapid Antigen Detection Testing (RADT) or throat culture on sheep blood agar plate (BAP). Swab specimens must be obtained properly from bilateral tonsillar surfaces and the posterior pharynx, avoiding oral surfaces (oral swabs yield less than 20% sensitivity compared to 80% for properly collected posterior pharyngeal swabs). RADT immunoassays or molecular real-time PCR assays exhibit overall 86% sensitivity and 96% specificity (with point-of-care PCR assays taking 15 minutes). Guidelines from the IDSA and AAP mandate that a negative RADT in children and adolescents be confirmed by throat culture. Specific diagnostic testing includes selective media (Löffler or Tinsdale medium) for suspected Corynebacterium diphtheriae; selective Thayer-Martin media for Neisseria gonorrhoeae; anaerobic culture for Fusobacterium necrophorum; extended 72-hour blood agar culture for Arcanobacterium haemolyticum; nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT/PCR) for Chlamydia pneumoniae and Mycoplasma pneumoniae; and multiplex RT-PCR panels for common respiratory viruses.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Throat culture on sheep blood agar plate (BAP) obtained from the tonsils and posterior pharynx serves as the microbiological gold standard for diagnosing Group A Streptococcal pharyngitis (supplemented by molecular nucleic acid amplification assays for rapid detection).

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:** Direct symptomatic therapy and pain management are the mainstays of treatment for acute pharyngitis. Systemic oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs, such as ibuprofen 25 mg lozenges or oral ibuprofen) or acetaminophen provide significant reduction in throat pain. Hydration, voice rest, and warm saline gargles are helpful supportive measures. A single dose of oral corticosteroids (such as dexamethasone) provides modest reduction in pain duration, but routine corticosteroid use is NOT recommended by IDSA or NICE guidelines for uncomplicated sore throat. Initial observation without antimicrobial therapy is recommended for patients with clinical features indicative of a viral etiology or low clinical prediction scores (Centor/McIsaac score 1 or lower).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:** For confirmed Group A Streptococcal pharyngitis, first-line regimens include: Oral Penicillin V (Children: 250 mg 2 to 3 times daily; Adolescents and Adults: 250 mg 3 to 4 times daily or 500 mg twice daily for 10 days); Oral Amoxicillin (50 mg/kg once daily, maximum 1000 mg/day, or 25 mg/kg twice daily for 10 days); or a single dose of Intramuscular Benzathine Penicillin G (600,000 units for patients under 27 kg; 1,200,000 units for patients 27 kg or greater).
- **Alternative Regimens:** For penicillin-allergic patients with mild/non-IgE-mediated allergy: First-generation oral cephalosporins (Cephalexin 20 mg/kg/dose twice daily, maximum 500 mg/dose, or Cefadroxil 30 mg/kg once daily for 10 days). For severe IgE-mediated (anaphylactic) penicillin allergy: Oral Macrolides including Erythromycin (formulation-dependent dosing for 10 days), Azithromycin (12 mg/kg once daily up to 500 mg on day 1, then 6 mg/kg up to 250 mg daily on days 2 to 5), or Clarithromycin (7.5 mg/kg/dose twice daily for 10 days), or Oral Clindamycin (7 mg/kg/dose 3 times daily for 10 days). For Fusobacterium necrophorum or Lemierre syndrome: Penicillin combined with a beta-lactamase inhibitor (Ampicillin-Sulbactam 1.5 to 3 g IV every 6 to 8 hours, or oral Amoxicillin-Clavulanate) plus Metronidazole (500 mg IV/PO every 8 hours), or IV Clindamycin (600 mg IV every 6 hours). For Corynebacterium diphtheriae: Penicillin G or Erythromycin/Azithromycin plus equine diphtheria antitoxin. For Neisseria gonorrhoeae pharyngitis: Intramuscular Ceftriaxone (500 mg IM single dose) plus oral Azithromycin or Doxycycline, with mandatory test-of-cure culture.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :** Surgical or procedural interventions are NOT indicated for uncomplicated acute pharyngitis. For suppurative complications, needle aspiration, incision and drainage, or quinsy tonsillectomy is required for peritonsillar abscess (quinsy) or parapharyngeal space abscess. Soft tissue decompression, surgical drainage, or emergency endotracheal intubation/tracheostomy is indicated for Ludwig angina or acute upper airway compromise. Surgical ligation or resection of the internal jugular vein is reserved for rare cases of Lemierre syndrome that fail medical antimicrobial therapy alone. Surgical tonsillectomy is considered for recurrent severe GAS pharyngitis meeting Paradise criteria (7 or more documented episodes in 1 year, 5 or more per year for 2 consecutive years, or 3 or more per year for 3 consecutive years).

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:** Suppurative local and regional complications of pharyngitis include Peritonsillar Abscess (quinsy, presenting with trismus, "hot potato" voice, contralateral uvular displacement, and severe dysphagia), Parapharyngeal Space Abscess, Retropharyngeal Abscess, Suppurative Cervical Lymphadenitis, Sinusitis, Otitis Media, Mastoiditis, Ludwig Angina, and invasive GAS infections (Necrotizing Fasciitis and Streptococcal Toxic Shock Syndrome). Vascular complications include Lemierre Syndrome (suppurative internal jugular vein septic thrombophlebitis with metastatic septic emboli to the lungs, joints, kidneys, and brain, caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum) and Carotid Artery Erosion/False Aneurysm.
- **Long-term Sequelae:** Nonsuppurative sequelae of Group A Streptococcal pharyngitis include Acute Rheumatic Fever (ARF, leading to chronic rheumatic heart disease, valvular damage, Sydenham chorea, polyarthritis, and carditis) and Poststreptococcal Acute Glomerulonephritis (PSGN, characterized by hematuria, hypertension, edema, and acute renal impairment; note that PSGN may follow Group A, C, or G streptococcal infections, whereas ARF occurs exclusively following GAS pharyngitis). Other sequelae include Pediatric Autoimmune Neuropsychiatric Disorders Associated with Streptococcal Infections (PANDAS), chronic recurrent tonsillitis, and tonsillosclerosis.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Triad of sore throat, fever, and pharyngeal inflammation" (classic defining features of acute pharyngitis); "Hot potato voice" (muffled voice quality characteristic of peritonsillar abscess or epiglottitis); "Sandpaper rash" (confluent scarlatiniform rash sparing palms and soles in GAS scarlet fever and Arcanobacterium haemolyticum); "Centor score / McIsaac score" (clinical prediction scoring systems for streptococcal pharyngitis); "Bull neck" (massive cervical lymphadenopathy and soft tissue swelling in severe Corynebacterium diphtheriae); "Pseudomembrane" (dark gray, leathery membrane that bleeds when dislodged, pathognomonic for diphtheria); "Lemierre syndrome / Postanginal sepsis" (suppurative internal jugular vein thrombophlebitis with septic pulmonary emboli following acute sore throat in young adults); "Herpangina" (discrete 1 to 4 mm erythematous-based vesicles on posterior pharynx caused by Coxsackievirus A); "Ampicillin rash" (diffuse maculopapular eruption triggered when ampicillin or amoxicillin is inadvertently administered during active EBV mononucleosis); and "Paradise criteria" (standardized frequency threshold for recurrent tonsillitis qualifying for tonsillectomy).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:** Redness of the pharynx alone without fever or acute sore throat does NOT establish acute bacterial pharyngitis, as mild pharyngeal vascular engorgement occurs frequently from crying, nasal congestion, cold exposure, or low-grade viral URIs. Empirical antibiotic treatment for pharyngitis based solely on clinical prediction rules (Centor score) is strongly discouraged by current IDSA and CDC guidelines because even a maximum Centor score has a positive predictive value of only 51% to 57%; microbiological confirmation by RADT or throat culture is required before initiating antibiotics. Group C and Group G Streptococci cause clinical pharyngitis indistinguishable from GAS and can trigger poststreptococcal acute glomerulonephritis, BUT non-GAS streptococci have NEVER been associated with Acute Rheumatic Fever, so routine antimicrobial treatment to prevent ARF is not indicated for non-GAS streptococci. Fusobacterium necrophorum is intrinsically resistant to macrolides (such as azithromycin and erythromycin) and exhibits increasing resistance to clindamycin, meaning standard empiric macrolide therapy for sore throat will completely fail to cover Fusobacterium. Up to 25% of Fusobacterium necrophorum isolates produce beta-lactamase, requiring beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations (Amoxicillin-Clavulanate, Ampicillin-Sulbactam) or metronidazole rather than penicillin monotherapy. Routine backup throat culture after a negative RADT is mandatory in children and adolescents, but is considered optional or unnecessary in adults due to the extremely low incidence of first-lifetime acute rheumatic fever in adult populations in developed countries.
