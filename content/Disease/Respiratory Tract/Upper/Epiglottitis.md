# Epiglottitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Acute epiglottitis (supraglottitis) is an invasive cellulitis of the epiglottis and its adjacent supraglottic structures (aryepiglottic folds, vallecula, base of tongue) that has the potential to cause abrupt, complete, life-threatening upper respiratory tract obstruction.
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Pediatric Epiglottitis: Historically a classic pediatric invasive bacteremic infection (predominantly Haemophilus influenzae type b) characterized by abrupt onset of high fever, toxic appearance, severe dysphagia, drooling, muffled "hot potato" voice, stridor, and tripod positioning.
  2. Adult Epiglottitis / Supraglottitis: Modern adult presentation which is typically nonbacteremic, somewhat less severe or subacute, involving a broader area of supraglottic structures (aryepiglottic folds, vallecula, tongue base), presenting primarily with severe sore throat and odynophagia out of proportion to physical exam, with lower rates of bacteremia (<15%) and lower rate of mechanical ventilation (5% to 38%).
  3. Noninfectious / Thermal / Toxic Epiglottitis: Supraglottic inflammation and edema mimicking infectious epiglottitis, caused by thermal injury (scald burns from hot beverages or steam inhalation), chemical irritants (crack cocaine smoke inhalation), angioneurotic edema, or foreign body trauma.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Historical vs. Modern Epidemiology: Prior to routine infant immunization with Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) conjugate vaccines in 1990, 65% to 75% of reported epiglottitis cases occurred in children 1 to 4 years of age (peak incidence at 3 years of age), with annual incidence of 3 to 6 cases per 100,000 children (and 21 to 41 cases per 100,000 children aged 0 to 4 years). Following widespread Hib vaccination, pediatric incidence declined by 95% to 99% (down to 0.02 to 0.6 cases per 100,000 children). Currently, 90% to 95% of all reported epiglottitis cases occur in adults, with a stable annual adult incidence of 1.8 to 2.0 cases per 100,000 adults in the United States and Europe.
  2. Demographics & Seasonality: Slight male predominance (~60% of cases in both children and adults occur in males). Slight seasonal excess in cases during spring and summer months.
  3. Risk Factors: Incomplete or lack of Hib immunization in young children; underlying immunocompromising conditions in adults; cigarette smoking; exposure to unimmunized/underimmunized household contacts; and thermal/chemical airway exposures (hot liquids, steam, crack cocaine).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib; historically accounted for 60% to 98% of pediatric cases; now rare in vaccinated populations but remains a potential cause in unimmunized individuals).
  - Streptococcus species: Streptococcus pneumoniae (including penicillin-resistant strains), Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus), Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus), Group C and G streptococci, and Viridans group streptococci (S. viridans).
  - Staphylococcus aureus (including Methicillin-Susceptible S. aureus / MSSA and Methicillin-Resistant S. aureus / MRSA).
  - Neisseria meningitidis.
  - Gram-Negative Bacilli (more common in surface cultures of adults or immunocompromised patients).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children (Pre-vaccine era vs. Modern era): Historically dominated by Haemophilus influenzae type b (60% to 98% bacteremic cases). In the post-vaccine era, pediatric cases are rare and caused by non-typeable H. influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Streptococcus pyogenes, and Staphylococcus aureus.
  - Adults: H. influenzae type b is isolated in <10% to 15% of cases. Microbial cause is often not identified (no pathogen isolated in most cases). When a bacterial pathogen is recovered from surface or blood cultures, Streptococcus spp. (S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, groups A, C, F, S. viridans), Haemophilus species (non-typeable or H. parainfluenzae), S. aureus, Neisseria meningitidis, and gram-negative bacilli predominate.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Fungal Pathogens (Unusual / Opportunistic): Candida species, Aspergillus species, Histoplasma capsulatum, Blastomyces dermatitidis, Coccidioides immitis/posadasii, Cryptococcus neoformans.
  - Viral Pathogens (Rare direct causes or predisposing viral URI antecedent): Herpes simplex virus (HSV), Varicella-zoster virus (VZV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Parainfluenza viruses, Influenza A and B, Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV).
  - Other Atypical / Rare Bacterial Agents: Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterobacteriaceae (in immunocompromised or intubated patients), Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Corynebacterium diphtheriae (causes pseudomembranous croup/diphtheria mimicking epiglottitis).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Primary Inoculation & Mucosal Invasion: Pathogens colonize the nasopharynx or oropharynx and directly invade the epithelial mucosa of the supraglottic region (epiglottis, aryepiglottic folds, arytenoids, and vallecula).
  - Local Invasion & Cellulitis: The supraglottic tissues possess a loose submucosal architectural layer that allows rapid accumulation of inflammatory exudate and fluid, leading to severe cellulitis, tissue edema, and marked enlargement of the epiglottis.
  - Bacteremic Dissemination: In classic pediatric Hib epiglottitis, local mucosal breach is accompanied by high-grade bacteremia (blood cultures positive in 60% to 98% of cases). In modern adult epiglottitis, infection is predominantly localized supraglottitis with low bacteremia rates (<10% to 15%).
  - Airway Obstruction Mechanism: Edema and swelling of the epiglottis and aryepiglottic folds significantly increase upper airway resistance and narrow the laryngeal inlet. Inspiration causes downward displacement of the swollen epiglottis over the glottic opening, acting as a ball-valve mechanism that can lead to sudden, complete respiratory tract occlusion.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Acute Inflammatory Infiltrate & Edema: Dense infiltration of polymorphonuclear neutrophils, vascular engorgement, hyperemic mucous membranes, and dramatic edema of the supraglottic structures.
  - Epiglottic Abscess Formation: In some adult cases (and rarely children), focal tissue necrosis and microabscesses develop within the epiglottic parenchyma, forming an epiglottic abscess that further compromises the airway lumen.
  - Airway Resistance & Respiratory Failure: Marked mechanical narrowing of the supraglottic airway generates high negative intrathoracic pressures during inspiration, leading to stridor, sternal/intercostal retractions, exhaustion, acute hypoxia, hypercapnia, asphyxiation, and fatal cardiopulmonary arrest if uncorrected.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Pediatric Classic Presentation: Abrupt, rapidly progressive onset of high fever, toxic appearance, severe sore throat or dysphagia, inability to swallow secretions resulting in drooling, muffled or hoarse "hot potato" voice, stridor, apprehension, and absence of cough (cough is distinctly uncommon in epiglottitis).
  - Adult Presentation: Predominantly subacute or indolent presentation over 1 to 2 days, dominated by severe sore throat and severe odynophagia (pain on swallowing out of proportion to pharyngeal exam findings); only 20% to 40% of adult patients present with fever, drooling, or stridor. Dysphagia, voice change / muffled voice, and neck pain are also common.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Posture & General Appearance: Toxic appearance, high fever, apprehension, sitting very still leaning forward with hyperextension of the neck, chin protruding, and bracing with hands on knees ("tripod position" or "sniffing position").
  - Oropharyngeal & Airway Signs: Inability to handle oral secretions resulting in active drooling; muffled "hot potato" voice; inspiratory stridor; tachypnea; intercostal and sternal retractions. Pharyngeal exam may show mild erythema, but the severity of sore throat and odynophagia is strikingly out of proportion to the minimal visible oropharyngeal findings.
  - Direct Visual Signs (Obtained ONLY in a Controlled Setting with Airway Equipment Ready): Direct or indirect laryngoscopy or fiberoptic nasopharyngeal endoscopy reveals an enlarged, edematous, intensely erythematous "cherry-red" epiglottis with swollen aryepiglottic folds and vallecular blurring.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Presence of severe sore throat, odynophagia, toxicity, and drooling oral secretions in a febrile patient who lacks a cough (drooling is 79% sensitive and 94% specific for epiglottitis, while absence of cough is 98% sensitive and 100% specific compared to viral croup).
  - Direct visualization of an edematous, cherry-red epiglottis on direct or indirect laryngoscopy or fiberoptic nasopharyngeal endoscopy (pathognomonic visual finding).
  - Radiographic "thumb sign" (a rounded, swollen epiglottis resembling a thumb) on lateral neck radiograph.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Peripheral leukocytosis with a left shift (increased immature band forms) is common but not universal.
  - Inflammatory Markers: Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR).
  - Phlebotomy Precaution: In a child with suspected epiglottitis, painful or anxiety-provoking procedures such as venipuncture/phlebotomy MUST BE DEFERRED until the airway is secured, to avoid triggering fatal laryngospasm or complete airway occlusion.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Lateral Neck Radiograph:
    - Findings: Demonstrates a swollen, enlarged epiglottis ("thumb sign" or "thumblike" appearance), ballooning of the hypopharynx, and prevertebral soft tissue swelling, in contrast to the normal thin epiglottis ("little finger sign"). Also contrasts with the "pencil-point" subglottic narrowing seen in viral croup on anteroposterior views.
    - Precaution: Lateral neck radiographs should NOT be performed in children with classic acute epiglottitis if it delays securing the airway or requires sending an unstable child to the radiology department without airway-trained personnel.
  - Computed Tomography (CT) of the Neck: CT is generally not required for initial diagnosis and should be avoided in acute airway compromise, but is useful in stable adults to evaluate for epiglottic abscess, deep neck space infection, or phlegmon.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Blood Cultures: In classic pediatric Hib epiglottitis, blood cultures yielded H. influenzae type b in 60% to 98% of cases. In modern adult epiglottitis and post-vaccine pediatric cases, blood culture yield is poor (<10% to 15% positive).
  - Epiglottic Surface Cultures: Swab cultures taken from the surface of the inflamed epiglottis immediately AFTER securing the airway (via endotracheal intubation in an operating room or ICU) yield a pathogen in approximately 20% of adult cases (recovering Streptococcus spp., Haemophilus spp., S. aureus, or gram-negative bacilli).
  - PCR & Molecular Assays: Multiplex RT-PCR panels on respiratory secretions or tissue can detect fastidious bacterial or viral nucleic acids.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - Direct Visualization of an Edematous, Cherry-Red Epiglottis via Direct Laryngoscopy, Indirect Laryngoscopy, or Fiberoptic Nasopharyngeal Endoscopy (performed in a controlled environment with airway equipment ready): Represents the definitive gold standard for establishing the diagnosis of epiglottitis.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Airway Management (Primary Essential Priority): Immediate evaluation and securing of the airway is the cornerstone of management. In children with suspected epiglottitis, handle as a top medical emergency. Minimize anxiety-provoking or painful procedures (phlebotomy, IV insertion, radiographs) until airway is secured. Accompany patient with personnel capable of emergency intubation/cricothyroidotomy during any transport. Maintain bag-mask ventilation if intubation is delayed (obstruction is usually not absolute).
  - Intubation Protocol: In classic pediatric epiglottitis, immediate endotracheal intubation in the operating room or ICU under direct visualization (which serves both diagnostic and therapeutic roles) is required in 70% to 100% of children. Emergent tracheotomy or cricothyroidotomy is rarely required (<5%). In adults, 75% to 80% are successfully managed conservatively without endotracheal intubation, but require admission to an ICU for close airway monitoring.
  - Humidified Oxygen & Heliox: Supplemental humidified oxygen; heliox (helium-oxygen mixture) may reduce work of breathing in upper airway turbulence.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Parenteral Empiric Regimens (Initiated immediately after securing airway and obtaining cultures):
    - Intravenous Third-Generation Cephalosporins: Intravenous Ceftriaxone (1 to 2 g IV daily in adults; 50 to 100 mg/kg/day IV divided q12-24h in children) OR Intravenous Cefotaxime (1 to 2 g IV q8h in adults; 100 to 200 mg/kg/day IV divided q6-8h in children); OR
    - Intravenous Beta-Lactamase Inhibitor Combination: Intravenous Ampicillin-sulbactam (1.5 to 3.0 g IV q6h in adults; 100 to 200 mg ampicillin component/kg/day IV divided q6h in children).
  - Additional Broadening Considerations:
    - Coverage for Pseudomonas / Gram-Negatives in Immunocompromised: Intravenous Piperacillin-tazobactam (3.375 g IV q6h).
    - Coverage for Resistant Pneumococci or MRSA Bacterial Tracheitis: Add Intravenous Vancomycin (15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h).
  - Total Duration & Oral Transition: Antibiotics are continued for a total duration of 7 to 10 days. Once clinical improvement occurs and artificial airway is removed, transition to oral third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., Cefpodoxime, Cefdinir) or oral respiratory fluoroquinolones (Moxifloxacin or Levofloxacin).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Regimens for Beta-Lactam Allergic Patients:
    - Intravenous Respiratory Fluoroquinolone (Levofloxacin 500 to 750 mg IV daily or Moxifloxacin 400 mg IV daily) COMBINED WITH Intravenous Clindamycin (600 mg IV q6-8h); OR
    - Intravenous Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) COMBINED WITH Intravenous Clindamycin.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Endotracheal Intubation under Direct Vision: Primary procedural airway intervention performed in the operating room or ICU by an experienced airway specialist (anesthesiologist or otolaryngologist).
  - Emergent Cricothyroidotomy or Tracheotomy: Surgical airway intervention reserved for sudden complete upper airway occlusion where endotracheal intubation fails or is impossible (<5% of cases).
  - Surgical / Endoscopic Drainage of Epiglottic Abscess: Needle aspiration or surgical incision and drainage of a localized epiglottic abscess identified on CT or laryngoscopy in adult patients.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Complete Upper Airway Obstruction & Asphyxiation: Sudden, fatal respiratory arrest secondary to complete laryngeal closure by the edematous epiglottis.
  - Epiglottic Abscess: Localized purulent collection within the epiglottis, reported in up to 20% to 30% of adult cases.
  - Bacterial Tracheitis & Deep Neck Space Infection: Downward extension into the trachea or surrounding spaces (peritonsillar abscess, retropharyngeal abscess, parapharyngeal space abscess).
  - Cardiopulmonary Arrest, Anoxic Brain Injury, & Pulmonary Edema: Hypoxic encephalopathy or post-obstructive negative-pressure pulmonary edema following acute severe upper airway obstruction.
  - Septic Shock & Bacteremia: Systemic bacteremic dissemination (more common in classic Hib pediatric disease).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Post-Anoxic Neurological Deficits: Long-term cognitive or motor deficits secondary to prolonged hypoxia during acute airway arrest.
  - Subglottic Stenosis or Laryngeal Scarring: Rare structural airway narrowing resulting from prolonged endotracheal intubation or traumatic airway instrumentation.
  - Overall Long-Term Prognosis: Excellent with prompt recognition, airway management, and antibiotic therapy, with full clinical recovery expected without permanent sequelae in the vast majority of cases.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Cherry-red epiglottis" (classic direct endoscopic visual descriptor of epiglottitis).
  - "Thumb sign" / "Thumbprint sign" (classic radiologic sign on lateral neck radiograph depicting the swollen epiglottis).
  - "Tripod position" / "Sniffing position" (characteristic posture of a child sitting upright, leaning forward, hyperextending neck, protruding chin, and bracing hands).
  - "Hot potato voice" (muffled, thick vocal quality secondary to supraglottic swelling).
  - "Cynanche trachealis" (18th-century medical term literally meaning "dog strangulation," historical descriptor for fatal upper airway obstruction).
  - "Death of George Washington" (historical event in 1799 now widely attributed to acute epiglottitis / supraglottitis, complicated by iatrogenic bloodletting).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Absence of Cough Distinguishes Epiglottitis from Viral Croup: While viral croup (laryngotracheobronchitis) is characterized by a prominent "barking" cough, coughing is DISTINCTLY UNCOMMON in acute epiglottitis; the presence of coughing and absence of drooling heavily favors croup, whereas absence of cough (100% specificity) and presence of drooling (94% specificity) heavily favors epiglottitis.
  - Absence of Fever in Adult Epiglottitis: While pediatric epiglottitis almost universally features high fever (>38.0 degrees C) and toxic prostration, only 20% to 40% of adult patients with acute epiglottitis present with fever, making severe sore throat/odynophagia out of proportion to physical exam the primary diagnostic clue in adults.
  - Safety of Direct Visualization in Adults vs. Children: In children with suspected epiglottitis, examination of the pharynx with a tongue depressor or direct laryngoscopy outside an operating room is STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED due to the risk of precipitating fatal laryngospasm/obstruction; in contrast, adult patients generally tolerate direct examination in the sitting-forward position with gentle tongue depression or nasal fiberoptic endoscopy without triggering acute arrest.
  - Inefficacy of Blood Cultures in Modern Epiglottitis: Blood cultures were positive for H. influenzae type b in 60% to 98% of classic pediatric cases, but are positive in FEWER THAN 10% to 15% of modern cases in adults and older vaccinated children, reflecting the shift in microbiology to non-bacteremic streptococcal and polymicrobial surface etiologies.
  - Non-Infectious Thermal and Chemical Epiglottitis: Epiglottitis is not exclusively an infectious bacterial disease; severe supraglottic cellulitis and edema mimicking infectious epiglottitis can occur following thermal injury (drinking scalding hot beverages, steam inhalation) or inhalation of illicit drug vapors (crack cocaine).
  - Edentulous Patient Exception for Primary Anaerobic Deep Neck Infections vs. Epiglottitis: While primary anaerobic lung abscesses are virtually non-existent in edentulous individuals due to lack of gingival crevice flora, acute epiglottitis occurs readily in edentulous adults because supraglottic infection is driven by capsulated or commensal aerobic/microaerophilic streptococci and upper respiratory pathogens rather than gingival anaerobes.
