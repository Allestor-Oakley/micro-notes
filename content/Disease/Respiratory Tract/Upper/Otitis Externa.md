# Otitis Externa

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Otitis externa is an infection and inflammation of the skin lining the external auditory canal, extending from the concha of the auricle to the outer surface of the tympanic membrane. It encompasses localized furunculosis, diffuse superficial maceration and infection, chronic dermatitis with otorrhea, and life-threatening invasive necrotizing infection of adjacent soft tissues, cartilage, and temporal bone.
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Acute Localized Otitis Externa: Localized infection occurring as a pustule or furuncle associated with hair follicles in the outer, cartilaginous portion of the canal.
  2. Acute Diffuse Otitis Externa ("Swimmer's Ear"): Generalized diffuse inflammation, edema, and erythema of the canal wall skin and auricle, typically triggered by fluid entrapment and maceration.
  3. Chronic Otitis Externa: Long-standing irritation and inflammation of the canal skin, commonly caused by persistent purulent drainage through a perforated tympanic membrane secondary to chronic suppurative otitis media, or rare systemic infectious/granulomatous conditions (tuberculosis, syphilis, yaws, leprosy, sarcoidosis).
  4. Invasive / Malignant Otitis Externa (Necrotizing Otitis Externa): A severe, life-threatening necrotizing infection that spreads from the squamous epithelium of the ear canal to surrounding soft tissues, blood vessels, cartilage, temporal bone, and skull base.
  5. Infection of the Pinna / Suppurative Perichondritis: Infection involving the auricle and cartilaginous pinna, frequently associated with piercing ornaments placed in the cartilaginous upper ear.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Environmental & Seasonal Risk Factors: Acute diffuse otitis externa occurs predominantly during hot, humid weather or following aquatic activities ("swimmer's ear"). Trapping of water in the external canal causes tissue irritation, softening, and skin maceration. Exposure to mobile redwood hot tub systems is specifically linked to severe hemorrhagic external otitis caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa serotype O:10.
  2. Mechanical Trauma & Foreign Bodies: Insertion of foreign objects into the canal (cotton-tipped applicators, fingers, earplugs, or foreign bodies inserted by young children) causes epidermal excoriation, maceration, and disruption of the cerumen protective barrier.
  3. Host Factors for Invasive/Malignant Disease: Invasive or malignant otitis externa occurs primarily in elderly, diabetic, immunocompromised, or severely debilitated patients. Microangiopathy in diabetic skin overlying the temporal bone impairs local microvascular perfusion, creating an ideal hypoxic milieu for invasive tissue necrosis.
  4. Anatomical & Procedural Factors: Placement of piercing ornaments into the cartilaginous pinna (unlike simple earlobe piercing) damages perichondrium and cartilage, predisposing to chronic chondritis and severe cosmetic deformity.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Pseudomonas aeruginosa (the single most common bacterial pathogen in acute diffuse swimmer's ear, cartilaginous pinna infections, and malignant/necrotizing otitis externa; serotype O:10 associated with hot tub exposure).
  - Staphylococcus aureus (the predominant pathogen in acute localized otitis externa / furunculosis).
  - Commensal Skin Flora & Facultative Colonizers: Staphylococcus epidermidis, Corynebacterium species, Propionibacterium acnes, and aerobic/anaerobic skin bacteria.
  - Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus; causes erysipelas involving the concha and canal).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children: Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, foreign body-associated mixed flora, and Candida albicans (in children with chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis).
  - Adults & Elderly / Diabetic Patients: Pseudomonas aeruginosa (dominant cause of invasive malignant otitis externa), Staphylococcus aureus, and Aspergillus species.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Fungal Pathogens (Otomycosis & Invasive Disease): Aspergillus species (particularly Aspergillus niger, which grows in cerumen and desquamated keratin, producing fluffy greenish or blackish colonies; causes necrotizing otitis externa in severely immunocompromised hosts), Candida albicans.
  - Rare Chronic & Systemic Agents: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Treponema pallidum (syphilis), Treponema pertenue (yaws), Mycobacterium leprae (leprosy).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  1. Anatomic & Barrier Physiology: The external auditory canal is approximately 2.5 cm long from the concha to the tympanic membrane. The outer half is cartilaginous with thicker skin, hair follicles, and cerumen-producing glands; the inner half is osseous with thin skin firmly attached to periosteum without a subcutaneous layer. The isthmus restricts debris entry. Cerumen and desquamated keratin are normally extruded by self-cleansing mechanisms.
  2. Maceration & Bacterial Invasiveness: Moisture accumulation (from swimming, bathing, or humidity) causes the stratum corneum to absorb water, leading to epithelial desquamation, denuding, and occlusion of apocrine/sebaceous ducts. In this warm, moist environment, resident flora and opportunistic gram-negative bacilli (Pseudomonas aeruginosa) proliferate and invade macerated skin, inducing acute cutaneous inflammation and suppuration.
  3. Invasiveness in Malignant Otitis Externa: P. aeruginosa penetrates through the squamous epithelium into deeper soft tissues, cartilage, and small blood vessels. The organism induces localized vasculitis, small vessel thrombosis, and extensive ischemic tissue necrosis. Infection spreads along the fissures of Santorini in the cartilaginous floor of the canal to the infratemporal fossa, temporal bone, sigmoid sinus, jugular bulb, skull base, meninges, and brain.
  4. Cartilaginous Pinna Inoculation: Piercing ornaments placed through cartilaginous portions of the ear breach the protective perichondrial layer, introducing P. aeruginosa directly into avascular cartilage, leading to suppurative chondritis and necrosis.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  1. Localized Inflammatory Response: Intense dermal and epidermal edema, hyperemic vascular engorgement, leukocyte infiltration, and purulent exudation. Because skin over the osseous canal lacks subcutaneous tissue and is tightly bound to periosteum, tissue expansion is severely restricted, generating disproportionately severe pain.
  2. Necrotizing Tissue Destruction: In malignant disease, progressive necrotizing osteomyelitis destroys the temporal bone and skull base. Inflammatory thrombosis and tissue destruction involve adjacent cranial nerves: facial nerve (CN VII) involvement causes permanent facial paralysis, while spread to the jugular foramen involves cranial nerves IX, X, and XII.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Localized & Diffuse Symptoms: Severe ear pain (otalgia) that worsens rapidly; intense itching (pruritus) in the canal; sensation of ear fullness or pressure; conductional hearing loss (due to luminal compromise by swelling and purulent debris); and persistent ear discharge (otorrhea).
  - Malignant / Invasive Symptoms: Unremitting, deep, nocturnal ear pain radiating to the temporomandibular joint and mastoid area, purulent otorrhea, and facial weakness or asymmetry (facial nerve palsy).
  - Pinna Infection Symptoms: Painful, erythematous, fluctuant swelling localized to the cartilaginous upper ear following piercing.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Acute Localized OE: Pustule or furuncle visible in the canal lumen; focal erythema and edema; bluish-red hemorrhagic bullae on the osseous canal wall or tympanic membrane; regional lymphadenopathy.
  - Acute Diffuse OE: Diffuse erythema, severe edema, and narrowing of the canal wall lining; thick purulent exudate mixed with desquamated keratin debris; pain elicited by tragal pressure or pinna manipulation.
  - Malignant / Invasive OE: Marked tenderness over the pinna, mastoid, and temporomandibular joint; presence of granulations or active pus at the junction of the osseous and cartilaginous canal; facial nerve weakness (CN VII palsy) or lower cranial nerve deficits (CN IX, X, XII).
  - Otomycosis: Visual identification of a fluffy, greenish, or blackish fungal colony (Aspergillus niger) growing on cerumen and keratinaceous debris inside the canal.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Exquisite pain elicited by gentle pressure on the tragus or pulling upward/backward on the auricle / pinna (classic diagnostic physical maneuver differentiating external otitis from middle ear infection).
  - Presence of red granulation tissue at the floor of the external auditory canal at the junction of the cartilaginous and osseous portions in an elderly diabetic patient with severe otalgia (pathognomonic visual hallmark of malignant necrotizing otitis externa).
  - Visible blackish or greenish fluffy fungal colonies studded on keratin plugs within the ear canal (pathognomonic sign of Aspergillus niger otomycosis).

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Blood Glucose & HbA1c: Mandatory screening in suspected malignant otitis externa to detect underlying or poorly controlled diabetes mellitus.
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis with neutrophilia in invasive or malignant disease; often normal in mild diffuse swimmer's ear.
  - Inflammatory Biomarkers: Markedly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) in malignant necrotizing otitis externa (useful for tracking response to prolonged therapy).
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Plain Radiography: Generally unhelpful and not recommended.
  - Computed Tomography (CT) of Temporal Bone with Contrast: Essential for evaluating suspected malignant otitis externa. Demonstrates soft tissue swelling, erosion of the osseous canal, cortical temporal bone destruction, and skull base involvement.
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) & MRA: Superior to CT for demonstrating soft tissue extension, dural enhancement, sigmoid sinus thrombosis, and intracranial extension.
  - Radionuclide Nuclear Imaging: Technetium-99m bone scan identifies early osteomyelitis of the temporal bone; Gallium-67 scan or Indium-111 labeled white blood cell scan is used to monitor resolution of active infection and guide duration of antibiotic therapy.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Canal Culture & Gram Stain: Swab or aspirated sample of canal exudate for Gram stain, aerobic culture, and susceptibility testing (recovering P. aeruginosa or S. aureus).
  - Fungal Preparations: Direct KOH mount or calcofluor white stain of debris to visualize Aspergillus hyphae or Candida yeast cells.
  - Biopsy: Biopsy of canal granulation tissue in refractory cases to rule out squamous cell carcinoma of the ear canal or confirm necrotizing fungal infection.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - Clinical Otoscopic Visualization of External Canal Inflammation, Edema, and Debris COMBINED WITH Microbiological Isolation of Pathogen from Canal Exudate (or CT/Nuclear Scan Evidence of Temporal Bone Erosion with Pseudomonas Isolation in Malignant OE): Represents the gold standard for diagnosis.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Canal Cleansing & Debridement (Crucial Primary Step): Meticulous removal of cerumen, pus, and desquamated keratin under direct otoscopic visualization.
  - Irrigation Guidelines: Irrigation with 3% hydrogen peroxide or warm water is permitted ONLY if the tympanic membrane is known to be intact. If the status of the tympanic membrane is unknown or perforated, non-sterile water irrigation is avoided.
  - Micro-cleansing Agents: Cleansing with hypertonic saline (3%), or a mixture of alcohol (70%-95%) and acetic acid; hydrophilic 50% Burow solution applied for 1 to 2 days to reduce severe canal swelling.
  - Ear Wick Placement: Insertion of a compressed cotton ear wick into a severely swollen canal to ensure distribution of topical eardrops.
  - Pain Management: Systemic analgesics (NSAIDs like ibuprofen, or acetaminophen) during initial inflammatory phase.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Mild to Moderate Uncomplicated Acute Diffuse Otitis Externa (Topical Therapy):
    - Topical Fluoroquinolone Otic Preparation: Ofloxacin 0.3% otic solution OR Ciprofloxacin 0.3%-dexamethasone 0.1% otic suspension (instilled for 10 days); OR
    - Topical Combination Drops: Neomycin-polymyxin B-hydrocortisone otic suspension (instilled for 10 days); OR
    - Topical Acetic Acid-Hydrocortisone Preparation: For mild early cases.
  - Invasive / Malignant Otitis Externa (Systemic Antipseudomonal Regimen for 4 to 6 Weeks PLUS Topical Drops):
    - Intravenous Antipseudomonal Parenteral Therapy: Intravenous Ceftazidime (2 g IV q8h), Cefepime (2 g IV q8h), or Piperacillin-tazobactam (4.5 g IV q6h) COMBINED WITH an Intravenous Aminoglycoside (Gentamicin or Tobramycin 1.7 mg/kg IV q8h); OR
    - Oral / Parenteral Fluoroquinolone: High-dose Ciprofloxacin (750 mg PO twice daily or 400 mg IV q8-12h) early in the course of invasive disease.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Fungal Otitis Externa (Otomycosis):
    - Thorough canal debridement PLUS Topical Antifungal Solution (Clotrimazole, miconazole, or amphotericin B drops); Systemic Voriconazole, Isavuconazole, or Amphotericin B for invasive Aspergillus necrotizing otitis externa in severely immunocompromised patients.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Incision and Drainage: Performed for localized furuncles or pustules in acute localized OE to relieve severe pressure pain.
  - Surgical Debridement in Malignant OE: Surgical excision of devitalized soft tissue and necrotic bone sequestra in refractory malignant otitis externa.
  - Pinna Infection Management: Removal of piercing ornaments, incision and drainage of fluctuant cartilage collections, and reconstructive otoplasty for severe cosmetic cartilage destruction.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Temporal Bone Osteomyelitis & Skull Base Extension: Direct progression of necrotizing infection from ear canal into cranial bones.
  - Cranial Nerve Palsies: Permanent facial nerve weakness (CN VII paralysis; most common), and lower cranial nerve palsies (CN IX, X, XII) due to jugular foramen involvement.
  - Vascular & Intracranial Suppuration: Septic thrombosis of the sigmoid sinus or jugular bulb, epidural abscess, meningitis, and brain abscess.
  - Severe Perichondritis & Pinna Cartilage Necrosis: Permanent structural collapse ("cauliflower ear") following cartilaginous piercing infections.
  - Hemorrhagic Bullous External Otitis: Formation of bluish-red blood-filled bullae on canal walls and tympanic membrane (associated with P. aeruginosa serotype O:10 from hot tubs).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Permanent Facial Nerve Paralysis: Persistent facial droop resulting from CN VII destruction.
  - External Auditory Canal Stenosis: Fibrotic narrowing of the canal lumen and conductive hearing loss.
  - Chronic Draining Otitis Externa: Persistent otorrhea and canal dermatitis.
  - Persistent Skull Base Osteomyelitis: Recurrent deep bone infection requiring months of systemic antimicrobial suppression.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Swimmer's ear" (classic popular synonym for acute diffuse otitis externa).
  - "Cotton ear wick" (device inserted into a swollen canal to wick topical antibiotic drops).
  - "Granulation tissue at the osseous-cartilaginous junction" (pathognomonic otoscopic finding in malignant otitis externa).
  - "Aspergillus niger black/green fluffy colonies" (classic visual appearance of fungal otomycosis).
  - "Mobile redwood hot tub systems" (associated with P. aeruginosa serotype O:10 hemorrhagic otitis externa).
  - "Cartilaginous ear piercing" (major risk factor for P. aeruginosa pinna perichondritis and cosmetic destruction).
  - "Tragal tenderness" (pain on pressing the tragus or pulling the pinna, key physical sign separating OE from otitis media).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Intact vs. Perforated Tympanic Membrane Rules: Irrigation with 3% hydrogen peroxide or tap water is strictly safe ONLY when the tympanic membrane is known to be INTACT; if the membrane is perforated or tympanostomy tubes are present, non-intact membrane otic solutions (e.g., fluoroquinolones like ofloxacin or ciprofloxacin-dexamethasone) must be used, and non-sterile irrigation avoided.
  - Distinguishing Acute Otitis Media with Perforation from Otitis Externa: A history of increasing ear pain that is suddenly RELIEVED when otorrhea appears indicates acute otitis media with tympanic membrane perforation; in contrast, increasing ear pain WITHOUT relief in the presence of otorrhea indicates acute otitis externa.
  - Otic Aminoglycoside / Polymyxin Ototoxicity: Aminoglycoside-containing or polymyxin/neomycin otic drops can cause cochlear and vestibular ototoxicity if they enter the middle ear through a perforated tympanic membrane; fluoroquinolone drops (ofloxacin, ciprofloxacin) are non-ototoxic and preferred when membrane integrity is uncertain.
  - Dual Role of Aspergillus niger in the Ear: Aspergillus niger frequently grows as an indolent saprophytic mold in cerumen and desquamated keratin without tissue invasion in normal hosts; however, in severely immunocompromised hosts, it acts as a true necrotizing pathogen causing invasive fungal otitis externa.
  - Primary Therapy for External Otitis is Topical, Not Oral: Uncomplicated acute diffuse otitis externa is treated almost exclusively with TOPICAL antimicrobial/anti-inflammatory eardrops rather than oral systemic antibiotics; systemic oral antibiotics are indicated ONLY for malignant/invasive disease, host immunodeficiency, or spread beyond the ear canal.
