# Sinusitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Sinusitis is defined as an inflammatory disorder of the paranasal sinuses (maxillary, ethmoid, frontal, and sphenoid sinuses). Because most cases of acute bacterial sinusitis are secondary to a viral upper respiratory infection (URI) or allergic inflammation involving the nasal mucosa, nasal inflammation is a universal concurrent finding, leading some experts to refer to this complex as rhinosinusitis; however, in instances of bacterial superinfection of the paranasal sinuses, the preferred clinical term is acute community-acquired bacterial sinusitis.
- **Major Classifications:** Classifications include Acute Bacterial Sinusitis (symptoms lasting less than 4 weeks, further stratified into 3 distinct clinical presentations: onset with persistent symptoms/signs lasting at least 10 days without evidence of substantial clinical improvement, onset with severe symptoms/signs of high fever of 39 degrees C or greater and purulent nasal discharge lasting for 3 to 4 consecutive days, or onset with worsening symptoms/signs termed "double sickening" characterized by new fever, headache, or increased nasal discharge following initial improvement of a typical 5- to 6-day viral URI); Subacute Sinusitis (symptoms lasting 4 to 12 weeks); Chronic Rhinosinusitis / Chronic Sinus Disease (signs and symptoms of sinus inflammation persisting for at least 12 weeks); Recurrent Acute Sinusitis; Nosocomial / Ventilator-Associated Sinusitis (frequently occurring in critical care settings associated with nasotracheal intubation or nasoenteric tubes); and Fungal Rhinosinusitis (classified into acute or chronic invasive fungal sinusitis, noninvasive fungal mycetoma/fungus ball, and allergic fungal sinusitis).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:** In the United States, bacterial infection of the sinuses is estimated to complicate 0.5% to 2% of cases of viral URI in adults and 6% to 13% (with prospective studies documenting 8.8%) of viral URIs in children, representing one of the most frequent reasons for primary care visits and accounting for over 20 million annual antibiotic prescriptions, 23 million healthcare visits, and 5.8 billion dollars in health expenditures. Sinusitis is diagnosed more frequently in adult women than men at a ratio of 1.8 to 1. Predisposing factors resulting in mucosal swelling or mechanical ostial obstruction include viral URIs, allergic rhinitis, asthma, day care attendance (doubling the rate of URIs in children), active or passive tobacco smoke exposure, environmental pollutants, swimming and diving, rhinitis medicamentosa, facial trauma, nasal intubation or nasoenteric tube placement (incidence of 15.7 cases per 1000 patient days in intubated patients), mechanical obstructions (deviated nasal septum, nasal polyps, foreign bodies, tumors, choanal atresia, enlarged ethmoid bullae), dental infections or apical periodontitis (producing odontogenic maxillary sinusitis), underlying systemic or genetic disorders (cystic fibrosis, primary ciliary dyskinesia, agammaglobulinemia, HIV/AIDS, chronic granulomatous disease), and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):** In acute community-acquired bacterial sinusitis documented by quantitative maxillary sinus puncture, the predominant bacterial pathogens are Streptococcus pneumoniae (historically isolated in 41% of adults and children), nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae (35% in adults, 27% in children), and Moraxella catarrhalis (22% in children, 4% in adults). Following universal infant immunization with pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV7 and PCV13), recovery of S. pneumoniae has decreased while nontypeable H. influenzae and M. catarrhalis have increased to account for up to 90% of pediatric middle ear and sinus isolates, with 70% to 80% producing beta-lactamase. Respiratory viruses (rhinovirus, adenovirus, parainfluenza virus, influenza virus, coronavirus) are isolated in approximately 10% of sinus aspirates, and 30% to 40% of aspirates yield no bacterial growth, reflecting an underlying or waning viral etiology.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:** In children, acute bacterial sinusitis is dominated by nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis (which occurs at a significantly higher frequency in children than adults), and non-vaccine serotypes of Streptococcus pneumoniae. In adults, acute bacterial sinusitis is driven by Streptococcus pneumoniae, nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, viridans and beta-hemolytic streptococcal species, and anaerobic bacteria (7% in adults). In critically ill or ICU patients with nosocomial/ventilator-associated sinusitis, pathogens include gram-negative enteric bacilli (Pseudomonas aeruginosa 10.7%, Klebsiella species 7.2%, Enterobacter species 7.2%, Escherichia coli 5.9%, Proteus mirabilis 5.9%), Staphylococcus aureus (9.5%), viridans streptococci (8.3%), and Candida species (8.4%). In chronic rhinosinusitis, microbiology includes polymicrobial anaerobic and aerobic flora, Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase-negative Staphylococci, Corynebacterium species, and bacterial biofilms.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:** In immunocompromised hosts (neutropenia, bone marrow/solid organ transplantation, advanced HIV) and uncontrolled diabetic ketoacidosis, acute invasive fungal sinusitis is caused by filamentous fungi and Zygomycetes including Mucor species, Rhizopus species, Aspergillus species (Aspergillus fumigatus, Aspergillus flavus), Fusarium species, Pseudallescheria boydii, and dematiaceous fungi (Alternaria, Bipolaris, Cladophialophora, Curvularia). Noninvasive fungal mycetoma/fungus ball is caused by Mucor, Rhizopus, Fusarium, or Alternaria. Allergic fungal sinusitis in atopic hosts with nasal polyps is caused by Aspergillus, Bipolaris, Curvularia, and Drechslera species. Streptococcus anginosus group is an important emerging pathogen in sinogenic intracranial and orbital suppurative complications.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:** Sinusitis develops from a breakdown in the three interdependent physiological determinants of sinus health: patency of the sinus ostia, mucociliary clearance, and secretion quality. The paranasal sinuses (maxillary, ethmoid, frontal, sphenoid) are normally sterile cavities lined by pseudostratified columnar ciliated respiratory epithelium, with the maxillary sinus draining against gravity into the middle meatus via a narrow outflow tract (infundibulum/hiatus semilunaris) within the osteomeatal complex. Viral URI or allergic inflammation induces mucosal hyperemia, edema, and elevated proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1beta, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-alpha), resulting in ostial obstruction. Ostial blockage leads to transient gas absorption, negative sinus pressure, oxygen depletion, and fluid accumulation. Activities such as nose blowing or sniffing generate transient high pressures that propel viscous, microbe-laden nasal and nasopharyngeal secretions into the closed sinus cavity. Simultaneously, viral infection induces ciliary ultrastructural dysmorphology, microtubular disruption, and patchy loss of ciliated cells, severely impairing mucociliary transport and allowing trapped bacteria to rapidly proliferate.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:** Histopathologically, acute bacterial sinusitis is characterized by an intact epithelial surface accompanied by marked lamina propria edema, dense vascular engorgement, and massive infiltration of neutrophils, macrophages, T lymphocytes, and B lymphocytes with microabscess formation. Neutrophil chemoattractants (specifically IL-8) drive localized tissue inflammation, while bacterial toxins and inflammatory debris thicken sinus secretions and further impair ciliary beating. In chronic rhinosinusitis, persistent mucosal inflammation leads to goblet cell hyperplasia, basement membrane thickening, tissue remodeling, mucosal polyp formation, and bacterial biofilm establishment (complex matrix-encased microbial communities displaying antibiotic tolerance, slow metabolic turnover, and persister cells). In complicated or invasive fungal sinusitis, aggressive vascular invasion by fungal hyphae or virulent bacteria leads to local tissue ischemia, vascular thrombosis, osteonecrotic bone destruction (such as lamina papyracea erosion), and direct or retrograde thrombophlebitic extension into the orbit and intracranial vault.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):** In acute bacterial sinusitis, symptoms present in 3 distinct clinical patterns: (1) Persistent presentation, featuring nasal congestion, anterior or posterior mucopurulent nasal discharge, and daytime cough lasting 10 days or longer without clinical improvement, often accompanied by low-grade fever, periorbital edema, or malodorous breath (halitosis); (2) Severe presentation, featuring high fever of 39 degrees C (102.2 degrees F) or higher accompanied by purulent nasal discharge for at least 3 to 4 consecutive days in an ill-appearing patient; or (3) Worsening presentation ("double sickening"), featuring initial symptom improvement over 5 to 6 days from a viral URI followed by sudden secondary deterioration with new fever, daytime cough, headache, or increased purulent nasal discharge. Other symptoms include facial pain, pressure, or fullness over the affected sinus (maxillary, frontal, or ethmoid), ear pressure, and dental pain involving the upper jaw teeth. In chronic rhinosinusitis (symptoms lasting at least 12 weeks), patients report persistent anterior or posterior mucopurulent drainage, nasal obstruction/congestion, facial fullness or pain, hyposmia or anosmia, and altered taste.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):** Anterior rhinoscopy or nasal endoscopy reveals mucosal erythema, mild-to-moderate turbinate edema, and purulent or mucopurulent drainage emerging from the middle meatus or dripping down the posterior pharynx; facial tenderness upon palpation or percussion over the maxillary or frontal sinuses (though an unreliable diagnostic sign); mild, soft, non-tender periorbital edema and minor eyelid discoloration; malodorous breath (halitosis) in the absence of dental caries or exudative pharyngitis; and visible nasal polyps or middle meatal polypoid tissue in patients with chronic rhinosinusitis.
- **Pathognomonic Features:** The presence of the "double sickening" phenomenon (sudden secondary worsening with new fever, purulent discharge, or headache after initial recovery from a viral cold); persistent mucopurulent nasal discharge and daytime cough lasting 10 days or longer without any signs of improvement; and Pott puffy tumor (a soft, tender, doughy subperiosteal swelling over the forehead caused by osteomyelitis of the frontal bone secondary to frontal sinusitis).

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):** Systemic laboratory testing (CBC, ESR, CRP) is non-specific and NOT routinely recommended for uncomplicated acute community-acquired bacterial sinusitis. Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and leukocytosis with a left shift may be observed in severe illness or when differentiating bacterial infection from viral URI in research protocols, but they lack diagnostic specificity. Systemic laboratory testing (CBC, blood cultures, inflammatory markers, renal/hepatic panels) is mandatory in patients with suspected intraorbital or intracranial suppurative complications, nosocomial/ICU sinusitis, or invasive fungal sinusitis.
- **Imaging Modalities:** Routine diagnostic imaging (plain film radiographs, CT, MRI, ultrasonography) is explicitly NOT recommended for uncomplicated acute bacterial sinusitis because viral URIs produce identical transient mucosal thickening, air-fluid levels, and sinus opacification in 55% to 90% of patients. Contrast-enhanced Computed Tomography (CT) of the paranasal sinuses and orbits is the imaging modality of choice for suspected orbital or intracranial complications, defining bony septal erosion, subperiosteal abscess, or orbital cellulitis, as well as for preoperative anatomical mapping in functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) or chronic rhinosinusitis. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) with gadolinium contrast or Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA) is indicated for evaluating suspected invasive fungal sinusitis, soft tissue extension, intracranial complications (epidural/subdural empyema, brain abscess), and cavernous sinus thrombosis or dural sinus septic thrombophlebitis.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):** Routine nasal or nasopharyngeal swabs are highly inaccurate due to heavy anterior nares colonization (with S. aureus and commensal flora) and are NOT recommended. Microbiologic diagnosis in adults can be obtained via rigid nasal endoscopy with middle meatal culture (yielding 80% sensitivity and 90% specificity compared to sinus puncture), but endoscopic middle meatal cultures are unreliable in children due to high baseline colonization. Direct maxillary sinus puncture via transnasal inferior turbinate approach with quantitative culture (threshold of 10 to the 4th power CFU/mL) remains the absolute gold standard for microbiologic investigation. In invasive fungal sinusitis, urgent KOH calcofluor white preparation, frozen section histopathology demonstrating mucosal hyphal tissue invasion, and fungal cultures are required.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Direct maxillary sinus puncture and aspiration with quantitative bacterial culture (colony count 10 to the 4th power CFU/mL or greater) serves as the definitive gold standard diagnostic test for establishing sinus infection microbiology (though acute bacterial sinusitis in routine clinical practice is diagnosed based on validated clinical criteria).

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:** Supportive care includes large-volume nasal saline irrigation or hypertonic saline washes (improving mucociliary clearance, reducing edema, and clearing crusts); oral analgesics (acetaminophen or ibuprofen) for facial pain and fever; and watchful waiting / initial observation for 3 days (AAP option for mild-to-moderate persistent presentation in children) or up to 7 days with a safety-net prescription (AAO-HNSF option for uncomplicated adult sinusitis). Systemic decongestants, oral antihistamines, topical intranasal steroids, and topical decongestants provide minimal to no proven benefit in acute bacterial sinusitis and are NOT routinely recommended by IDSA guidelines, although intranasal corticosteroids are recommended in chronic rhinosinusitis or underlying allergic rhinitis.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:** Standard-dose oral Amoxicillin-potassium clavulanate (45 mg/kg/day divided twice daily in children; 500 mg PO three times daily or 875 mg PO twice daily in adults) or oral Amoxicillin (45 to 90 mg/kg/day in children) for uncomplicated cases without risk factors for resistance. High-dose oral Amoxicillin-potassium clavulanate (90 mg/kg/day of amoxicillin component with 6.4 mg/kg/day of clavulanate divided twice daily in children; 2000 mg PO twice daily extended-release in adults) is recommended for high-risk patients: regions with 10% or greater nonsusceptible S. pneumoniae, severe infection, age under 2 years or over 65 years, day care attendance, recent hospitalization, or antibiotic use within the past 30 days. Standard treatment duration is 10 days (or 7 days after complete resolution of signs and symptoms).
- **Alternative Regimens:** For penicillin-allergic patients (non-type I mild allergy): Oral second- or third-generation cephalosporins including Cefdinir (14 mg/kg/day divided once or twice daily), Cefuroxime axetil (30 mg/kg/day divided twice daily), or Cefpodoxime proxetil (10 mg/kg/day divided twice daily); or Cefixime (8 mg/kg/day) combined with Clindamycin (30 to 40 mg/kg/day) or Linezolid to ensure pneumococcal and staphylococcal coverage. For severe type I beta-lactam allergy in adults: Respiratory fluoroquinolones (Levofloxacin 500 mg PO daily or Moxifloxacin 400 mg PO daily) or Doxycycline (100 mg PO twice daily or 200 mg daily). Macrolides (azithromycin, clarithromycin) and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole are NOT recommended as primary empiric agents due to high rates of resistance in S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae. For treatment failure after 48 to 72 hours on initial therapy: Switch to high-dose amoxicillin-clavulanate, levofloxacin, or cefixime plus linezolid. For severe, complicated, or inpatient sinusitis: Intravenous Ampicillin-sulbactam (1.5 to 3 g IV every 6 hours), Ceftriaxone (1 to 2 g IV daily) plus Metronidazole (500 mg IV every 6 to 8 hours), or Levofloxacin (750 mg IV daily). For acute invasive fungal sinusitis: Urgent surgical débridement plus intravenous Liposomal Amphotericin B (5 mg/kg/day) or Isavuconazole / Voriconazole.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :** Surgical intervention is NOT indicated for uncomplicated acute bacterial sinusitis. Immediate surgical intervention (Functional Endoscopic Sinus Surgery (FESS), antral washout, or open surgical drainage) is mandatory for intraorbital complications (orbital abscess, large subperiosteal abscess failing 24 to 48 hours of medical therapy), intracranial complications (epidural/subdural empyema, brain abscess, Pott puffy tumor osteomyelitis), severe acute refractory sinusitis failing parenteral antimicrobial therapy, and acute invasive fungal sinusitis (requiring urgent wide surgical débridement of necrotic tissues). Functional Endoscopic Sinus Surgery (FESS) is utilized in chronic rhinosinusitis unresponsive to maximal medical therapy to restore osteomeatal complex drainage and remove diseased mucosa or polyps.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:** Extracranial / Orbital complications (arising primarily from ethmoid sinusitis spreading across the paper-thin lamina papyracea or via retrograde valveless veins) include periorbital / preseptal edema, orbital cellulitis, subperiosteal abscess, orbital abscess (causing proptosis, chemosis, ophthalmoplegia, and vision loss), and visual impairment/blindness. Intracranial complications (arising primarily from frontal, ethmoid, or sphenoid sinusitis) include Subdural Empyema (most common intracranial complication), Epidural Abscess, Intraparenchymal Brain Abscess, Bacterial Meningitis, and Septic Venous Sinus Thrombosis (Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis and Superior Sagittal / Lateral Sinus Thrombosis). Osseous complications include Pott Puffy Tumor (subperiosteal abscess and osteomyelitis of the frontal bone presenting as forehead swelling).
- **Long-term Sequelae:** Long-term sequelae include chronic rhinosinusitis with or without nasal polyposis, permanent visual impairment or blindness, persistent cranial nerve palsies (CN III, IV, V1/V2, VI deficits from cavernous sinus thrombosis or petrous apicitis), long-term focal neurological deficits or seizure disorders following intracranial abscesses, structural sinonasal destruction, and recurrent orbital or intracranial suppurative infections.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Double sickening" (classic Scandinavian descriptor for secondary bacterial worsening following initial viral URI improvement); "Pott puffy tumor" (subperiosteal abscess and frontal bone osteomyelitis presenting as a doughy forehead mass); "Lamina papyracea" (paper-thin bony partition separating ethmoid air cells from the orbit, serving as a low-resistance pathway for orbital extension); "Osteomeatal complex" (anatomical drainage bottleneck comprising the middle meatus, hiatus semilunaris, and infundibulum); "Hiatus semilunaris" (curved anatomical groove into which the maxillary, anterior ethmoid, and frontal sinuses drain); "Pituitary gland / Pyx / Pituita" (historical Latin origin where nasal mucus was believed to emanate from fluid at the base of the brain through the hypophysis); "Trocar" (historical surgical instrument used for transnasal or alveolar sinus puncture); and "Air-fluid level / Opacification on CT" (radiographic findings that are non-specific and present in up to 90% of uncomplicated viral common colds).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:** Abnormal sinus imaging (mucosal thickening, complete opacification, air-fluid levels) on plain radiographs, CT, or MRI is NOT diagnostic of acute bacterial sinusitis, as over 50% to 90% of patients with uncomplicated viral colds or asymptomatic state exhibit identical sinus imaging abnormalities; diagnosis must be established on clinical grounds. Staphylococcus aureus is NOT a significant cause of uncomplicated acute community-acquired bacterial sinusitis (isolated in less than 3% of direct sinus aspirates), and studies claiming high S. aureus rates rely on middle meatal swabs contaminated by heavy anterior nares colonization; however, S. aureus IS a major pathogen in sinogenic orbital and intracranial complications, nosocomial ICU sinusitis, and chronic post-surgical disease. Antihistamines, oral decongestants, and topical nasal steroids provide no significant benefit in acute bacterial sinusitis and are not recommended for routine acute therapy, despite widespread over-the-counter use. Bacterial sinusitis can occur in infants as young as neonates because the maxillary and ethmoid sinuses are present at birth, whereas the frontal sinuses do not develop until age 5 to 6 years and sphenoid sinuses pneumatize later in childhood. While macrolides (azithromycin) are frequently prescribed in clinical practice for sore throat and sinus complaints, current IDSA guidelines explicitly advise AGAINST macrolides for empiric treatment of acute bacterial sinusitis due to high pneumococcal resistance (exceeding 30%) and poor activity against Haemophilus influenzae.
