# Otitis Media

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Acute Otitis Media (AOM) is defined as an acute illness characterized by moderate-to-severe bulging of the tympanic membrane (TM) or new onset of otorrhea not due to acute otitis externa, accompanied by acute signs of systemic illness and signs or symptoms of middle ear inflammation. Otitis Media with Effusion (OME) is defined by the presence of middle ear fluid without acute signs of illness or inflammation of the middle ear mucosa, typically following an episode of AOM or resulting from barotrauma or allergy. Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media (CSOM) involves persistent perforation of the tympanic membrane with chronic purulent drainage from the middle ear and mastoid cavity lasting for months or longer.
- **Major Classifications:** Classifications include Acute Otitis Media (further stratified into severe AOM characterized by toxic appearance, persistent otalgia for more than 48 hours, or temperature of 39 degrees C (102.2 degrees F) or higher in the past 48 hours versus nonsevere AOM; unilateral versus bilateral AOM; and AOM with otorrhea versus AOM without otorrhea); Otitis Media with Effusion (non-acute fluid accumulation without mucosal inflammation); Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media (persistent TM perforation with chronic discharge); Recurrent Acute Otitis Media (defined as 2 or more episodes in the first 6 months of life, or 3 or more episodes in 6 months, or 4 or more episodes in 1 year with at least 1 in the preceding 6 months); and distinct clinical syndromes such as Conjunctivitis-AOM Syndrome (associated primarily with nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae) and Bullous Myringitis (presence of hemorrhagic bullae on the TM).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:** Peak incidence occurs in the first 3 years of life, specifically with debut peaks at 0 to 6 months, 7 to 18 months, and 19 months to 7 years, declining in school-aged children, adolescents, and adults. Debut at 0 to 6 months is a powerful predictor of recurrent or complex disease (64% developing 4 or more episodes by age 7 years). Higher frequency occurs in males and individuals with a family history (siblings or parents), with twin studies and cytokine gene polymorphisms (TNFA, IL6, IL10, LRT4) confirming genetic susceptibility. Significantly increased incidence and severity occur in Native Americans, Alaskan and Canadian Eskimos, and Australian Aboriginals. Anatomic risk factors include cleft palate, submucous cleft, cleft uvula, patulous eustachian tube, Down syndrome, and adenoidal hypertrophy. Immunologic risk factors include HIV/AIDS (elevated incidence beginning at 6 months of age), antibody deficiencies against encapsulated bacteria, and subtle vaccine antigen non-responsiveness. Environmental risk factors include large-group daycare attendance (21% receiving myringotomy/tubes vs 3% in home care), passive tobacco smoke exposure (documented by cotinine levels), lack of breastfeeding (breastfeeding for 3 or more months is protective), air pollution (sulfur dioxide levels), and winter/spring respiratory viral seasons.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):** Bacterial pathogens are led by Streptococcus pneumoniae, nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae (NTHi), and Moraxella catarrhalis in nearly equal distribution in the post-pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13) era. Pneumococcal serotypes 15A, 15B, 15C, 21, 23A, 23B, 11A, and previously multidrug-resistant 19A predominate among non-vaccine strains. Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus) accounts for approximately 3.1% of AOM episodes and is disproportionately present in cases with spontaneous TM perforation. Staphylococcus aureus (including MSSA and MRSA) is uncommon in acute primary AOM but frequent in persistent otorrhea following tympanostomy tube placement. Viral pathogens are detected in middle ear fluids in 25% of cases and in the nasopharynx in up to 50%, frequently co-infecting alongside bacteria to increase clinical severity and impede bacterial clearance. Key viruses include Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), Influenza viruses (A and B), Rhinoviruses, Coronaviruses, Enteroviruses, Human Metapneumovirus, and Parainfluenza viruses.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:** In neonates and infants under 2 months of age, etiology includes gram-negative enteric bacilli (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella species, Pseudomonas aeruginosa), Group B Streptococcus (Streptococcus agalactiae), Streptococcus pneumoniae, nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae, and Chlamydia trachomatis (associated with afebrile pneumonia and middle ear infection in infants under 6 months). In infants and children aged 6 months to 5 years, etiology is dominated by nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Moraxella catarrhalis, and common respiratory viruses. In older children, adolescents, and adults, primary pathogens remain Streptococcus pneumoniae, nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, Group A Streptococcus, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (which can cause concomitant middle ear symptoms during atypical pneumonia).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:** Candida species (such as Candida albicans) and Aspergillus species (such as Aspergillus niger) occur in middle ear fluids of immunodeficient patients with chronic suppurative OM. Pseudomonas aeruginosa occurs in chronic suppurative OM, persistent TM perforations, and necrotizing ear infections. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and biofilm-forming organisms occur on tympanostomy tubes. Rare or unusual pathogens include Corynebacterium diphtheriae (diphtheritic otitis), Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (Mycobacterium chelonae), Clostridium tetani (otogenous tetanus), and Ascaris lumbricoides. Non-infectious disease mimickers include Wegener granulomatosis (granulomatosis with polyangiitis).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:** The middle ear space is an anatomical extension of the upper respiratory tract continuous with the nares, nasopharynx, eustachian tube, and mastoid air cell system, lined with ciliated respiratory epithelium and mucus-secreting goblet cells. Eustachian tube dysfunction is the central mechanism in AOM development, compromising its three physiological functions: middle ear protection from nasopharyngeal secretions, fluid drainage into the nasopharynx, and gas pressure ventilation. Viral upper respiratory infections induce mucosal hyperemia, secretion, and edema in the nasopharynx and eustachian tube, resulting in obstruction at the narrow isthmus. Obstruction leads to negative middle ear pressure, fluid accumulation, and reduced mucociliary clearance. Nasopharyngeal bacterial pathogens (S. pneumoniae, NTHi, M. catarrhalis) are aspirated or refluxed into the trapped, stagnant middle ear fluid, rapidly proliferating to establish acute bacterial middle ear infection.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:** Infection triggers a robust inflammatory response with dense neutrophil infiltration, mucosal swelling, and purulent exudate accumulation within the non-yielding bony middle ear cavity. Rising intratympanic fluid pressure causes intense membrane stretch, severe otalgia, and outward bulging of the tympanic membrane. Excessive pressure can compromise tympanic membrane vascular perfusion, causing focal ischemic necrosis and spontaneous TM perforation with purulent otorrhea. Bacterial biofilms on middle ear mucosa contribute significantly to persistent middle ear effusion (OME), treatment failure, and chronic recurrent otitis media. Persistent inflammation leads to mucosal hypertrophy, ossicular erosion, tympanosclerosis, adhesive otitis media, epidermoid ingrowth (cholesteatoma), and conductive or sensorineural hearing loss.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):** Acute onset of severe ear pain (otalgia), often causing sudden nighttime awakening; ear pulling, tugging, or rubbing in non-verbal infants; acute ear discharge (otorrhea) following spontaneous perforation or via tympanostomy tubes; sensation of ear fullness, pressure, or clogged hearing; mild to moderate conductive hearing loss; tinnitus, vertigo, or balance instability; systemic signs including moderate-to-high fever, marked irritability, inconsolable crying, lethargy, anorexia, vomiting, and diarrhea; and a preceding history of viral upper respiratory infection (rhinorrhea, nasal congestion, cough, sore throat).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):** Otoscopic examination reveals moderate-to-severe bulging of the tympanic membrane; intense erythema or fiery red appearance of the TM; opacity, cloudiness, or loss of normal TM translucency and malleus landmarks; visible purulent fluid or air-fluid levels behind the TM; active purulent otorrhea emerging through a TM perforation or tympanostomy tube; and hemorrhagic bullae on the TM or osseous canal wall in bullous myringitis. Pneumatic otoscopy demonstrates significantly decreased, dampened, or absent mobility of the tympanic membrane under positive and negative pressure.
- **Pathognomonic Features:** Moderate-to-severe bulging of the tympanic membrane or new onset of purulent otorrhea not caused by otitis externa, accompanied by acute signs of illness and middle ear inflammation; sudden, dramatic cessation of severe ear pain immediately preceding the onset of purulent ear discharge (signifying acute spontaneous TM perforation); and the conjunctivitis-otitis media syndrome, characterized by concurrent acute purulent conjunctivitis and acute otitis media (highly specific for nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae).

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):** Systemic laboratory testing (CBC, ESR, CRP) is non-specific, generally normal, and NOT routinely indicated for uncomplicated AOM in outpatient settings. Complete blood count (CBC) showing leukocytosis with a left shift, elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and blood cultures are reserved for neonates under 60 days of age, toxic-appearing patients, critically ill individuals, immunodeficient hosts, or those with suspected systemic or intracranial suppurative complications.
- **Imaging Modalities:** Diagnostic imaging (plain radiographs, CT, MRI) is NOT recommended or indicated for routine uncomplicated AOM or OME. Clinical office diagnostic modalities include pneumatic otoscopy (essential standard to evaluate TM mobility), tympanometry (electroacoustic impedance bridge recording TM compliance and middle ear pressure to confirm fluid), and acoustic reflectometry (spectral gradient analysis measuring sound reflectivity). High-resolution Computed Tomography (CT) of the temporal bone with contrast is indicated for suspected acute coalescent mastoiditis, petrous apicitis, or bony septal destruction. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) with contrast is indicated for suspected intracranial complications (subdural empyema, brain abscess, dural sinus thrombosis).
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):** Routine throat or nasopharyngeal cultures are not predictive of middle ear microbiology and are not recommended. Tympanocentesis (needle aspiration of middle ear effusion) with Gram stain and quantitative culture is indicated in select high-risk scenarios: critically ill or toxic patients, failure of initial antimicrobial therapy after 48 to 72 hours in a toxic patient, neonates/infants under 60 days, immunodeficient hosts, and severe recurrent AOM to guide targeted therapy. Otorrhea swab cultures are obtained when spontaneous perforation or tube otorrhea is present. Reverse transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR) and multiplex nucleic acid amplification panels are utilized in specialized settings to detect viral and fastidious bacterial pathogens.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Pneumatic otoscopy demonstrating moderate-to-severe bulging or fluid with impaired mobility and acute inflammation serves as the clinical gold standard. Tympanocentesis (needle aspiration of middle ear fluid) followed by Gram stain and bacterial culture serves as the microbiological gold standard.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:** Pain management is mandatory for all patients with AOM: oral systemic analgesics (acetaminophen or ibuprofen) for mild-to-moderate pain; topical otic analgesics (procaine or lidocaine preparations) for children 2 years of age or older ONLY if the TM is intact without perforation or tubes. Initial observation without immediate antibiotics (for 48 to 72 hours with guaranteed follow-up) is structured by age and severity: infants < 6 months require immediate antibiotic treatment regardless of severity; children 6 months to 2 years require immediate antibiotics for severe AOM, otorrhea, or bilateral AOM, but observation is an option for nonsevere unilateral AOM without otorrhea; children >= 2 years require immediate antibiotics for severe AOM or otorrhea, but observation is an option for nonsevere unilateral or bilateral AOM without otorrhea. Severe AOM is defined as toxic appearance, persistent otalgia > 48 hours, temperature >= 39 degrees C (102.2 degrees F) in past 48 hours, or uncertain follow-up.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:** High-dose oral Amoxicillin at 80 to 90 mg/kg/day divided into 2 equal daily doses is the preferred initial drug for patients without recent beta-lactam exposure within 30 days, without concomitant purulent conjunctivitis, and without penicillin allergy. High-dose oral Amoxicillin-Clavulanate at 90 mg/kg/day of amoxicillin component with 6.4 mg/kg/day of clavulanate (14:1 formulation) divided into 2 doses is preferred if the patient received amoxicillin in the past 30 days, has concomitant purulent conjunctivitis, or fails initial amoxicillin.
- **Alternative Regimens:** For treatment failure after 48 to 72 hours of initial amoxicillin or observation, switch to high-dose oral Amoxicillin-Clavulanate (90 mg/kg/day) or intramuscular/intravenous Ceftriaxone at 50 mg/kg once daily for 1 to 3 consecutive days. For non-type I (mild/non-anaphylactic) penicillin allergy, use oral cephalosporins: Cefdinir (14 mg/kg/day in 1 or 2 doses), Cefuroxime axetil (30 mg/kg/day in 2 doses), Cefpodoxime proxetil (10 mg/kg/day in 2 doses), or Ceftriaxone IM/IV. For severe type I (anaphylactic) penicillin allergy, use macrolides: Azithromycin (10 mg/kg on day 1, then 5 mg/kg/day on days 2 to 5), Clarithromycin (15 mg/kg/day in 2 doses), Erythromycin-Sulfisoxazole, or Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole. For acute otorrhea in children with tympanostomy tubes or TM perforation, topical fluoroquinolone otic drops (Ofloxacin otic or Ciprofloxacin-Dexamethasone otic suspension) are preferred.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :** Myringotomy (surgical incision of the TM) to drain purulent fluid, providing immediate pain relief and indicated for severe toxic illness, intractable otalgia, persistent treatment failure, or acute mastoiditis. Tympanostomy tube placement (collar-button ventilating tubes) inserted through a TM incision to provide continuous middle ear drainage and ventilation, indicated for recurrent AOM (3 or more episodes in 6 months, or 4 or more in 12 months), persistent OME lasting 3 months or longer with hearing loss, or AOM caused by multidrug-resistant S. pneumoniae. Adenoidectomy is recommended in combination with tympanostomy tubes in children 4 years of age or older undergoing surgery for persistent OME or recurrent AOM.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:** Spontaneous tympanic membrane perforation (most common acute complication, causing purulent otorrhea and sudden pain relief); Acute mastoiditis (suppurative extension into mastoid air cells with bony septum necrosis, postauricular erythema, tenderness, edema, and outward/downward pinna displacement); Facial nerve palsy (CN VII weakness from inflammatory canalicular compression); Labyrinthitis (suppurative or serous toxin extension through the round window causing acute vertigo, nystagmus, and sensorineural hearing loss); Gradenigo syndrome (petrous apicitis featuring the triad of periorbital pain from CN V involvement, diplopia from CN VI palsy, and otorrhea); and Intracranial extension (epidural abscess, subdural empyema, meningitis, temporal lobe or cerebellar brain abscess, and lateral/sigmoid sinus septic thrombophlebitis).
- **Long-term Sequelae:** Conductive hearing loss (secondary to persistent OME, ossicular chain erosion, or fixation); Sensorineural hearing loss (secondary to labyrinthine injury); Speech, language, and cognitive developmental delay (resulting from chronic fluctuating conductive hearing loss during early childhood); Tympanosclerosis and adhesive otitis media (TM mucosal calcification, TM collapse onto ossicles, and middle ear obliteration); Cholesteatoma (keratinizing squamous epithelial retraction sac with progressive bone and ossicular destruction); and Chronic suppurative otitis media with persistent TM perforation.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:** "Thumb sign" (classic lateral neck radiograph marker for epiglottitis, contrasted with otic disease); "Early and often" (epidemiological concept establishing that AOM debut within the first 6 months of life strongly predicts recurrent or complex otitis media); "Conjunctivitis-otitis media syndrome" (concurrent purulent conjunctivitis and AOM, characteristic of nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae); "Bullous myringitis" (hemorrhagic bullae on the TM, historically linked to Mycoplasma pneumoniae); "Collar-button tubes" (descriptive term for tympanostomy ventilating tubes); and "Pneumatic otoscopy mobility dampening" (key physical diagnostic sign).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:** Redness of the tympanic membrane alone is NOT diagnostic of AOM, as TM vascular engorgement occurs frequently from crying, fever, or non-infectious URI without middle ear fluid or infection; bulging of the TM is required for diagnostic certainty. Mycoplasma pneumoniae causes bullous myringitis in experimental inoculation trials, but clinical aspirate studies confirm M. pneumoniae is isolated in less than 1% of clinical AOM cases, and bullous myringitis in practice is caused by standard bacterial otopathogens (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis). Daily antibiotic chemoprophylaxis (amoxicillin or sulfisoxazole) is explicitly discouraged in current guidelines due to selection of multidrug-resistant bacterial strains; tympanostomy tube placement is preferred. Antihistamines, decongestants, and systemic or nasal corticosteroids are explicitly NOT recommended for treating otitis media with effusion, as clinical trials show no efficacy and increased adverse effects.
