# Laryngitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Acute laryngitis is a self-limited clinical syndrome characterized by the sudden onset of hoarseness, a husky voice, decreased phonation, reduced voice projection, voice breaks, or complete aphonia, usually occurring in the setting of or following an acute upper respiratory tract infection with cough, rhinorrhea, and sore throat.
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Temporal Classifications:
     - Acute Laryngitis: Short-duration laryngeal inflammation lasting from several days up to 3 weeks (median duration 3 days, 75th percentile 5.5 days in common cold cohorts).
     - Chronic Laryngitis / Chronic Laryngotracheitis: Persistent laryngeal inflammation and hoarseness lasting longer than 3 weeks to 6 weeks, often associated with atypical or persistent pathogens (Bordetella pertussis, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, MSSA, MRSA, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Serratia marcescens, Mycobacterium tuberculosis) or noninfectious triggers.
  2. Etiologic Classifications:
     - Viral Laryngitis (Most common etiology, accounting for the vast majority of acute self-limited cases).
     - Bacterial Laryngitis (Primary or secondary bacterial involvement including Group A Streptococcus, Moraxella catarrhalis, Haemophilus influenzae, Diphtheria, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, MRSA).
     - Fungal & Mycobacterial Laryngitis (Uncommon or opportunistic etiologies including Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Candida, Cryptococcus neoformans, Histoplasma capsulatum, Blastomyces dermatitidis, Coccidioides immitis/posadasii, Sporothrix schenckii, Mucormycosis).
     - Noninfectious Laryngitis (Vocal abuse / vocal strain, gastroesophageal reflux disease / GERD, environmental tobacco smoke, laryngeal malignancy).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Population Prevalence & Incidence: Approximately 1% of all medical care claims are filed for dysphonia, with 42% of those receiving a primary diagnosis of acute laryngitis. Over 20% of adults aged 65 years or older are diagnosed with acute laryngitis at least once over a 5-year period. In the United States, approximately 18 million adults (7.6% of the population) report a voice disturbance annually, with acute laryngitis secondary to a respiratory infection representing the single most frequent diagnosis (17.8% of voice problems). Among children and adults presenting with acute respiratory symptoms, 2% receive a primary diagnosis of laryngitis.
  2. Demographics & Seasonality: Acute laryngitis is diagnosed more frequently in women (mean age, 36 years) than in men (mean age, 41 years). Cases occur year-round but demonstrate a distinct seasonal peak during the colder months (winter), where case frequency is nearly double that observed in warmer seasons.
  3. High-Morbidity Populations: Substantial occupational and functional morbidity occurs in intercollegiate athletes, who report laryngitis significantly more often as a cause of missed athletic practice and impaired performance compared with cough, nasal discharge, or myalgia. High prevalence is also documented in school-aged children and adolescents (noted in 22% of those presenting with nonstreptococcal sore throat).
  4. Environmental & Host Risk Factors: Viral upper respiratory tract exposure, cigarette smoking (identified as a major risk factor for symptomatic atypical bacterial respiratory disease), occupational voice strain, inhaled corticosteroid use (predisposing to laryngeal candidiasis or leishmaniasis), and underlying immunocompromising conditions.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Viral Pathogens (Primary Etiologies): Rhinoviruses (25% to 29% frequency), Influenza A and B viruses (28% to 35% frequency; hoarseness reported in 29% of influenza cases vs 2.3% of Group A streptococcal cases), Parainfluenza viruses (5% to 25% frequency; HPIV-1, HPIV-2, HPIV-3), Adenoviruses (22% to 35% frequency), Coronaviruses (25% frequency), Human Metapneumovirus (hMPV; 3% to 91% frequency; hoarseness reported in 91% of young adults with hMPV vs 42% with RSV), Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV; 42% frequency in young adults).
  - Bacterial Pathogens (Primary, Secondary, or Co-Infecting Agents): Group A beta-hemolytic Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes; 2.3% to 19% frequency), Moraxella catarrhalis (isolated from nasopharyngeal cultures in 55% of adults with acute laryngitis vs 14% of controls in Swedish case-control studies), Haemophilus influenzae (recovered in 8% to 20% of adult laryngitis cases), Chlamydia pneumoniae (Chlamydophila pneumoniae; 12% to 30% frequency; diagnosed in 12.2% of children with RT-PCR-confirmed acute respiratory infections), Mycoplasma pneumoniae (3% to 37% frequency).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children: Human parainfluenza viruses (HPIV-2 associated with hoarseness in 23.9% of infected children vs 5.5% in HPIV-3), Human metapneumovirus (hMPV; primary diagnosis in 3.3% of children aged 1 month to 14 years hospitalized with acute respiratory illness), Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), Adenovirus, Rhinovirus, Group A beta-hemolytic Streptococcus, Chlamydia pneumoniae (12.2% of pediatric acute respiratory cases), Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Corynebacterium diphtheriae (in unimmunized children globally).
  - Adults & Older Adults: Rhinovirus, Influenza A and B, Coronaviruses (hoarseness in 25% of hospitalized older adults with coronavirus/rhinovirus), Human metapneumovirus (91% in young adults), Moraxella catarrhalis (55% isolation rate), Haemophilus influenzae (8% to 20%), Group A Streptococcus, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Bordetella pertussis (chronic laryngotracheitis >6 weeks), Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA and MRSA), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Serratia marcescens, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Mycobacteria & Higher Bacteria: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (laryngeal TB; historically presenting with cranial nerve palsies and ulcerative lesions of the posterior larynx, now increasingly presenting as anterior tumor-like masses, odynophagia, or suspected carcinoma; only 55% have prior pulmonary TB diagnosis), Actinomyces species (primary laryngeal actinomycosis in immunosuppressed hosts).
  - Fungi & Molds: Candida species (mucosal candidiasis), Cryptococcus neoformans (laryngeal cryptococcosis causing hoarseness and stridor), Histoplasma capsulatum (laryngeal histoplasmosis; complication of disseminated or primary infection presenting with indolent hoarseness without cough), Blastomyces dermatitidis (laryngeal blastomycosis; mistaken for squamous cell carcinoma due to pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia on biopsy), Coccidioides immitis/posadasii, Sporothrix schenckii (causing stridor in young children), Mucormycosis (laryngotracheal mucormycosis), Aspergillus species.
  - Viral & Opportunistic Agents: Herpes simplex virus types 1 and 2 (HSV-1, HSV-2; presenting as glottic/supraglottic edema, vesiculoulcerative lesions, or acute airway obstruction with or without vocal cord paralysis), Varicella-zoster virus (VZV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Parvovirus B19 (associated with laryngitis and neutropenia), Human papillomavirus types 6 and 11 (HPV-6, HPV-11; recurrent respiratory papillomatosis causing hoarseness, stridor, and chronic cough), SARS-associated coronavirus.
  - Rare / Fastidious Bacteria & Parasites: Corynebacterium diphtheriae (diphtheritic laryngitis / pseudomembrane formation), Group G beta-hemolytic Streptococcus (causing upper airway obstruction), Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA; severe infectious laryngitis), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Serratia marcescens, Microsporidium (Anncaliia algerae vocal cord infection), Clinostomum complanatum (laryngeal trematode / parasite infection), Leishmania species (laryngeal leishmaniasis in patients taking inhaled corticosteroids).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Inoculation & Viral Replication: Inhalation of aerosolized droplets or direct mucosal transfer of respiratory viruses into the upper respiratory tract. Viruses infect and replicate within the ciliated columnar epithelial cells lining the laryngeal mucosa and true vocal folds.
  - Inflammatory Cascade & Hyperemia: Local viral replication triggers the host immune response, releasing inflammatory mediators (such as bradykinin and prostaglandins) that induce intense vascular engorgement, mucosal hyperemia, and capillary leakage.
  - Vocal Fold Edema & Biomechanical Alteration: Fluid accumulates in the interstitial space (lamina propria / Reinke space) of the true vocal cords, causing marked tissue swelling and edema. Increased vocal cord mass and altered mucosal compliance disrupt normal mucosal wave dynamics and vocal fold vibration frequency during phonation. Swelling prevents complete glottic closure during speech, leading to air leakage, voice breaks, pitch lowering, raspy hoarseness, or complete loss of voice (aphonia).
  - Chronic Granulomatous & Invasive Mechanisms: In atypical or chronic granulomatous laryngitis (tuberculosis, blastomycosis, histoplasmosis), pathogens infiltrate the submucosa, triggering chronic granulomatous inflammation, pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia, tissue ulceration, or exophytic tumor-like mass formation that fixedly impairs vocal fold mobility or narrows the glottic lumen.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Vascular Engorgement & Erythema: Direct laryngeal inspection demonstrates intense vascular engorgement, diffuse mucosal hyperemic redness, and swelling involving the true and ventricular vocal folds.
  - Secretion Accumulation & Cough Reflex: Mucosal inflammation promotes hypersecretion of mucus and accumulation of cellular debris along the glottis. Secretion stasis directly stimulates airway sensory nerves (mediated by bradykinin and tachykinin), provoking a dry, hacking, nonproductive cough.
  - Structural & Neuromuscular Damage: In severe necrotizing or ulcerative viral infections (HSV, VZV, CMV) or invasive bacterial/fungal infections, deep mucosal ulceration, pseudomembrane formation, or neuritis can result in vocal cord paralysis, glottic narrowing, subglottic extension, stridor, and potential upper airway compromise.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Primary Laryngeal Symptoms: Recent, abrupt onset of hoarseness, a husky or raspy voice, decreased voice projection, voice breaks, lowering of vocal pitch, and episodes of aphonia (complete loss of voice).
  - Associated Respiratory & Systemic Symptoms: Dry, hacking cough (frequently provoked by laryngeal sensory irritation or mucus vibration); sore throat; odynophagia (pain on swallowing); persistent throat clearing; rhinorrhea; nasal congestion; malaise; and low-grade fever occurring in the context of an upper respiratory tract infection.
  - Symptoms in Chronic or Atypical Presentations: Indolent onset of progressive hoarseness lasting longer than 3 to 6 weeks, chronic throat clearing, odynophagia, absence of cough (characteristic of laryngeal histoplasmosis), stridor, or substernal chest pain with dysphagia (suggesting concomitant herpes simplex esophagitis).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Vocal & Acoustic Examination Signs: Dysphonia, coarse or raspy hoarseness, breathy voice quality, pitch instability, or complete aphonia.
  - Oropharyngeal & General Physical Signs: Mild pharyngeal erythema, rhinorrhea, nasal mucosal congestion, low-grade fever (or normal body temperature), absence of tonsillar exudates (differentiating from Group A streptococcal pharyngitis), and absence of toxic prostration, drooling, or tripod positioning (differentiating from acute epiglottitis).
  - Direct Visual Laryngeal Signs (via Laryngoscopy / Endoscopy):
    - Edema and vascular engorgement of the laryngeal mucous membranes.
    - Intense hyperemia and bright erythematous appearance of the true vocal folds (vocal cords) and ventricular (false) vocal folds.
    - Thick, tenacious mucus secretions adhering to the vocal fold margins.
    - Findings in atypical etiologies: Vesicular or ulcerative lesions (HSV, VZV, CMV), pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia or exophytic tumor-like masses (Blastomycosis, Histoplasmosis, Tuberculosis), pseudomembranes (Diphtheria), or glottic/supraglottic edema with vocal cord paralysis.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Recent onset of hoarseness with decreased voice projection and voice breaks following an acute viral upper respiratory tract illness (classic clinical presentation of acute laryngitis).
  - Direct visualization of hyperemic, intensely erythematous, edematous true vocal folds with vascular engorgement of the laryngeal mucosa on laryngoscopy.
  - Demonstration of pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia on laryngeal biopsy in a patient presenting with indolent hoarseness (characteristic histological feature of laryngeal blastomycosis or histoplasmosis).

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Typically normal in uncomplicated viral acute laryngitis. Mild leukocytosis may be present in acute secondary bacterial infections or influenza. Neutropenia may be observed in parvovirus B19 laryngitis.
  - Inflammatory Biomarkers: C-reactive protein (CRP) and Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) are generally normal or minimally elevated in acute viral laryngitis, but markedly elevated in tuberculous, fungal, or invasive bacterial laryngitis.
  - Routine Workup Precaution: Routine blood work is unnecessary and not recommended for typical, self-limited acute laryngitis in primary care.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Plain Radiography: Routine chest radiographs or lateral neck radiographs are NOT indicated for uncomplicated acute laryngitis. Chest radiography is indicated if chronic cough, pulmonary tuberculosis, or underlying parenchymal lung disease is suspected.
  - Computed Tomography (CT) & Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): CT or MRI of the neck/larynx is indicated in chronic laryngitis (symptoms >3 to 6 weeks), progressive stridor, suspected laryngeal malignancy, deep neck space infection, or laryngeal cryptococcosis/mucormycosis.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Routine Diagnostic Workup: Microbiological testing (sputum cultures, throat swabs, routine viral PCRs) is NOT recommended for typical acute laryngitis because the condition is benign, self-limited, and viral.
  - Targeted Diagnostics in Chronic or Atypical Cases:
    - Nasopharyngeal / Viral PCR Panels: Real-time RT-PCR for influenza A/B, parainfluenza (HPIV-1-3), RSV, hMPV, adenovirus, rhinovirus, coronavirus, C. pneumoniae, M. pneumoniae, and B. pertussis in epidemic or severe hospitalized cases.
    - Sputum & Laryngeal Swab Stains / Cultures: Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) Ziehl-Neelsen stain and culture for Mycobacterium tuberculosis; KOH / Calcofluor white mount and fungal cultures for Candida, Cryptococcus, Histoplasma, Blastomyces.
    - Histopathology & Laryngeal Biopsy: Mandatory for chronic hoarseness (>3 to 6 weeks) or non-resolving exophytic/ulcerative laryngeal lesions to perform tissue biopsy, submucosal fungal/AFB staining, and rule out laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - Clinical Diagnosis Based on a Characteristic History of Recent Hoarseness / Dysphonia Following an Upper Respiratory Infection COMBINED WITH Direct Visualization of Hyperemic, Erythematous, Edematous Vocal Folds via Indirect Laryngoscopy or Flexible Fiberoptic Laryngoscopy: Represents the gold standard for confirming acute laryngitis.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Mainstay Supportive Measures:
    - Strict Voice Rest (avoiding both speaking and shouting; avoiding whispering, as whispering generates greater mechanical strain on inflamed vocal cords than soft conversational speech).
    - Inhaled Humidification & Hydration (steam inhalation, room humidifiers, generous oral fluid intake).
    - Analgesics & Antipyretics: Acetaminophen or Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs, e.g., Ibuprofen 250 to 400 mg PO) for laryngeal pain, sore throat, and low-grade fever.
    - Elimination of Vocal Irritants: Complete avoidance of cigarette smoke, alcohol, and caffeine.
  - Airway Monitoring: Monitoring for stridor or respiratory distress (rare in uncomplicated laryngitis, but required if HSV, fungal, or diphtheritic involvement causes glottic/supraglottic compromise).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Uncomplicated Acute Laryngitis Guideline Recommendation: Antibiotics are STRICTLY NOT RECOMMENDED and NOT EFFECTIVE for typical acute laryngitis. Updated Cochrane systematic reviews (2013 and 2015) concluded that antibiotics do NOT show objective clinical benefit over placebo in treating acute laryngitis, despite historical prescription rates.
  - Penicillin V or Erythromycin Study Findings: Randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trials evaluating oral Penicillin V or Erythromycin in adults with acute laryngitis (even those with high M. catarrhalis nasopharyngeal isolation) failed to demonstrate any objective clinical benefit compared to placebo.
  - Specific Targeted Antimicrobial Therapy (When Specific Non-Viral Pathogens are Confirmed):
    - Atypical Bacterial Laryngitis (C. pneumoniae, M. pneumoniae, B. pertussis): Oral Macrolide (Azithromycin 500 mg PO day 1, then 250 mg PO daily on days 2 to 5; or Clarithromycin 500 mg PO twice daily for 7 to 14 days) or Doxycycline (100 mg PO twice daily).
    - Laryngeal Tuberculosis: Standard 6-month 4-drug antituberculous regimen (RIPE: Rifampin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol for 2 months, followed by Rifampin and Isoniazid for 4 months).
    - Laryngeal Fungal Infections (Candida, Cryptococcus, Histoplasma, Blastomyces): Oral Fluconazole (200 to 400 mg PO daily), Itraconazole (200 mg PO twice daily), Voriconazole, or IV Amphotericin B for severe/disseminated disease.
    - Laryngeal Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1, HSV-2): Oral or Intravenous Acyclovir (400 mg PO 5 times daily or 5 mg/kg IV q8h) or Valacyclovir (1000 mg PO twice daily).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Single-Injection Local Anesthetic: Superior laryngeal nerve block with a single local anesthetic injection reported for severe post-laryngitis superior laryngeal neuralgia.
  - Corticosteroid Therapy: Meta-analyses show modest pain reduction with corticosteroids in acute sore throat, but IDSA and NICE guidelines DO NOT recommend routine corticosteroid administration for typical acute laryngitis.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Laryngeal Biopsy & Resection / Laser Ablation: Surgical excision or KTP/CO2 laser ablation required for localized fungal lesions (e.g., laryngeal cryptococcosis), refractory exophytic masses, or persistent laryngeal tissue changes where malignancy must be excluded.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Acute Upper Airway Obstruction & Stridor: Rare complication occurring in severe HSV laryngitis, laryngeal cryptococcosis, Group G streptococcal laryngitis, or diphtheritic membrane formation, requiring emergency airway intervention.
  - Superior Laryngeal Neuralgia: Unilateral severe neuropathic neck pain developing after acute laryngitis, responsive to local anesthetic nerve block.
  - Vocal Cord Hemorrhage & Polyps: Ruptured subepithelial microvessels on swollen vocal cords secondary to coughing or vocal strain during acute inflammation, leading to vocal fold hematoma or permanent vocal polyps.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Chronic Laryngitis & Persistent Dysphonia: Continuation of hoarseness and vocal fatigue lasting longer than 6 weeks, often secondary to persistent infection, vocal misuse, or underlying structural changes.
  - Vocal Cord Paralysis & Permanent Voice Changes: Persistent hoarseness resulting from viral neuritis (HSV, VZV, CMV) or invasive tuberculous/fungal destruction of laryngeal structures.
  - Idiopathic Ulcerative Laryngitis: Persistent ulceration of true vocal cords following acute laryngitis.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Hot potato voice" (muffled vocal quality associated with influenza, streptococcal pharyngitis, or peritonsillar abscess, in contrast to raspy hoarseness in laryngitis).
  - "Whispering strain" (whispering exerts MORE mechanical strain and hyperadduction on inflamed vocal cords than soft conversational speech).
  - "Pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia" (histological hallmark on laryngeal biopsy in fungal blastomycosis or histoplasmosis mimicking squamous cell carcinoma).
  - "Cherry-red vocal folds" (hyperemic, erythematous appearance of the larynx on direct laryngoscopy).
  - "Athletic morbidity" (intercollegiate athletes report laryngitis as a leading cause of missed athletic practice and impaired performance).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Inefficacy of Antibiotics Despite High Bacterial Isolation: Although Moraxella catarrhalis is isolated from the nasopharynx in 55% of adults with acute laryngitis (compared to 14% of controls), treating patients with oral penicillin V or erythromycin yields ZERO objective clinical benefit over placebo, demonstrating that bacterial isolation does not equate to antibiotic indication.
  - Absence of Cough in Laryngeal Histoplasmosis: While cough is the dominant symptom accompanying hoarseness in acute viral laryngitis, laryngeal histoplasmosis presents as an indolent hoarseness with a complete ABSENCE of cough.
  - Changing Epidemiology of Laryngeal Tuberculosis: Historically, laryngeal TB occurred in young patients with known, advanced cavitary pulmonary TB and high sputum organism burden; modern laryngeal TB frequently occurs without recognized pulmonary TB (only 55% have prior pulmonary TB diagnosis), presents as anterior tumor-like masses mimicking carcinoma or odynophagia, and has low sputum AFB positivity rates.
  - Mistaking Laryngeal Fungal Infection for Squamous Cell Carcinoma: Laryngeal blastomycosis and histoplasmosis present with indolent hoarseness, exophytic masses on laryngoscopy, and pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia on tissue biopsy, leading to frequent misdiagnosis as laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma.
  - Whispering Increases Vocal Cord Strain: Patients with acute laryngitis frequently whisper to "rest" their voice; however, video-stroboscopic studies demonstrate that whispering causes hyperadduction of the arytenoid cartilages and generates greater mechanical tension across the vocal folds than quiet conversational speech.
  - Inhaled Corticosteroids as a Risk Factor for Laryngeal Leishmaniasis: Inhalation of corticosteroids (e.g., fluticasone or budesonide inhalers for asthma) alters local mucosal immunity, predisposing patients to rare laryngeal leishmaniasis presenting as chronic hoarseness.
