# Bronchitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Acute bronchitis is a self-limited clinical syndrome distinguished by a relatively brief, acute-onset inflammatory process of the large and mid-sized conducting airways (trachea and bronchi) characterized by cough (dry or productive) lasting less than 3 weeks (typically 7 to 21 days) without clinical or radiographic evidence of acute pneumonia. Acute exacerbation of chronic bronchitis (AECB) or acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) represents an acute worsening of respiratory symptoms (increased dyspnea, increased sputum volume, and/or increased sputum purulence) beyond normal day-to-day variation in patients with preexisting chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or chronic bronchitis.
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Acute Uncomplicated Bronchitis: Brief, self-limited inflammatory infection of large and mid-sized airways in previously healthy individuals without underlying chronic lung disease, primarily caused by respiratory viruses.
  2. Acute Exacerbation of Chronic Bronchitis (AECB) / Acute Exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD): Acute clinical deterioration in patients with chronic bronchitis / COPD categorized by Anthonisen / GOLD clinical criteria:
     - Mild Exacerbation (Type 3): Presence of 1 cardinal symptom (increased dyspnea, increased sputum volume, or increased sputum purulence) plus at least 1 secondary criterion (URI symptoms within 7 days, increased wheezing, fever, or HR/RR increase >20% from baseline).
     - Moderate Exacerbation (Type 2): Presence of 2 cardinal symptoms, with increased sputum purulence as one of the two.
     - Severe Exacerbation (Type 1): Presence of all 3 cardinal symptoms (increased dyspnea, increased sputum volume, and increased sputum purulence) or requiring mechanical ventilation.
  3. Occupational / Industrial / Irritant Bronchitis: Non-infectious or secondary bronchial inflammation caused by environmental exposures (tobacco smoke, secondhand smoke, occupational dust, chemical fumes, burning biomass fuels, particulate matter).
  4. Pediatric Acute Bronchiolitis vs. Bronchitis: Distinct clinical syndrome in infants involving small airways (<2 mm) closely linked to RSV and hMPV, distinguished from large/mid-sized airway bronchitis in older children and adults.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Epidemiology: Acute bronchitis is one of the most common reasons for primary care physician visits across all age groups. In the United States, 5% of individuals report physician-diagnosed acute bronchitis annually (National Health Interview Survey), with peak rates in winter months and in children younger than 5 years old. In the UK, primary care incidence is 54 cases per 1000 persons annually (ranging from 36 per 1000 in young men to 225 per 1000 in adults >85 years). Acute bronchitis accounts for approximately 75% of ambulatory antibiotic prescriptions alongside other acute upper respiratory infections. AECOPD is more frequent in winter months and causes significant hospitalization and mortality worldwide.
  2. Risk Factors for Acute Bronchitis: Active cigarette smoking, exposure to secondhand smoke, environmental air pollution/dusts, seasonal circulating respiratory viruses (winter/fall peaks), contact with young children in daycare, crowded living conditions (military barracks, dormitories), incomplete pertussis vaccination or waning vaccine-induced immunity in adolescents/adults, and underlying asthma or airway hyperreactivity.
  3. Risk Factors for Acute Exacerbations of Chronic Bronchitis / AECOPD: Preexisting COPD (irreversible airflow limitation with reduced FEV1 and FEV1/FVC ratio), history of frequent prior exacerbations (>=3 episodes/year), advanced age (>60-65 years), low baseline FEV1 (<50% predicted), homozygous (PI*ZZ) or heterozygous (PI*MZ) alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, IgA deficiency, HIV infection (promotes matrix metalloproteinase expression and smoking-related emphysema), environmental particulate matter exposure, gastroesophageal reflux / aspiration risk, and seasonal circulating viral infections.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Viral Pathogens (Cause >90% of acute uncomplicated bronchitis cases overall and ~33% of AECOPD cases): Rhinoviruses (most common cause of cold-associated cough; human rhinovirus A, B, C), Influenza A and B viruses (highest efficiency to infect and damage bronchiolar epithelial cells during winter outbreaks), Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV; causes wheezing in all ages, attack rates 3%-5% in adults), Human Metapneumovirus (hMPV; winter/spring outbreaks), Coronaviruses (endemic strains and newer strains), Parainfluenza viruses (types 1-4), Adenoviruses (types 4, 7, and 14 causing outbreaks in military barracks/dormitories), Enteroviruses (Echovirus, Coxsackievirus, Enterovirus D68), Human Bocavirus (often co-infects small children), and Measles virus (prodromal cough in malnourished children).
  - Bacterial Pathogens in Uncomplicated Acute Bronchitis (Account for <=10% of cases): Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and Bordetella pertussis (and Bordetella holmesii). Normal oropharyngeal colonizers (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis) can be isolated from sputum but their pathogenic role in uncomplicated acute bronchitis in healthy hosts is unproven.
  - Bacterial Pathogens in Acute Exacerbations of Chronic Bronchitis / AECOPD (Isolated in ~50% of AECOPD cases): Haemophilus influenzae (nontypeable, most common bacterial isolate in AECOPD), Streptococcus pneumoniae, Moraxella catarrhalis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa (increased prevalence in severe airflow obstruction FEV1 <35%-50%, frequent past antibiotic courses >=4/year, or recent hospitalization within 90 days), Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, and Enterobacteriaceae (Klebsiella pneumoniae, E. coli, Proteus, Serratia). Acquisition of a new bacterial strain is strongly associated with AECOPD.
  - Co-infections: Simultaneous viral-viral co-infection occurs in 10% to 30% of acute bronchitis cases (especially bocavirus in children). Bacterial-viral co-infection occurs in 25% of AECOPD cases, leading to higher bacterial burden, greater lung function decline, and longer hospitalization.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Infants and Children (<5 Years): Viruses predominate (RSV, hMPV, Parainfluenza, Rhinovirus, Adenovirus, Enteroviruses, Bocavirus). Primary Mycoplasma pneumoniae manifests mostly as URI in <3 years, transitioning to bronchitis/pneumonia in older children (>5 years). Bordetella pertussis causes severe paroxysmal illness in non-immunized infants.
  - Adolescents and Young Adults (5 to 40 Years): Mycoplasma pneumoniae (peak incidence ages 5-15 years and college students), Chlamydia pneumoniae, Bordetella pertussis (waning immunity in adolescents), Influenza A and B, Adenoviruses (types 4, 7, 14 in military recruits), and Rhinoviruses.
  - Adults and Older Adults (>65 Years & AECOPD Patients): Influenza A and B, RSV (causes severe lower respiratory illness in elderly/high-risk adults), hMPV, Rhinoviruses, Coronaviruses, nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Moraxella catarrhalis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacteriaceae, and oral anaerobes via microaspiration (Prevotella spp., Veillonella spp.).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Atypical & Zoonotic Pathogens: Chlamydia psittaci (psittacosis from bird inhalation presenting with systemic atypical bronchitis/pneumonia), Coxiella burnetii (Q fever), Bordetella holmesii, Bordetella bronchiseptica (canine pathogen causing human bronchitis in immunocompromised hosts).
  - Opportunistic & Immunocompromised Hosts: Herpes Simplex Virus type 1 (associated with acute hemorrhagic tracheobronchitis in intubated/critically ill adults), Human Papillomavirus (HPV types 6 and 11, producing juvenile or adult laryngotracheal papillomatosis with chronic cough and stridor), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Burkholderia cepacia complex, and atypical mycobacteria.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Entry & Epithelial Adherence: Respiratory viruses and atypical bacteria gain entry via inhalation of aerosolized droplets or self-inoculation of contaminated secretions into conjunctival/nasal mucosa. Pathogens adhere to ciliated respiratory epithelial cells lining the large and mid-sized conducting airways (trachea, mainstem bronchi, bronchioles).
  - Viral Tropism & Cytopathology: Influenza viruses directly infect and lyse bronchiolar epithelial cells, causing submucosal congestion, hemorrhage, and desquamation of necrotic debris that occludes airway lumen. RSV and hMPV infect terminal bronchiolar lining cells. Rhinovirus infects bronchial epithelial cells patchily up to 15 days post-exposure, inducing cytokine release despite lower direct cytopathology compared to influenza.
  - Bacterial Colonization & New Strain Acquisition in AECOPD: Stable COPD airways are frequently colonized by S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis, and oral anaerobes (Prevotella, Veillonella). AECOPD is triggered by the acquisition of a new strain of a potentially pathogenic microorganism or a sudden increase in bacterial load, altering the lower airway microbiome diversity (increasing Proteobacteria) and driving acute inflammatory cascades.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Innate Cytokine Release & Inflammatory Infiltrate: Infection triggers respiratory epithelial cells and resident macrophages to release proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines (type I/III IFNs, TNF-alpha, IL-1 beta, IL-6, IL-8). IL-8 serves as a potent chemoattractant for neutrophils, causing rapid PMN migration into bronchial submucosa and epithelial lining.
  - Mucociliary Dysfunction & Hypersecretion: Submucosal edema, mucosal hyperemia, neutrophil elastase release, and goblet cell hyperplasia cause excessive bronchial mucin secretion (MUC5AC/MUC5B) and loss of ciliary function (squamous metaplasia). Accumulation of thick mucus and cellular debris impairs mucociliary clearance.
  - Airway Hyperreactivity & Bronchospasm: Sensory nerve endings (unmyelinated C-fibers) in bronchial mucosa become hyperreactive due to virus-induced inflammation and mediator release (bradykinin, tachykinins). Airway hyperreactivity and transient bronchospasm can persist for up to 6 to 8 weeks after infection. B. pertussis elaborates bacterial toxins that contribute to prolonged, paroxysmal cough.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Acute Bronchitis (Uncomplicated): Initial prodrome of common cold symptoms (nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, sore throat, low-grade fever, malaise) lasting 1 to 3 days, followed by the onset of cough, which becomes the dominant, defining symptom. Cough may be dry/nonproductive initially, becoming productive of clear, mucoid, or purulent (yellow/green) sputum. Cough typically lasts 7 to 10 days, but can persist for 3 weeks or longer (up to 6 weeks). Retrosternal burning or chest wall discomfort exacerbated by coughing. Wheezing and dyspnea may occur.
  - Acute Exacerbation of Chronic Bronchitis / AECOPD: Sudden, acute worsening of baseline symptoms beyond daily variation, characterized by Anthonisen cardinal symptoms: (1) worsening dyspnea / shortness of breath, (2) increase in sputum volume, and (3) transition of sputum purulence (sputum changing from clear/mucoid to thick yellow or green). Associated with fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, and low-grade fever.
  - Pathogen-Specific Symptom Clues: High fever and prominent systemic prostration suggest Influenza A/B or Adenovirus. Wheezing is more common with RSV or hMPV. Persistent, intractable, exhausting dry cough with minimal phlegm suggests Mycoplasma pneumoniae. Prolonged paroxysmal cough lasting >4 weeks with post-tussive vomiting or inspiratory "whoop" suggests Bordetella pertussis.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Vital Signs: Low-grade fever (<38.0 degrees C; higher fever >=38.5 degrees C suggests influenza, adenovirus, or pneumonia); heart rate and respiratory rate usually normal or mildly elevated. Vital signs are CRITICAL: absence of any vital sign abnormality (RR >20-24, HR >100, Temp >37.8 degrees C) strongly argues against pneumonia (<1% probability).
  - Thoracic & Auscultatory Findings: Normal tactile fremitus and resonant percussion throughout all lung fields. Auscultation reveals coarse crackles (rales) that clear or shift after coughing, diffuse ronchi, and prolonged expiratory phase with scattered wheezing. ABSENCE of definitive signs of focal lobar consolidation (no dullness to percussion, no bronchial breath sounds, no egophony E-to-A changes, no whispered pectoriloquy).
  - Pharyngeal & Upper Airway Signs: Mild pharyngeal erythema, nasal mucosal congestion, or conjunctival injection. Bullous myringitis (vesicles on tympanic membrane) occasionally seen with Mycoplasma pneumoniae.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Acute cough as the dominant complaint in a patient lacking vital sign abnormalities and lacking physical signs of consolidation (hallmark clinical picture distinguishing acute bronchitis from pneumonia).
  - Purulent sputum transition accompanied by increased dyspnea and increased sputum volume in a patient with chronic airflow obstruction (classic Anthonisen cardinal triad for AECOPD).
  - Paroxysmal coughing fits ending in an inspiratory "whoop" or post-tussive emesis (pathognomonic feature of Bordetella pertussis infection).

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Routine CBC is UNNECESSARY for uncomplicated acute bronchitis and generally normal or shows mild lymphocytosis. Leukocytosis with neutrophilia or bandemia suggests bacterial pneumonia or severe AECOPD.
  - Inflammatory Biomarkers (Procalcitonin & CRP):
    - Serum Procalcitonin (PCT): Highly valuable biomarker to differentiate bacterial infection requiring antibiotics from self-limited viral bronchitis. Serum procalcitonin <0.1 ng/mL (or <0.25 mcg/L) reliably identifies acute bronchitis or AECOPD that does NOT require antibiotic therapy, reducing unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions by 50% without compromising clinical outcomes.
    - C-Reactive Protein (CRP): Routine CRP does not reliably discriminate viral bronchitis from bacterial infection; however, low CRP (<10 mg/L) helps rule out pneumonia.
  - Serum Cold Agglutinins: Historical bedside test for Mycoplasma pneumoniae (titer >=1:32 or >=1:64); positive in ~68%-70% of M. pneumoniae cases, but modern molecular testing is preferred.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Chest Radiography (PA and Lateral): Chest X-ray is NOT routinely recommended for uncomplicated acute bronchitis. The primary role of chest radiography is to RULE OUT acute pneumonia when clinical suspicion is high.
  - Indications for Chest Radiography in Patients with Acute Cough:
    1. Abnormal vital signs: Fever (Temp >37.8 degrees C / 100.0 degrees F), Tachycardia (HR >100 beats/min), or Tachypnea (RR >24 breaths/min).
    2. Physical examination signs of focal pulmonary consolidation (dullness to percussion, bronchial breath sounds, egophony, rales).
    3. Advanced age (>75 years) or severe underlying cardiopulmonary disease (COPD, heart failure) presenting with acute respiratory symptoms.
    4. Immunocompromised status (HIV, chemotherapy, high-dose steroids).
    - Radiographic Finding in Acute Bronchitis: Normal lung parenchyma or mild peribronchial thickening WITHOUT pulmonary infiltrates or consolidation (only ~7% of patients clinically diagnosed with acute bronchitis demonstrate radiographic pneumonia).
  - Chest Computed Tomography (CT): Not used for acute bronchitis. In COPD, high-resolution CT demonstrates bronchial wall thickening (CD8+ cell infiltration) and emphysematous parenchymal destruction.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Routine Sputum Gram Stain and Culture: NOT recommended for uncomplicated acute bronchitis or routine AECOPD. Sputum cultures frequently grow normal oropharyngeal flora or colonizers (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis) which does not correlate with clinical response to antibiotics. Sputum culture IS indicated in AECOPD when prior antibiotic therapy has failed or in patients at high risk for Pseudomonas aeruginosa (>=4 antibiotic courses in past year, recent hospital stay within 90 days, prior P. aeruginosa culture, FEV1 <50%).
  - Molecular Testing (Multiplex RT-PCR Panels): Multiplex automated respiratory PCR panels (e.g., BioFire FilmArray, GenMark ePlex) from nasopharyngeal swabs rapidly detect viral nucleic acids (Influenza A/B, RSV, hMPV, Parainfluenza, Coronaviruses, Rhinovirus/Enterovirus, Adenovirus, Bocavirus) and atypical bacteria (Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Bordetella pertussis) within 1 hour.
  - Specific Pertussis & Atypical Diagnostics: B. pertussis confirmed via nasopharyngeal swab RT-PCR, culture (Regan-Lowe or Bordet-Gengou media), or serology. M. pneumoniae confirmed via RT-PCR or specific serum IgM/IgG. C. pneumoniae confirmed via RT-PCR or serology (MIF assay).
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - Clinical Diagnosis of Exclusion based on History and Physical Examination (Acute cough in the absence of clinical or radiographic evidence of acute pneumonia): Represents the gold standard for acute bronchitis. For etiologic pathogen identification, Multiplex Reverse Transcriptase-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) of nasopharyngeal/respiratory secretions is the gold standard.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Uncomplicated Acute Bronchitis (Self-Limited Viral Illness): Reassurance, patient education regarding expected 2- to 3-week cough duration, and symptomatic care. Empirical antibacterial therapy is UNNECESSARY and CONTRAINDICATED in routine cases (guidelines from ACP, CDC, IDSA).
  - Symptomatic Pharmacotherapy:
    - Analgesics / Antipyretics: Acetaminophen or NSAIDs (Ibuprofen 400 mg PO) for fever, malaise, myalgias, and chest wall soreness.
    - Antitussives & Expectorants: Over-the-counter cough suppressants (Dextromethorphan, Codeine) or expectorants (Guaifenesin) offer modest or inconsistent benefit; combination of oral Ibuprofen (400 mg) plus Chlorpheniramine (12 mg) q12h reduces cough in viral illness.
    - Inhaled Bronchodilators: Inhaled short-acting beta-2 agonists (Albuterol MDI 2 puffs q4-6h prn) or anticholinergics (Ipratropium bromide) are recommended ONLY if objective evidence of bronchial hyperreactivity, bronchospasm, or wheezing is present.
  - Non-Antimicrobial Management of AECOPD: Increased short-acting bronchodilator frequency (Albuterol plus Ipratropium MDI or nebulizer). Systemic corticosteroids (Oral Prednisolone 30 to 40 mg PO daily for 5 to 7 days, or 10-14 days) reduce recovery time, improve FEV1, improve oxygenation, and reduce early relapse rate. Supplemental oxygen to target SpO2 88%-92% (preventing hypercapnia). Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIV/BiPAP) for severe hypercapnic respiratory acidosis (pH <7.35, PaCO2 >45 mmHg).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Acute Uncomplicated Bronchitis: Routine antibiotics are NOT RECOMMENDED regardless of cough duration or sputum purulence (since purulent sputum reflects sloughed epithelial cells and PMNs, not necessarily bacterial infection).
  - Indications & Regimens for Specific Pathogens in Acute Bronchitis:
    - Bordetella pertussis (Early Treatment within 3 weeks of onset): Macrolide therapy to reduce transmission: Oral Azithromycin (500 mg PO on day 1, then 250 mg PO daily on days 2-5) OR Oral Clarithromycin (500 mg PO twice daily for 7 days) OR Oral Doxycycline (100 mg PO twice daily for 7-10 days).
    - Mycoplasma pneumoniae / Chlamydia pneumoniae (When documented or during outbreaks): Oral Azithromycin (500 mg day 1, 250 mg days 2-5) OR Oral Doxycycline (100 mg PO twice daily for 7-14 days) OR Oral Moxifloxacin (400 mg PO daily for 7-14 days).
    - Influenza Virus (Early within 48 hours of symptom onset): Neuraminidase inhibitors: Oral Oseltamivir (75 mg PO twice daily for 5 days) or Inhaled Zanamivir or Oral Baloxavir marboxil.
  - Acute Exacerbations of Chronic Bronchitis / AECOPD (GOLD / ACP Guidelines for Antibiotics):
    - Indications for Antibiotics in AECOPD: Antibiotics ARE indicated if patient meets GOLD criteria: (1) All 3 cardinal symptoms (increased dyspnea, increased sputum volume, increased sputum purulence); OR (2) 2 cardinal symptoms if increased sputum purulence is one of the two; OR (3) Severe exacerbation requiring mechanical ventilation (invasive or NIV).
    - First-Line Oral Regimens for Outpatient AECOPD (5 to 7 days duration):
      - Oral Amoxicillin-Clavulanate (875/125 mg PO twice daily or 2000/125 mg PO twice daily); OR
      - Second-generation Oral Cephalosporins: Cefpodoxime (200 mg PO twice daily), Cefuroxime axetil (500 mg PO twice daily), or Cefdinir (300 mg PO twice daily); OR
      - Oral Doxycycline (100 mg PO twice daily for 5-7 days); OR
      - Oral Macrolide: Azithromycin (500 mg day 1, then 250 mg days 2-5) or Clarithromycin (500 mg PO twice daily); OR
      - Oral Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (1-2 DS tablets PO twice daily).
    - Regimens for Complicated AECOPD / Inpatient / Risk Factors (Age >65, FEV1 <50%, >=3 exacerbations/year, cardiac disease):
      - Oral Respiratory Fluoroquinolone: Levofloxacin (750 mg PO/IV daily) or Moxifloxacin (400 mg PO/IV daily) or Amoxicillin-clavulanate.
    - Inpatient AECOPD with Pseudomonas aeruginosa Risk Factors (>=4 antibiotic courses in past year, recent hospital stay within 90 days, prior P. aeruginosa isolation, FEV1 <50%):
      - Antipseudomonal IV Beta-lactam: Piperacillin-tazobactam (3.375-4.5 g IV q6h), Cefepime (2 g IV q8h), Ceftazidime (2 g IV q8h), or Levofloxacin (750 mg IV/PO daily).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Penicillin-Allergic Patients with AECOPD: Oral Doxycycline (100 mg PO twice daily), Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, Azithromycin, or Levofloxacin / Moxifloxacin.
  - Prophylactic Antibiotic Therapy for Frequent AECOPD: Chronic maintenance oral Azithromycin (250 mg PO daily or 500 mg PO 3 times weekly for 1 year) in advanced COPD patients with frequent exacerbations despite optimal inhaler therapy. Reduces AECOPD rate from 1.82 to 1.48 per patient-year and extends time to first exacerbation from 174 to 266 days (requires baseline ECG to exclude prolonged QTc interval and monitoring for hearing loss/macrolide resistance). Pulsed moxifloxacin is an alternative.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Bronchoscopic Debridement & Papilloma Excision: Rigid or flexible bronchoscopy with CO2 laser ablation, microdebrider resection, or intralesional cidofovir for airway-obstructing juvenile/adult laryngeal and tracheobronchial papillomatosis (HPV types 6 and 11).
  - Endotracheal Intubation & Mechanical Ventilation: Indicated in severe AECOPD with acute life-threatening hypercapnic respiratory failure, severe acidosis (pH <7.25), somnolence, or failure of NIV.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Secondary Bacterial Pneumonia: Post-viral bacterial pneumonia (superinfection by S. pneumoniae, S. aureus, or H. influenzae following influenza or parainfluenza bronchitis).
  - Acute Respiratory Failure & Acidosis: Acute hypercapnic hypoxemia respiratory failure in AECOPD requiring ICU care or mechanical ventilation.
  - Exacerbation of Asthma: Viral bronchitis (especially rhinovirus, RSV, hMPV) triggers acute severe asthma exacerbations and status asthmaticus.
  - Pneumothorax & Pneumomediastinum: Alveolar rupture secondary to severe paroxysmal coughing or air-trapping in AECOPD.
  - Post-tussive Rib Fractures & Syncope: Mechanical trauma from violent, repetitive coughing fits (especially in pertussis or M. pneumoniae).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Post-infectious Cough & Persistent Airway Hyperreactivity: Bronchial hyperresponsiveness and dry cough persisting for 3 to 8 weeks following acute viral bronchitis, RSV, or influenza.
  - Accelerated FEV1 Decline: Repeated AECOPD episodes drive progressive bronchial wall thickening, loss of lung function, and accelerated permanent FEV1 decline.
  - Post-infectious Bronchiectasis: Irreversible bronchial dilation following necrotizing or severe childhood pertussis, adenovirus, or measles tracheobronchitis.
  - Chronic Cough Syndromes & Cough Hypersensitivity: Persistent cough lasting >8 weeks necessitating evaluation for upper airway cough syndrome (postnasal drip), cough-variant asthma, or GERD.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Walking pneumonia" (common lay descriptor for mild M. pneumoniae or C. pneumoniae atypical bronchitis/pneumonia).
  - "Whooping cough" (classic descriptor for Bordetella pertussis paroxysmal bronchitis).
  - "Currant-jelly sputum" (descriptor for Klebsiella in pneumonia/AECOPD).
  - "Sputum purulence transition" (color change from clear to green/yellow serving as the best guide for prescribing antibiotics in AECOPD).
  - "Anthonisen criteria" (3 cardinal symptoms defining AECOPD severity and antibiotic need).
  - "Bullous myringitis" (tympanic membrane vesicles associated with Mycoplasma pneumoniae).
  - "CARDS toxin" (Community-Acquired Respiratory Distress Syndrome toxin elaborated by Mycoplasma pneumoniae).
  - "Faget sign / Relative bradycardia" (fever with inappropriately low heart rate in Legionella, Chlamydia psittaci, or Q fever).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - The "Sputum Purulence Does Not Equal Bacteria in Acute Bronchitis" Rule: In uncomplicated acute bronchitis in healthy hosts, green or yellow purulent sputum is caused by sloughed respiratory epithelial cells and neutrophil peroxidase enzyme, NOT bacterial superinfection; purulent sputum is NOT an indication for antibiotic therapy in uncomplicated acute bronchitis.
  - The "Purulent Sputum DOES Equal Bacteria in AECOPD" Exception: In sharp contrast to acute bronchitis, transition to purulent sputum in AECOPD IS a highly reliable indicator of bacterial infection (high neutrophil/bacterial load) and serves as the single strongest clinical guide for prescribing antibiotic therapy.
  - Inefficacy of Antibiotics in Uncomplicated Acute Bronchitis Meta-Analyses: Multiple Cochrane meta-analyses of 17+ randomized controlled trials demonstrate that routine antibiotic therapy for acute bronchitis provides NO significant clinical benefit or symptom reduction, yet acute bronchitis remains one of the top 5 causes of outpatient antibiotic overprescribing (~60%-80% of patients receive unnecessary antibiotics).
  - Absence of Infiltrate Distinguishes Bronchitis from Pneumonia: The fundamental diagnostic distinction between acute bronchitis and acute pneumonia is the ABSENCE of a new pulmonary infiltrate on chest radiography and ABSENCE of physical signs of consolidation.
  - Lack of Temperature Elevation Sensitivity in Elderly AECOPD: Older adults with severe AECOPD or acute bronchitis frequently do NOT mount a fever response, presenting solely with subtle lethargy, confusion, or increased dyspnea.
  - Discrepancy Between Normal Flora Sputum Isolation and Pathogenicity: Isolating Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, or Moraxella catarrhalis from expectorated sputum in a healthy patient with acute bronchitis reflects normal upper airway colonization (16S rRNA sequencing shows normal lower airway microbiota), not active bacterial infection.
