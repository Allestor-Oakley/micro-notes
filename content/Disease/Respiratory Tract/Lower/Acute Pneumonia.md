# Acute Pneumonia

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Acute pneumonia is an acute microbial infection of the lung parenchyma characterized by inflammation of the distal respiratory airways, terminal bronchioles, alveoli, and interstitium, resulting in alveolar air space consolidation, impaired gas exchange, and systemic inflammatory manifestations. It is the single most common cause of infection-related mortality worldwide and a leading cause of hospitalization across all age groups.
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Epidemiologic / Setting-Based Classifications:
     - Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP): Acute pneumonia acquired in the community or presenting within 48 hours of hospital admission in patients without recent health care exposure.
     - Health Care-Associated Pneumonia (HCAP): Pneumonia occurring in non-hospitalized patients with extensive health care contact (hospitalization for 2 or more days within 90 days, residence in long-term care or nursing home, home IV therapy, wound care, or chemotherapy within 30 days, or attendance at hemodialysis or hospital clinics).
     - Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia (HAP / Nosocomial Pneumonia): Pneumonia developing 48 hours or more after hospital admission that was not incubating at the time of admission.
     - Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP): Pneumonia developing 48 to 72 hours or more following endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation.
  2. Anatomical & Radiographic Patterns:
     - Lobar Pneumonia: Homogeneous consolidation confined to one or more anatomical lobes, typical of Streptococcus pneumoniae and Legionella.
     - Bronchopneumonia: Patchy, multifocal consolidation centered around bronchioles, typical of Staphylococcus aureus, Haemophilus influenzae, and fungal pathogens.
     - Interstitial / Diffuse Pneumonitis: Reticulonodular or patchy ground-glass opacities involving interlobular septa and interstitium, typical of viral infections, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and Pneumocystis jirovecii.
     - Aspiration Pneumonia / Chemical Pneumonitis: Infection or chemical lung injury resulting from macro-aspiration of oropharyngeal or gastric contents, characteristically involving dependent lung segments (posterior segment of upper lobes, superior and basilar segments of lower lobes).
  3. Clinical Syndromic Categories:
     - Typical Pneumonia: Abrupt onset of high fever, chills, rigors, pleuritic chest pain, productive cough with purulent or rust-colored sputum, and signs of lobar consolidation.
     - Atypical Pneumonia: Subacute onset, prominent dry or intractable cough, headache, malaise, low-grade fever, extrapulmonary manifestations, and radiologic findings out of proportion to mild physical signs.
  4. Severity-Based Stratification:
     - Mild / Outpatient CAP: Low risk score (PSI Class I-II, CURB-65 0-1, CRB-65 0) manageable without hospitalization.
     - Moderate / Inpatient Ward CAP: Intermediate risk (PSI Class III-IV, CURB-65 2) requiring general ward admission.
     - Severe CAP / ICU-Level Pneumonia: Presence of major criteria (septic shock requiring vasopressors, acute respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation) or 3 or more minor criteria (RR >= 30, PaO2/FiO2 <= 250, multilobar infiltrates, confusion, uremia BUN >= 20 mg/dL, leukopenia < 4000/mm3, thrombocytopenia < 100,000/mm3, hypothermia < 36 degrees C, hypotension requiring fluid resuscitation) necessitating ICU admission.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Epidemiology: Acute pneumonia is the most common cause of infection-related death globally and among the top 10 overall causes of death worldwide. The annual incidence of pneumonia requiring hospital admission increases dramatically with age, reaching its highest rate in adults aged 80 years or older (164 per 10,000 adults). Peak incidence of pneumococcal bacteremic pneumonia occurs in midwinter and early spring, whereas Legionella peak incidence occurs in summer and early fall. Cyclic epidemics of Mycoplasma pneumoniae occur every 3 to 5 years.
  2. Patient Comorbidities & Risk Factors: Advanced age (65 years or older), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis, congestive heart failure, diabetes mellitus, chronic renal disease, liver disease, cerebrovascular disease or stroke, neurological impairment (predisposing to dysphagia and aspiration), alcoholism, structural lung disease, and immunosuppression (HIV/AIDS, neutropenia, malignancy, organ transplant, corticosteroids, biologic TNF-alpha inhibitors).
  3. Lifestyle & Environmental Risk Factors: Cigarette smoking (disrupts mucociliary clearance and macrophage function), alcohol abuse (suppresses cough and epiglottic reflexes, impairs monocyte TNF response, increases oropharyngeal gram-negative colonization), overcrowded living conditions (>10 people per household, military dormitories, boarding schools, nursing homes), exposure to young children, poor dentition or periodontal disease (increases inoculum of oral anaerobes), exposure to contaminated water or aerosolized water systems (Legionella), contact with birds (Chlamydia psittaci), contact with farm animals or birth fluids (Coxiella burnetii), and prior influenza or respiratory viral infection (preceding viral infection disrupts epithelial barriers and predisposes to secondary bacterial superinfection).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Typical Bacterial Pathogens: Streptococcus pneumoniae (most common bacterial cause of CAP overall), Haemophilus influenzae (nontypeable and type b), Moraxella catarrhalis, Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA and MRSA; community-associated PVL-producing strains), Aerobic gram-negative bacilli (Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, Enterobacter species, Serratia marcescens, Acinetobacter species).
  - Atypical Bacterial Pathogens: Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila (and Legionella micdadei), Chlamydia psittaci, Coxiella burnetii.
  - Respiratory Viral Pathogens: Influenza A and B viruses, Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), Human Metapneumovirus (hMPV), Parainfluenza viruses (types 1-4), Adenovirus (serotypes 3, 4, 7, 14), Rhinovirus, Coronaviruses (including endemic strains, SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV), Human Bocavirus, Enteroviruses, Parechoviruses.
  - Anaerobic Pathogens (Aspiration Pneumonia): Bacteroides species, Prevotella species (P. melaninogenica), Fusobacterium species, Peptostreptococcus species, Peptococcus species, Porphyromonas species.
  - Fungal Pathogens: Histoplasma capsulatum, Coccidioides immitis, Blastomyces dermatitidis, Cryptococcus neoformans, Aspergillus species, Agents of Mucormycosis (Rhizopus, Mucor, Absidia, Cunninghamella), Pneumocystis jirovecii.
  - Mycobacterial Pathogens: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (MAC, M. kansasii, M. abscessus, M. chelonae, M. fortuitum, M. xenopi).
  - Parasitic Pathogens: Toxoplasma gondii, Strongyloides stercoralis, Ascaris lumbricoides, Paragonimus westermani.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children (Under 5 Years): Viral etiologies predominate (RSV, Human Metapneumovirus, Parainfluenza, Influenza, Adenovirus, Rhinovirus, Bocavirus). Bacterial causes include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pyogenes, Staphylococcus aureus, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (in children over 5 years).
  - Adolescents and Young Adults (5 to 40 Years): Mycoplasma pneumoniae (peak incidence 5-15 years and young adults in dormitories or military), Chlamydia pneumoniae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Influenza viruses, Adenovirus (types 4 and 7 in military recruits).
  - Adults (40 to 65 Years): Streptococcus pneumoniae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila, Influenza A and B, Respiratory syncytial virus, Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus.
  - Older Adults (65 Years or Older & Nursing Home / HCAP): Streptococcus pneumoniae, nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, Aerobic gram-negative bacilli (Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa), Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA and MRSA), polymicrobial oral anaerobes (aspiration pneumonia), Respiratory syncytial virus, Influenza viruses, Human Metapneumovirus.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Immunocompromised / Transplant / HIV Hosts: Pneumocystis jirovecii (PCP), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Aspergillus species, Cryptococcus neoformans, Mucormycosis agents, Nocardia species, Actinomyces species, Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Burkholderia cepacia, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Legionella micdadei, Toxoplasma gondii, Strongyloides stercoralis hyperinfection.
  - Zoonotic & Occupational Atypical Agents: Chlamydia psittaci (psittacosis from pet birds or parrots), Coxiella burnetii (Q fever from sheep, cattle, or goats birth fluids), Francisella tularensis (tularemia from rabbits or ticks), Bacillus anthracis (inhalation anthrax), Yersinia pestis (pneumonic plague), Burkholderia pseudomallei (melioidosis in tropical regions), Hantavirus (Hantavirus pulmonary syndrome from deer mice urine or feces), Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (MERS-CoV from camels).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  1. Entry Routes into Lower Respiratory Tract:
     - Aspiration of Oropharyngeal Resident Flora: Primary mechanism for most bacterial pneumonias. Small-volume microaspiration occurs silently during sleep in healthy individuals, but macroaspiration occurs when cough and epiglottic reflexes are impaired (altered consciousness, alcohol, sedation, stroke, seizure, neuromuscular disease, endotracheal intubation).
     - Inhalation of Aerosolized Droplets: Direct inhalation of airborne infectious particles (Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila from water aerosols, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Influenza, RSV, fungal spores).
     - Hematogenous / Metastatic Seeding: Inoculation of the lung via bloodstream seeding from an extrapulmonary focus (right-sided infective endocarditis, tricuspid S. aureus, infected central venous catheters, Lemierre syndrome, septic pelvic thrombophlebitis).
     - Direct Contiguous Spread: Extension from an adjacent infected focus (pleural empyema, subdiaphragmatic abscess, mediastinitis).
  2. Impairment of Pulmonary Host Defenses:
     - Disruption of Upper and Conducting Airway Defenses: Anatomical barriers (nasal turbinates, epiglottic closure), mucociliary escalator disruption (smoking, toxic gases, viral infection destroying ciliated epithelium), and altered surface liquid antimicrobial proteins (lysozyme, lactoferrin, defensins, surfactant proteins A and D).
     - Cellular & Immune Defect Mechanisms: Alcohol and narcotics suppress cough and epiglottic reflexes and inhibit neutrophil chemotaxis and oxidative burst. Viral infections (influenza, parainfluenza) destroy respiratory epithelium, impair ciliary beat frequency, decrease alveolar macrophage phagocytosis, and induce transient immunosuppression (increasing bacterial adherence via up-regulated PAF receptors). HIV depletes memory and naive CD4+ T cells, impairs alveolar macrophage function, and disrupts bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (BALT).
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  1. Pattern Recognition & Cytokine Cascade: Pathogens express pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) recognized by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs, e.g., Toll-like receptors TLR2, TLR4) on alveolar macrophages, dendritic cells, and respiratory epithelial cells. Activation of transcription factors (NF-kappaB, MAP kinase) leads to production of early response proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-1 beta, IL-6) and chemokines (IL-8), recruiting polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) from pulmonary capillaries into alveolar air spaces.
  2. Four Classic Pathologic Stages of Lobar Pneumonia (Pneumococcal Prototype):
     - Congestion (First 24 Hours): Vascular engorgement, serous intra-alveolar exudate with abundant bacteria and sparse neutrophils.
     - Red Hepatization (Days 2 to 4): Massive extravasation of neutrophils, red blood cells, and fibrin filling alveolar spaces, giving the lung a liver-like firm, airless appearance.
     - Gray Hepatization (Days 5 to 7): Disintegration of red blood cells, persistent fibrinopurulent exudate with abundant neutrophils, fibrin deposition, and clearance of bacteria.
     - Resolution (Day 8 Onward): Enzymatic digestion of fibrinous exudate by macrophage proteases, resorption of fluid, clearing of cellular debris by macrophages, and restoration of alveolar architecture.
  3. Tissue Destruction & Gas Exchange Impairment: In necrotizing pneumonias (S. aureus with Panton-Valentine leukocidin / PVL, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella, anaerobes), bacterial cytotoxins and PMN elastases/collagenases destroy alveolar walls and pulmonary capillaries, causing tissue necrosis, abscess formation, pneumatoceles, and pulmonary hemorrhage. Intra-alveolar exudate and ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch lead to intrapulmonary right-to-left shunting, arterial hypoxemia, decreased lung compliance, and potential progression to Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) or septic shock.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Respiratory Symptoms: Cough (the sine qua non of pneumonia; present in >80%-90% of cases, productive of purulent or rust-colored sputum in >60%), dyspnea / shortness of breath, pleuritic chest pain (present in 35%-48%, sharp pain exacerbated by deep inspiration or coughing), hemoptysis (present in ~15%, rust-colored in S. pneumoniae, currant-jelly in K. pneumoniae, frank blood in necrotizing S. aureus), and hoarseness.
  - Constitutional & Systemic Symptoms: Fever (present in 65%-90%, often with sudden onset of rigors or shivering), sweats, fatigue/malaise, headache, myalgias, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea (frequently reported in Legionella, Mycoplasma, and viral pneumonias).
  - Variations by Host Age & Setting: Older adults and immunocompromised patients frequently present with subtle, atypical, or non-respiratory symptoms, including confusion / altered mental status, lethargy, anorexia, hypothermia, or falls, often lacking fever or cough.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Vital Sign Abnormalities: Fever (>37.8 degrees C or >38.0 degrees C; or hypothermia <36.0 degrees C in severe sepsis or elderly), tachycardia (heart rate >100 beats/min), tachypnea (respiratory rate >20-30 breaths/min; one of the most sensitive physical signs), arterial hypotension (systolic BP <90 mmHg or diastolic BP <=60 mmHg), and decreased pulse oximetry oxygen saturation (SpO2 <90%-92% on room air).
  - Thoracic & Lung Examination Findings:
    - Inspection: Respiratory distress, intercostal retraction, use of accessory muscles, nasal flaring, "splinting" or inspiratory lag on the affected side.
    - Palpation: Increased tactile fremitus over consolidated lung tissue; decreased fremitus over pleural effusion.
    - Percussion: Dullness to percussion over areas of lobar consolidation or pleural effusion.
    - Auscultation: Fine or coarse crackles (rales) over affected lung zones, bronchial breath sounds (high-pitched tubular breath sounds over consolidated lung), egophony (E-to-A changes on vocalization), whispered pectoriloquy, bronchophony, and pleural friction rub (if pleuritis is present).
  - General Physical Signs: Cyanosis (in severe hypoxemia), altered sensorium / disorientation, poor dentition / periodontal disease (in anaerobic aspiration pneumonia), digital clubbing (in chronic lung abscess or underlying bronchiectasis), and jaundice (occasionally seen in severe pneumococcal or Legionella bacteremia).
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Rust-colored sputum accompanied by sudden onset of a single shaking rigor, high fever, and lobar consolidation (classic pathognomonic presentation of bacteremic Streptococcus pneumoniae pneumonia).
  - Thick, sticky "currant-jelly" sputum combined with bulging interlobar fissure on chest radiograph in an alcoholic patient (classic sign of Klebsiella pneumoniae pneumonia).
  - Intractable, persistent, debilitating dry cough out of proportion to physical signs in a young adult, associated with bullous myringitis and elevated cold agglutinins (classic presentation of Mycoplasma pneumoniae).
  - High fever (>40 degrees C) accompanied by bradycardia (relative bradycardia / Faget sign), diarrhea, hyponatremia, and confusion (classic clinical constellation suggestive of Legionella pneumophila pneumonia).
  - Foul-smelling / putrid sputum in a patient with altered consciousness, poor dentition, and cavitary lower-lobe consolidation (pathognomonic sign of anaerobic aspiration pneumonia or lung abscess).

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis (WBC 15,000 to 35,000/mm3) with neutrophilia and increased immature band forms (left shift). Leukopenia (WBC <4,000/mm3) or severe thrombocytopenia (<100,000/mm3) represents a poor prognostic sign associated with severe sepsis and higher mortality.
  - Inflammatory Biomarkers:
    - Serum Procalcitonin (PCT): Calcitonin gene product elevated in bacterial infections (>0.25-0.5 mcg/L supports bacterial pneumonia and guides antibiotic initiation/stewardship duration; levels <0.1-0.25 mcg/L suggest viral etiology or absence of bacterial infection).
    - C-Reactive Protein (CRP): Acute-phase reactant; levels <10 mg/L help exclude pneumonia, whereas levels >100 mg/L support pneumonia diagnosis and predict 30-day mortality/ICU admission risk.
  - Comprehensive Metabolic & Organ Function Panel: Serum electrolytes (hyponatremia <130 mEq/L common in Legionella and severe CAP due to SIADH), blood urea nitrogen (BUN >=20 mg/dL / urea >7 mmol/L used in CURB-65 scoring), serum creatinine (detecting acute kidney injury), liver transaminases and bilirubin (elevated in Legionella, severe sepsis, or heart failure), serum lactate (elevated >2 mmol/L indicating hypoperfusion), and arterial blood gas (ABG) assessing PaO2/FiO2 ratio (<=250 mmHg indicating severe lung injury/ARDS) and acid-base status.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Standard Chest Radiography (PA and Lateral): The fundamental gold standard for establishing the clinical diagnosis of acute pneumonia. Required in all patients suspected of pneumonia to confirm presence of new pulmonary infiltrates, differentiate pneumonia from acute bronchitis, assess severity, and detect complications (pleural effusion, cavitation, pneumatoceles).
    - Radiographic Infiltrate Patterns:
      - Lobar Consolidation: Homogeneous dense air-space opacification with air bronchograms (S. pneumoniae, K. pneumoniae, Legionella).
      - Bronchopneumonia: Patchy, multifocal peribronchial opacities (S. aureus, H. influenzae, Pseudomonas, fungi).
      - Interstitial / Ground-Glass Infiltrates: Diffuse bilateral reticulonodular or patchy ground-glass opacities (Mycoplasma, C. pneumoniae, viral pneumonias, P. jirovecii / PCP showing "crazy-paving" pattern on CT).
      - Cavitary Lesions & Pneumatoceles: Thin- or thick-walled cavities with air-fluid levels (S. aureus with PVL, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, anaerobes, TB, fungal).
      - Dependent Segment Consolidation: Posterior segment of upper lobes or superior and basilar segments of lower lobes (aspiration pneumonia).
  - Computed Tomography (CT) of the Chest: Higher sensitivity than chest radiograph for detecting early or subtle infiltrates, occult cavitation, empyema, loculated pleural fluid, mediastinal adenopathy, or underlying obstructing mass or foreign body. Indicated in severe, non-resolving, or diagnostic dilemma cases.
  - Thoracic Ultrasound: Bedside point-of-care ultrasound detecting subpleural consolidation, B-lines, and characterization of pleural effusions (detecting septations/loculations in complicated parapneumonic effusions).
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Sputum Gram Stain and Culture: Expectorated sputum evaluated for acceptability (Geckler criteria: >25 neutrophils and <10 squamous epithelial cells per low-power field). Gram stain provides rapid initial identification (Gram-positive lancet-shaped diplococci for S. pneumoniae; Gram-positive cocci in clusters for S. aureus; small Gram-negative coccobacilli for H. influenzae; Gram-negative rods for Enterobacteriaceae/Pseudomonas). Quantitative or semi-quantitative sputum culture isolates pathogen and provides antimicrobial susceptibility testing.
  - Blood Cultures: Two sets of aerobic/anaerobic blood cultures obtained prior to antibiotic therapy. Positive in 4% to 17% of hospitalized CAP cases (higher in severe CAP/ICU). Recommended for all patients hospitalized with severe CAP, ICU admission, HCAP/HAP, cavitary lesions, leukopenia, active alcohol abuse, asplenia, positive pneumococcal urinary antigen, or pleural effusion.
  - Urinary Antigen Detection Assays:
    - Streptococcus pneumoniae Urinary Antigen Test: Rapid immunochromatographic assay detecting cell wall C-polysaccharide. Sensitivity 60%-80%, specificity >90%. Unaffected by prior antibiotic administration.
    - Legionella pneumophila Urinary Antigen Test: Detects L. pneumophila serogroup 1 antigen (causes >84%-90% of Legionella cases). Sensitivity 70%-90%, specificity >99%.
  - Molecular Diagnostic Testing (PCR & Multiplex NAAT Panels): Multiplex respiratory PCR panels (e.g., BioFire FilmArray, ePlex) directly detect viral pathogens (Influenza, RSV, hMPV, Parainfluenza, Adenovirus, Rhinovirus, Coronaviruses) and atypical/typical bacterial DNA (M. pneumoniae, C. pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila, S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, S. aureus, MRSA, P. aeruginosa) from nasopharyngeal swabs or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid within 1 to 2 hours. MRSA nasal PCR screening swab has high negative predictive value (96%-99%) for ruling out MRSA pneumonia.
  - Invasive Respiratory Sampling: Fiberoptic bronchoscopy with Bronchoalveolar Lavage (BAL) or Protected Specimen Brush (PSB), endotracheal aspiration (in intubated VAP), percutaneous needle lung aspiration, or video-assisted thoracoscopic / open lung biopsy (reserved for non-responding, severe, or immunocompromised hosts).
  - Pleural Fluid Analysis: Diagnostic thoracentesis required for all significant pleural effusions. Fluid analyzed for Gram stain, AFB stain, bacterial/fungal/TB culture, cell count/differential, pH (pH < 7.2 indicates complicated parapneumonic effusion requiring chest tube drainage), LDH (>1000 U/L), glucose (<60 mg/dL), and pleural pneumococcal/TB antigen/ADA.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - Demonstration of a New Pulmonary Infiltrate on Chest Radiography (or Chest Computed Tomography) in a Patient with Consistent Clinical Symptoms and Physical Findings: Represents the definitive gold standard for establishing a clinical diagnosis of acute pneumonia. Microbiological isolation/PCR identification from blood, pleural fluid, BAL, or acceptable sputum provides the gold standard for etiologic pathogen confirmation.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Site-of-Care Triage & Clinical Decision Rules: Stratify patients using validated scoring systems:
    - Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI / PORT Score): Evaluates 20 variables (demographics, comorbidities, physical exam, lab/radiology). Classes I-II (treated outpatient), Class III (observation or short inpatient stay), Classes IV-V (inpatient ward or ICU).
    - CURB-65 Score: Assigns 1 point each for Confusion, Urea > 7 mmol/L (BUN >= 20 mg/dL), Respiratory rate >= 30 breaths/min, Blood pressure (systolic < 90 mmHg or diastolic <= 60 mmHg), and Age >= 65 years. Score 0-1 (outpatient), Score 2 (inpatient ward), Score 3-5 (inpatient ward or ICU).
    - CRB-65 Score: Laboratory-free bedside tool (Confusion, Respiratory rate >= 30, BP < 90/60, Age >= 65). Score 0 (outpatient), Score 1-2 (referral or inpatient evaluation), Score 3-4 (urgent hospital admission).
  - Oxygenation & Ventilatory Support: Supplemental oxygen to maintain SpO2 >= 92% (or 88%-92% in chronic hypercapnic COPD). High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) or non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIV/BiPAP) for acute hypoxemic respiratory failure. Invasive mechanical ventilation with lung-protective lower tidal volume strategy (6 mL/kg predicted body weight) for severe ARDS or refractory respiratory failure.
  - Hemodynamic Resuscitation & Sepsis Care: Early goal-directed crystalloid fluid resuscitation (30 mL/kg IV within 3 hours) for septic shock or lactate > 4 mmol/L, followed by vasopressors (Norepinephrine target MAP >= 65 mmHg) for persistent hypotension.
  - General Supportive Measures: Intravenous hydration, chest physiotherapy, early mobilization, deep vein thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis, stress ulcer prophylaxis, and fever management.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Outpatient CAP - Previously Healthy (No Recent Antibiotics within 3 Months, No Comorbidities):
    - Advanced Macrolide: Azithromycin (500 mg PO on day 1, then 250 mg PO daily on days 2-5) OR Clarithromycin (500 mg PO twice daily); OR
    - Doxycycline (100 mg PO twice daily for 7-10 days); OR
    - High-Dose Amoxicillin (1 g PO 3 times daily) in areas with low macrolide resistance.
  - Outpatient CAP - Comorbidities (COPD, Diabetes, Renal/Heart Failure, Malignancy, Alcoholism) OR Recent Antibiotic Use:
    - Respiratory Fluoroquinolone Monotherapy: Levofloxacin (750 mg PO daily) OR Moxifloxacin (400 mg PO daily) for 5-7 days; OR
    - Combination Therapy: Advanced Macrolide (Azithromycin or Clarithromycin) OR Doxycycline (100 mg PO twice daily) COMBINED WITH an Oral Beta-Lactam: High-dose Amoxicillin-Clavulanate (2000/125 mg PO twice daily or 875/125 mg PO twice daily), Cefpodoxime (200 mg PO twice daily), or Cefuroxime axetil (500 mg PO twice daily).
  - Inpatient CAP - General Medical Ward (Non-ICU):
    - Respiratory Fluoroquinolone Monotherapy: Levofloxacin (750 mg IV/PO daily) OR Moxifloxacin (400 mg IV/PO daily); OR
    - Combination Therapy: Intravenous Beta-Lactam (Ceftriaxone 1-2 g IV daily, Cefotaxime 1-2 g IV q8h, Ampicillin-sulbactam 1.5-3 g IV q6h, or Ertapenem 1 g IV daily) COMBINED WITH an Advanced Macrolide (Azithromycin 500 mg IV/PO daily) OR Doxycycline (100 mg IV/PO twice daily).
  - Inpatient CAP - Intensive Care Unit (Severe CAP / ICU):
    - Combination Therapy (Pseudomonas NOT Suspected): Intravenous Beta-Lactam (Ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily, Cefotaxime 2 g IV q8h, or Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h) COMBINED WITH EITHER Azithromycin (500 mg IV daily) OR a Respiratory Fluoroquinolone (Levofloxacin 750 mg IV daily or Moxifloxacin 400 mg IV daily).
  - Empirical Regimens for Special Risk Factors:
    - Pseudomonas aeruginosa Suspected (Cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis, severe COPD with frequent steroids/antibiotics, recent hospitalization): Antipseudomonal Beta-Lactam (Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h, Cefepime 2 g IV q8h, Meropenem 1 g IV q8h, or Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h) COMBINED WITH Levofloxacin (750 mg IV daily) or Ciprofloxacin (400 mg IV q8h), OR combined with an Aminoglycoside (Tobramycin/Amikacin) plus Azithromycin.
    - MRSA / CA-MRSA Suspected (Severe cavitary necrotizing pneumonia, post-influenza superinfection, hemoptysis, recent HCAP/HAP): Add Vancomycin (15-20 mg/kg IV q8-12h, targeting trough 15-20 mcg/mL) OR Linezolid (600 mg IV/PO twice daily). (Linezolid inhibits PVL toxin synthesis).
    - Aspiration Pneumonia / Lung Abscess Suspected: Amoxicillin-clavulanate (PO/IV), Ampicillin-sulbactam (1.5-3 g IV q6h), Clindamycin (600 mg IV q8h or 300 mg PO q6h), or Moxifloxacin.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Beta-Lactam Allergy Alternatives: Respiratory Fluoroquinolone (Levofloxacin or Moxifloxacin) monotherapy or Azithromycin/Doxycycline combined with Aztreonam (2 g IV q8h) for gram-negative coverage.
  - Pediatric Empirical Regimens (Infants & Children >3 Months):
    - Outpatient (Presumed Bacterial): High-dose Amoxicillin (90 mg/kg/day divided twice daily) or Amoxicillin-clavulanate.
    - Outpatient (Presumed Atypical): Azithromycin (10 mg/kg on day 1, 5 mg/kg days 2-5), Clarithromycin, or Doxycycline (children >7 years).
    - Inpatient Fully Immunized: Ampicillin (50 mg/kg IV q6h) or Penicillin G; or Ceftriaxone (50-100 mg/kg/day IV) / Cefotaxime if unimmunized or high pneumococcal resistance. Add Vancomycin/Clindamycin if CA-MRSA suspected.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Tube Thoracostomy / Intercostal Chest Drain Insertion: Mandatory insertion of a chest tube for complicated parapneumonic effusions (pleural fluid pH < 7.2, glucose < 60 mg/dL, positive Gram stain/culture) or frank purulent empyema to evacuate fluid and re-expand the lung.
  - Intrapleural Fibrinolytic / Enzyme Therapy: Intrapleural administration of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA / Alteplase) combined with recombinant human DNase (Dornase alfa) via chest tube to break down loculated fibrinous septations in empyema.
  - Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery (VATS) or Decortication: Surgical debridement, breakdown of loculations, and pleural decortication for loculated, organized empyema or failed tube thoracostomy drainage.
  - Percutaneous Catheter Drainage or Surgical Resection: Image-guided percutaneous pigtail drainage of large, non-draining lung abscesses. Surgical lobectomy or pneumonectomy reserved for massive hemoptysis, pulmonary gangrene, unresponsive destructive necrotizing lung abscess, or obstructing endobronchial malignancy.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Pleural Complications: Parapneumonic pleural effusion (occurs in >40% of hospitalized CAP), complicated parapneumonic effusion, thoracic empyema (pus in pleural space), and pneumothorax / pyopneumothorax.
  - Parenchymal Destruction & Suppuration: Lung abscess formation (localized cavitary tissue necrosis), necrotizing pneumonia, pneumatoceles (thin-walled air-filled cysts, common in staphylococcal and gram-negative pneumonias), and pulmonary gangrene.
  - Respiratory Failure & Pulmonary Vascular Damage: Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), severe hypoxemic respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, severe hemoptysis (due to bronchial artery erosion in necrotizing pneumonia), and acute exacerbation of underlying COPD or heart failure.
  - Systemic & Cardiovascular Complications: Bacteremia, septic shock, Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC), acute myocardial infarction (increased cardiac event risk during acute CAP), acute heart failure, cardiac arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation), acute kidney injury, multiorgan dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and metastatic suppuration (pericarditis, endocarditis, brain abscess, septic arthritis, meningitis).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Chronic Pulmonary Dysfunction: Persistent impaired diffusion capacity, restrictive or obstructive lung defects, chronic post-infectious bronchiectasis, and localized pulmonary fibrosis / scarring.
  - Post-Pneumonia Functional Decline & Mortality: Increased long-term all-cause and cardiovascular mortality persisting for years after episode resolution, physical deconditioning, cognitive impairment, and high 30-day rehospitalization rates.
  - Fibrothorax & Pleural Thickening: Pleural entrapment and restrictive ventilatory defect following organized, un-drained empyema.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Captain of the Men of Death" (historical descriptor for pneumonia coined by Sir William Osler in 1901).
  - "Headlight in the fog" (referring to dense focal vitritis, but in lung radiology: focal consolidation visible through dense hazy ground-glass opacities).
  - "Currant-jelly sputum" (classic descriptor for thick blood-tinged mucoid sputum in Klebsiella pneumoniae pneumonia).
  - "Rust-colored sputum" (classic descriptor for pneumococcal sputum filled with altered red blood cells and PMNs).
  - "Bulging interlobar fissure sign" (radiographic sign on chest X-ray characteristic of Klebsiella pneumoniae lobar consolidation).
  - "Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL)" (pore-forming cytotoxin produced by community-associated MRSA strains causing severe necrotizing pneumonia).
  - "Crazy-paving pattern" (CT finding of ground-glass opacities with interlobular septal thickening seen in Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia).
  - "CARDS toxin" (Community-Acquired Respiratory Distress Syndrome toxin elaborated by Mycoplasma pneumoniae causing epithelial cell damage).
  - "Cold agglutinins" (anti-I IgM antibodies causing red blood cell agglutination at 4 degrees C in Mycoplasma pneumoniae).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Absence of Infiltrates on Admission Chest Radiograph: Up to 20% of patients hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia (especially dehydrated patients, neutropenic hosts, or early presentation) have a normal admission chest radiograph, but develop a visible pulmonary infiltrate within 24 to 48 hours following fluid rehydration.
  - Absence of Fever and Respiratory Symptoms in Elderly Patients: Older adults with severe acute pneumonia frequently do NOT present with fever, cough, or purulent sputum; instead, they present solely with apathy, confusion, lethargy, hypothermia, or acute worsening of underlying baseline disease.
  - Dissociation Between Radiographic Findings and Clinical Exam in Atypical Pneumonia: Patients with Mycoplasma pneumoniae or Chlamydia pneumoniae pneumonia routinely exhibit striking, extensive bilateral pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiography despite minimal or near-normal physical examination findings on chest auscultation ("walking pneumonia").
  - Pitfall of Sputum Normal Flora Output After Single Antibiotic Dose: Sputum cultures obtained after even a single dose of antibiotic therapy rapidly lose diagnostic yield for fastidious organisms like Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae, overgrowing with resistant normal microbiota or gram-negative bacilli.
  - False-Positive Pneumococcal Urinary Antigen in Children: The pneumococcal urinary antigen assay produces false-positive results in children under 5 years of age due to high rates of nasopharyngeal pneumococcal colonization rather than active lower respiratory infection.
  - Inefficacy of Anaerobic Coverage in Standard Uncomplicated AECOPD / Mild Aspiration: Although oral anaerobes are classic pathogens in indolent cavitary lung abscesses, routine uncomplicated community-acquired aspiration or AECOPD does not require dedicated anaerobic coverage (such as clindamycin) unless necrotizing abscess, putrid sputum, or empyema is present.
  - Steroid Benefit in Severe CAP vs. Avoidance in Viral Pneumonia: Systemic corticosteroids reduce mortality and organ failure in severe bacterial CAP with septic shock or high inflammation (CRP >150 mg/L), but increase viral replication and mortality in uncomplicated influenza pneumonia.
