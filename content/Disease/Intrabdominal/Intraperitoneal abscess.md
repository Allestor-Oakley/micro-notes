# Intraperitoneal Abscess

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Intraperitoneal abscesses are focal, encapsulated collections of purulent exudate (pus) and necrotic tissue sequestered within the peritoneal cavity or its potential spaces and recesses. They develop as a localized complication of primary or secondary peritonitis, organ perforation, penetrating abdominal trauma, or surgical procedures.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - By Pathogenesis: (1) Secondary Intraperitoneal Abscesses (most common, resulting from localized secondary peritonitis, diseased abdominal viscera, trauma, or surgical operations); (2) Primary Intraperitoneal Abscesses (rare, developing without an overt identifiable primary source of peritoneal breach, analogous to primary peritonitis).
  - By Anatomical Location:
    - Perihepatic Space Abscesses: Right subphrenic space (between liver and diaphragm), right subhepatic space (Morison pouch, the most dependent portion of the right paravertebral groove in the supine position), left subphrenic space, and left subhepatic space (divided by gastrohepatic omentum into anterior space and lesser sac).
    - Lesser Sac Abscesses: Located in the largest recess behind the stomach, pancreas, and kidneys, communicating with the greater sac via the foramen of Winslow.
    - Pelvic Abscesses: Located in the dependent rectovesical or rectouterine (pouch of Douglas) spaces.
    - Paracolic and Interloop Abscesses: Situated along the right or left paracolic gutters or trapped between adjacent loops of small or large bowel and mesentery.
    - Visceral & Perivisceral Abscesses: Formed immediately adjacent to diseased hollow viscera (periappendiceal, pericolic, pericholecystic, tubo-ovarian).
  - By Acquired Risk Category: Community-acquired (low-risk vs. high-risk based on APACHE II scores, comorbidities, and resistance risk) vs. Healthcare-associated / Postoperative (complicating elective or emergency operations, characterized by multidrug-resistant nosocomial pathogens).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Frequency and Etiologic Causes: Intraperitoneal abscesses occur secondarily in 10% to 30% of complicated peritonitis cases. Historical distribution of locations has shifted: historical series (1970s) showed 44% in right lower quadrant, 14% left lower quadrant, 14% pelvic, 20% perihepatic; while later series show subphrenic (26%), pelvic (20%), paracolic (13%), periappendicular (13%), retroperitoneal (10%), hepatic (7%), interloop (4%), and lesser sac (4%).
  - Underlying Primary Diseases: Acute appendicitis (associated most commonly with right lower quadrant, pelvic, and subphrenic abscesses, responsible for >50% of subphrenic abscesses in children); colonic diverticulitis (left lower quadrant and pelvic abscesses); acute pancreatitis or pancreatic necrosis (lesser sac abscesses); peptic ulcer perforation (right subhepatic or lesser sac); inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn disease, ulcerative colitis); penetrating abdominal trauma (stabbing, gunshot); abdominal surgery (dehiscence of surgical anastomoses, operative contamination); and pelvic inflammatory disease / tubo-ovarian abscesses.
  - Postoperative Risk Factors: Left subphrenic abscesses are particularly frequent following gastric surgery if an incidental splenectomy was performed (whereas right subhepatic abscesses predominate if splenectomy was omitted).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Polymicrobial Nature: Intraperitoneal abscesses are characteristically polymicrobial, containing an average of 4.5 species per specimen (typically 2.5 anaerobic species and 2 aerobic/facultative species). Anaerobes are recovered in 60% to 90% of cases when meticulous anaerobic collection and transport techniques are utilized.
  - Obligate Anaerobes: Bacteroides fragilis (the single most prevalent anaerobic isolate), non-fragilis Bacteroides species, Prevotella species (including Prevotella melaninogenica), Fusobacterium species, Bilophila wadsworthia, Peptostreptococcus species, Parvimonas species, Eubacterium species, and Clostridium species (such as Clostridium perfringens).
  - Aerobic and Facultative Gram-Negative Bacilli: Escherichia coli (the predominant facultative isolate), Klebsiella species, Proteus species, Enterobacter species, Citrobacter species, and Serratia species.
  - Aerobic and Facultative Gram-Positive Cocci: Viridans group streptococci, Streptococcus anginosus group, Enterococcus faecalis, Enterococcus faecium, and Staphylococcus aureus (in 2% to 4% of community cases, but higher in healthcare-associated cases).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children: Appendicitis remains the leading cause (>50% of subphrenic/pelvic abscesses), yielding polymicrobial flora dominated by E. coli, B. fragilis, Fusobacterium species (marked expansion in pediatric appendiceal abscesses), Bilophila wadsworthia, and anaerobic cocci. Primary pneumococcal peritonitis/abscess occurs in children with nephrotic syndrome.
  - Adults & Elderly: Polymicrobial flora dominated by B. fragilis, E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Pseudomonas, and enterococci. Elderly patients, those with healthcare-associated infections, or those with prolonged antibiotic exposure have higher isolation rates of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter species, ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae, and enterococci.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Nosocomial / Resistant Pathogens (in Healthcare-Associated or Tertiary Abscesses): Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Acinetobacter baumannii, Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococci (VRE, e.g., Enterococcus faecium).
  - Fungi: Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, Candida tropicalis, and non-albicans Candida species (isolated in complicated, perforated, or tertiary peritonitis/abscesses, especially in patients with gastric/duodenal perforations, central lines, broad-spectrum antibiotics, or TPN).
  - Parasites & Mycobacteria: Entamoeba histolytica (amebic liver abscess rupture into peritoneum causing amebic intraperitoneal abscess in ~10% of cases); Mycobacterium tuberculosis (tuberculous peritonitis/abscess with caseating granulomas); Coccidioides immitis (granulomatous peritonitis/abscess); Strongyloides stercoralis; and Actinomyces israelii (pelvic/abdominal actinomycosis).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Anatomical Compartmentalization & Fluid Gravity: The peritoneal cavity is divided by anatomical barriers: the transverse mesocolon divides it into upper and lower compartments; the greater omentum separates upper from lower space; the small bowel mesentery divides the lower space; the phrenicocolic ligament partially bridges the left paracolic gutter and left perihepatic space; while the right paracolic gutter communicates freely with the right subhepatic (Morison pouch) and right subphrenic spaces. The falciform ligament separates right and left subphrenic spaces, preventing bilateral spread (explaining why only 5% to 15% of subphrenic abscesses are bilateral). Fluid exudate drains along dependent anatomical pathways (paracolic gutters, Morison pouch, rectovesical/rectouterine pouches).
  - Synergistic Biphasic Pathogenesis: Experiments demonstrate a classic two-stage development: initial peritoneal contamination with colonic flora leads to an acute phase driven by facultative gram-negative bacilli (E. coli), causing early bacteremia and high mortality; surviving hosts enter a chronic second phase characterized by localized intraabdominal abscess formation where obligate anaerobes (B. fragilis) predominate.
  - Molecular Immunopathogenesis of Abscess Formation: B. fragilis capsule and capsular polysaccharides (such as zwitterionic capsular polysaccharide A / PSA) interact with host components, specifically stimulating IL-17-producing CD4+ T lymphocytes. IL-17 signaling recruits neutrophils and drives localized fibrinous encapsulation, sequestering bacteria within an abscess.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Fibrinous Exudation & Wall Isolation: Inflammatory stimuli trigger peritoneal capillary permeability, pouring out protein-rich fluid (>3 g/dL) containing fibrinogen and polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs). Fibrinogen polymerizes into fibrinous exudative plaques that coat inflamed peritoneal surfaces, gluing together adjacent bowel loops, mesentery, and omentum to wall off purulent exudate into a fibrous capsule.
  - Localized Hypoxia & Bacterial Protection: Within the walled-off fibrous capsule, intense neutrophilic phagocytosis, respiratory burst, and bacterial metabolism consume available oxygen, creating a severely hypoxic and acidic microenvironment. Anaerobic bacteria thrive, producing succinic acid and short-chain fatty acids that inhibit remaining neutrophil killing and impair antibiotic penetration.
  - Pathologic Effects of Uncontained Abscess: Continued expansion of an uncontained abscess leads to erosion into surrounding blood vessels (hemorrhage), erosion into adjacent hollow viscera (forming internal fistulas), rupture into the free peritoneal cavity (causing secondary diffuse peritonitis and uroseptic shock), or erosion through the abdominal wall (colocutaneous fistula).

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Insidious or Delayed Postoperative Fever: Characteristically develops in a patient recovering from abdominal surgery, peritonitis, or trauma who stops improving; fever returns (spiking, intermittent, or hectic fever), accompanied by shaking chills / rigors, night sweats, and persistent malaise.
  - Localized Pain and Tenderness: Abdominal pain and localized tenderness over the involved site. Pain varies by location:
    - Subphrenic Abscess: Upper abdominal pain accompanied by chest findings, shoulder pain (referred pain via phrenic nerve irritation of central diaphragmatic peritoneum), pleuritic chest pain, hiccoughs, cough, and dyspnea.
    - Subhepatic / Morison Pouch Abscess: Dominant subcostal or right upper quadrant pain with less pulmonary involvement.
    - Pelvic Abscess: Suprapubic pain, pelvic pressure, diarrhea, tenesmus, mucus in stool (rectal irritation), urinary frequency, or dysuria (bladder irritation).
    - Interloop / Paracolic Abscess: Deep, poorly localized abdominal aching, localized tenderness, partial mechanical small bowel obstruction, or palpable mass.
  - Systemic & Constitutional Symptoms: Anorexia, nausea, vomiting, weight loss, failure to thrive, lethargy, or fever of unknown origin (FUO) in chronic indolent subphrenic cases lasting months.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Fever & Tachycardia: High intermittent fever (temperatures up to 40°C to 42°C), resting tachycardia, and tachypnea.
  - Localized Abdominal Signs: Localized abdominal tenderness, voluntary or involuntary guarding, and rebound tenderness over the abscess site; a fixed, tender, palpable abdominal or pelvic mass may be detected on abdominal examination or digital rectal / bimanual vaginal examination (in pelvic abscesses).
  - Thoracic / Diaphragmatic Signs (in Subphrenic Abscesses): Costal tenderness, decreased breath sounds at the right or left lung base, dullness to percussion (pleural effusion), basilar rales, and elevation or immobility of the hemidiaphragm.
  - Septic Shock Signs: Hypotension, altered mental status (apathy, restlessness, or delirium), hypothermia (subnormal temperature ~35°C in late severe sepsis), oliguria, and cool/clammy extremities.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Spiking Postoperative / Post-Peritonitis Fever + Localized Tenderness + Encapsulated Fluid Collection on CT: The classic diagnostic combination confirming an intraperitoneal abscess.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Marked leukocytosis (15,000 to 25,000 cells/uL or higher) with a left shift (bandemia >10%). Leukopenia (<4,000 cells/uL) indicates severe uroseptic shock. Anemia (hemoglobin <10 g/dL) is an independent predictor of increased mortality.
  - Systemic Inflammatory Markers: Markedly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR), and procalcitonin (PCT).
  - Serum Chemistry & Organ Function: Elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN >25-28 mg/dL, an independent predictor of mortality) and creatinine indicating acute kidney injury; hypoalbuminemia (<3.0 g/dL); elevated liver enzymes; and metabolic acidosis.
  - Peritoneal Fluid / Paracentesis Analysis (if aspirated): Total protein >1 g/dL (often >3 g/dL), elevated LDH above upper limit of normal for serum, glucose <50 mg/dL, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) >5 ng/mL, or alkaline phosphatase >240 U/L (indicates bowel perforation). Ascitic neutrophil count >=250 cells/mm3 with polymicrobial Gram stain.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Computed Tomography (CT) of Abdomen and Pelvis with Oral and Intravenous Contrast (Diagnostic Imaging Modality of Choice): Preferred initial diagnostic study (overall diagnostic sensitivity and specificity >90% to 95%). CT findings include: (1) Well-defined, low-density fluid tissue collection with a peripheral rim-enhancing capsule; (2) Extraluminal gas / air-fluid levels within the fluid collection (highly suggestive of abscess); (3) Mass effect displacing adjacent viscera or obstructing ureters (causing hydronephrosis); (4) Perilesional inflammatory fat stranding.
  - Ultrasonography: Useful for rapid bedside evaluation, particularly in right upper quadrant (subphrenic/subhepatic), pelvic, and retroperitoneal collections, showing fluid collection with irregular walls and internal echoes. Limited by overlying bowel gas, obesity, open wounds, dressings, and drains.
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): High sensitivity for soft tissue resolution, three-dimensional depiction, and evaluating liver/pancreatic collections without ionizing radiation or iodinated contrast.
  - Plain Abdominal & Chest Radiographs: Abnormal in ~50% of subphrenic abscesses; demonstrates elevation or immobility of hemidiaphragm, basilar atelectasis, pleural effusion, extraluminal gas, air-fluid levels, or visceral displacement.
  - Radionuclide Scanning (Indium-111 or Gallium-67 Tagged Leukocyte Scans): Historical utility for detecting occult inflammation ("hot spots"), largely supplanted by CT and MRI due to false positives from bowel excretion/healing surgical wounds.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Abscess Fluid Culture (MANDATORY Core Diagnostic Standard): Gram stain and quantitative aerobic/anaerobic cultures of purulent fluid obtained via image-guided percutaneous needle aspiration or surgical drainage. Meticulous collection, transport in anaerobic transport media, and rapid processing are essential for recovering fastidious anaerobes (B. fragilis, Peptostreptococcus, Fusobacterium).
  - Blood Cultures: Mandatory in hospitalized, septic, or high-risk cases prior to antibiotics; positive in 20% to 30% of intraperitoneal abscess cases (recovering B. fragilis, E. coli, or enterococci).
  - Molecular Diagnostics: 16S rRNA gene PCR sequencing identifies unculturable or fastidious anaerobic bacteria.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Abdominal and Pelvic Contrast-Enhanced Computed Tomography (CT) combined with CT-guided percutaneous fine-needle aspiration/drainage yielding purulent fluid and positive microbial cultures represents the gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Hemodynamic Resuscitation: Immediate intravenous fluid resuscitation with balanced crystalloids (Lactated Ringer's or Normal Saline) to correct volume depletion, maintain urinary output (>0.5 mL/kg/h), and preserve renal perfusion; vasopressor support (norepinephrine) for persistent septic shock.
  - Sepsis & Nutritional Support: ICU admission for severe sepsis or organ failure; NPO status; enteral nutrition (preferred as soon as bowel function returns) or total parenteral nutrition (TPN) to maintain gut mucosal barrier; antipyretics and analgesics.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Principles of Antibiotic Therapy: Antibiotic therapy ALONE is insufficient for established abscesses and MUST be combined with source control (drainage); empiric coverage must extend to enteric facultative gram-negative bacilli (E. coli) AND obligate anaerobes (B. fragilis).
  - Mild-to-Moderate Community-Acquired Intraperitoneal Abscess Regimens:
    1. Single-Agent Parenteral: Ertapenem 1 g IV q24h OR Moxifloxacin 400 mg IV q24h.
    2. Combination Parenteral: Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Cefotaxime 2 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Regimens for Severe, High-Risk Community-Acquired, Healthcare-Associated, or Tertiary Abscesses:
    1. Single-Agent Parenteral: Piperacillin-tazobactam 3.375 g to 4.5 g IV q6h OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Imipenem-cilastatin 500 mg IV q6h OR Doripenem 500 mg IV q8h OR Meropenem-vaborbactam 2 g/1 g IV q8h.
    2. Combination Parenteral: Cefepime 2 g IV q8-12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Ceftazidime-avibactam 2.5 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Ceftolozane-tazobactam 1.5 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Aztreonam 2 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h PLUS Vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg IV q8-12h.
    3. Addition of Antienterococcal / Anti-MRSA / Antifungal Agents: Add Vancomycin or Linezolid / Daptomycin for healthcare-associated infections, VRE colonization, or prior antibiotic exposure. Add Fluconazole (or Echinocandins such as Caspofungin / Micafungin) for high-risk patients with upper GI perforations, recurrent post-operative abscesses, or positive fungal stains.
  - Oral Step-Down Therapy: Transition to oral amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg PO bid OR ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO bid plus metronidazole 500 mg PO tid once fever resolves, leukocytosis normalizes, and oral intake is tolerated.
  - Duration of Antibiotic Therapy: Continue for 4 to 7 days post-drainage once adequate source control is established and clinical improvement occurs.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Image-Guided Percutaneous Catheter Drainage (Preferred First-Line Interventional Standard): CT- or ultrasound-guided percutaneous catheter drainage is successful in 80% to 90% of cases. Requirements for percutaneous drainage: (1) Safe percutaneous imaging window avoiding vital organs/vessels; (2) Unilocular fluid collection; (3) Absence of uncorrected coagulopathy; (4) Available surgical backup. Catheter is placed for continuous gravity or low-suction drainage until daily output is minimal (<10-15 mL/day) and repeat scanning documents cavity collapse (typically 5 to 14 days).
  - Open or Laparoscopic Surgical Drainage: Indicated when percutaneous drainage fails, when abscesses are poorly organized, multiloculated, multiple, or inaccessible via a safe percutaneous route, when thick viscid pus/debris clogs catheters, or when concurrent surgical repair of underlying pathology (e.g., bowel resection, fistula closure) is required.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Septic Shock, Multiorgan Dysfunction Syndrome (MODS), and Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS).
  - Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) secondary to hypovolemia or uroseptic shock.
  - Free Rupture into Peritoneal Cavity causing secondary generalized peritonitis.
  - Erosion into Blood Vessels causing massive intraabdominal or gastrointestinal hemorrhage.
  - Suppurative Pylephlebitis (portal vein septic thrombophlebitis) and Pyogenic Liver Abscesses.
  - Procedural Complications of Percutaneous Drainage (5% to 15% rate): Hemorrhage, bacteremic seeding, bowel perforation, or inadvertent fistula formation.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Recurrent or Residual Intraperitoneal Abscesses (secondary to incomplete drainage or persistent source).
  - Fistula Formation: External colocutaneous fistulas, or internal colovesical, colovaginal, or enterocutaneous fistulas.
  - Extensive Intraabdominal Adhesions leading to late adhesive small bowel obstruction.
  - Prolonged Hospitalization and Morbidity (average hospital stays 21 to 47 days).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Morison Pouch: Most dependent recess of the right paravertebral groove (right subhepatic space) in the supine position.
  - Foramen of Winslow: Anatomical opening connecting the lesser sac to the greater peritoneal cavity.
  - Biphasic Experimental Sepsis Model: Onderdonk/Weinstein rat model demonstrating early E. coli mortality followed by late B. fragilis intraabdominal abscess formation.
  - Zwitterionic Capsular Polysaccharide A (PSA): B. fragilis capsular antigen that directly stimulates IL-17-producing CD4+ T cells to induce intraperitoneal abscess formation.
  - Rim-Enhancing Capsule: Characteristic CT finding of a fluid collection surrounded by a hyperenhancing fibrous wall.
  - Percutaneous Catheter Drainage (PCD): First-line interventional procedure replacing routine open surgical drainage for unilocular abscesses.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Falciform Ligament Isolation Exception: The falciform ligament physically separates the right and left subphrenic spaces, preventing bilateral spread and explaining why only 5% to 15% of subphrenic abscesses are bilateral.
  - Antibiotic Monotherapy Ineffectiveness: Systemic antibiotics alone CANNOT cure an established encapsulated intraperitoneal abscess without mechanical source control (percutaneous or surgical drainage).
  - Splenectomy Left Subphrenic Shift: Postoperative subphrenic abscesses following gastric operations occur in the left subphrenic space if an incidental splenectomy was performed, but in the right subhepatic space if splenectomy was omitted.
  - Enterococcal Coverage Omission in Low-Risk Cases: Although enterococci are isolated from ~20% of intraperitoneal abscesses, routine antienterococcal coverage is UNNECESSARY for low-risk community-acquired cases (treating E. coli and B. fragilis reduces enterococcal counts), but IS MANDATORY in healthcare-associated cases, liver transplants, or VRE colonization.
  - Postoperative Drainage Timing Rule: Percutaneous drains are removed when daily output drops to minimal levels (<10-15 mL/day) and CT/ultrasound confirms complete cavity collapse.
