# Diverticulitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Diverticulitis is defined as inflammation and secondary bacterial infection of the colonic wall initiated by microperforation or macroperforation of one or more colonic diverticula (saccular mucosal and submucosal herniations through the muscularis propria of the colon).
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Uncomplicated Diverticulitis: Inflammation confined to the colonic wall or immediate peridiverticular tissue without phlegmon, abscess, stricture, fistula, or free intraperitoneal perforation (accounts for 70% to 80% of clinical cases).
  - Complicated Diverticulitis: Inflammation accompanied by local or distant abscess formation, phlegmon, stricture/intestinal obstruction, fistula formation (colovesical, colovaginal, colocutaneous, or enterocolic), or macroperforation leading to generalized purulent or fecal peritonitis.
  - Hinchey Staging Classification for Perforated / Complicated Diverticulitis:
    - Stage I: Pericolic or mesenteric abscess confined by local pericolic tissues.
    - Stage II: Distal pelvic, retroperitoneal, or abdominal abscess resulting from local perforation.
    - Stage III: Generalized purulent peritonitis resulting from rupture of a pericolic or pelvic abscess (no open communication with colonic lumen).
    - Stage IV: Generalized fecal peritonitis resulting from free open perforation of a diverticulum with gross fecal contamination.
  - Anatomic / Geographic Classifications:
    - Left-Sided Diverticulitis (Western Type): Involves false pseudodiverticula (mucosa and submucosa herniated through muscularis) located predominantly in the sigmoid and descending colon.
    - Right-Sided Diverticulitis (Asian Type): Predominantly right-sided (cecal/ascending colon), involves all layers of the colonic wall (true diverticula), often presents at a younger age, and likely represents a distinct pathophysiologic entity.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Prevalence & Demographics: Diverticulosis is present in 30% to 40% (and up to 50% to 66% of individuals over 70 to 85 years) of Western populations, but less than 1% in rural Asia and Africa. Approximately 4% to 25% (commonly cited 10% to 25%) of individuals with diverticulosis develop symptomatic clinical disease (diverticulitis). In the United States, diverticulitis causes over 150,000 hospital admissions and 24,000 elective colectomies annually.
  - Risk Factors: Low dietary fiber intake (the most extensively studied risk factor, which increases stool transit time and intracolonic pressure); high intake of red meat and Western diet; physical inactivity / lack of exercise; obesity (elevated BMI increases risks of diverticulitis and diverticular bleeding); prolonged use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroids, or opiates (strongly associated with increased risk of diverticulitis and colonic perforation); advanced age; and immunosuppression (transplant recipients, chronic steroids, renal failure). Contrary to historical myth, consumption of nuts, corn, and popcorn does NOT increase the risk of diverticulitis (and may be protective).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Polymicrobial Infection: Diverticulitis is characteristically a polymicrobial infection caused by endogenous colonic anaerobic and facultative bacterial flora escaping across damaged mucosal barriers.
  - Obligate Anaerobes: Bacteroides species (notably Bacteroides fragilis, present in the vast majority of intraabdominal infections), Peptostreptococcus species, Prevotella species, Fusobacterium species, and Clostridium species.
  - Facultative and Aerobic Gram-Negative Bacilli: Enterobacteriaceae family, predominantly Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae, as well as Proteus species, Enterobacter species, and Citrobacter species.
  - Gram-Positive Cocci: Viridans group streptococci, Enterococcus faecalis, Enterococcus faecium, and Streptococcus anginosus group.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Adults and Elderly: Endogenous polymicrobial flora dominated by Bacteroides fragilis, Peptostreptococcus species, E. coli, Klebsiella species, viridans streptococci, and enterococci. Older adults and immunocompromised hosts have higher rates of multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (ESBL producers) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
  - Younger Adults (<40 years): Polymicrobial colonic flora; younger patients (especially young obese males) may present with more virulent or recurrent episodes requiring surgical evaluation.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter species, and Enterobacteriaceae producing extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) or carbapenemases (in healthcare-associated, post-surgical, or tertiary peritonitis cases).
  - Fungi: Candida albicans and non-albicans Candida species (isolated in complicated, perforated, or tertiary peritonitis in immunocompromised or broad-spectrum antibiotic-treated hosts).
  - Parasitic & Mycobacterial Mimics: Entamoeba histolytica (ameboma / amebic colitis), Mycobacterium tuberculosis (gastrointestinal TB), Schistosoma species (bilharzioma), and Actinomyces species (pelvic/colonic actinomycosis).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Herniation & Vascular Vulnerability: Pseudodiverticula develop at anatomically weak points in the colonic wall where nutrient arteries (vasa recta) penetrate the circular muscle layer. High intraluminal pressure combined with focal mural weakness drives mucosal/submucosal herniation.
  - Mechanical Obstruction & Microperforation: Classically, a hard inspissated fecal mass (fecalith) becomes trapped within the neck of a diverticulum. Continued mucus secretion leads to intraluminal distention, venous congestion, focal ischemia, and necrosis of the thin diverticular wall, causing microperforation and bacterial invasion into pericolic fat.
  - Intestinal Dysbiosis & Immune Homeostasis: Emerging evidence indicates that low-fiber Western diets alter the colonic microbiome (intestinal dysbiosis), disrupting mucosal immune homeostasis and inducing chronic low-grade mucosal inflammation, which predisposes to mucosal erosion and microperforation without requiring mechanical fecalith obstruction.
  - Spectrum of Perforation & Contained Spread:
    - Microperforation: Inflammation remains well localized, contained by pericolic fat and mesentery, forming a small pericolic phlegmon or localized abscess.
    - Macroperforation: Extravasation of gas and pus forms larger distant intraabdominal or pelvic abscesses, or erodes into adjacent organs (forming colovesical, colovaginal, or enterocolic fistulas).
    - Free Rupture / Macro-Perforation: Free rupture of an uncontained diverticulum or abscess into the peritoneal cavity causes diffuse purulent (Hinchey III) or fecal (Hinchey IV) peritonitis.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Acute Suppurative Inflammation & Abscess Formation: Bacterial invasion into pericolic fat triggers intense neutrophilic infiltration, microvascular thrombosis, mural edema, and tissue necrosis. The host immune response attempts to wall off the infection using omentum and adjacent bowel loops, creating a pericolic phlegmon or abscess.
  - Fibrosis, Stricture & Fistulization: Repeated or chronic episodes of diverticular inflammation induce transmural fibrosis, muscular hypertrophy, and luminal narrowing, resulting in colonic strictures and intestinal obstruction. Inflammation eroding into adjacent epithelial structures creates epithelialized fistulous tracts, most commonly between the sigmoid colon and bladder (colovesical fistula) or vagina (colovaginal fistula).

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Left Lower Quadrant Abdominal Pain: Constant, aching, or crampy abdominal pain classically localized to the left lower quadrant (LLQ) in Western patients (or right lower quadrant in right-sided Asian diverticulitis). Pain is often present for several days prior to presentation and may be recurrent.
  - Associated Gastrointestinal Symptoms: Low-grade fever, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, constipation, or diarrhea / alteration in bowel habits. Trace blood in stool may occur, but frank hematochezia is uncommon and suggests an alternative diagnosis.
  - Systemic & Complicated Symptoms: High fever, rigors, severe generalized abdominal pain, distention, and prostration indicate free perforation and diffuse peritonitis.
  - Fistula-Specific Symptoms: Pneumaturia (passage of gas in urine), fecaluria (passage of feces in urine), and recurrent urinary tract infections (indicates colovesical fistula); or passage of feces/flatus per vagina (indicates colovaginal fistula).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Localized Abdominal Tenderness: Point tenderness on palpation in the left lower quadrant or suprapubic region, often accompanied by localized voluntary guarding and rebound tenderness.
  - Palpable Abdominal Mass: A tender, fixed mass may be palpable in the left lower quadrant (representing a pericolic phlegmon, walled-off abscess, or inflammatory mass).
  - Peritoneal Signs & Distention: Involuntary abdominal rigidity, generalized rebound tenderness, and absent bowel sounds indicate free perforation and diffuse peritonitis. Hyperactive bowel sounds may indicate secondary mechanical obstruction.
  - Digital Rectal Examination: Tenderness or a palpable mass in the rectovesical or rectouterine pouch.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Left Lower Quadrant Pain + Localized Tenderness + Fever + Leukocytosis: The classic clinical quadra for acute sigmoid diverticulitis.
  - Pneumaturia and Fecaluria: Highly specific clinical signs indicating a colovesical fistula complicating diverticulitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis (typically 12,000 to 18,000 cells/uL) with a neutrophilic shift and bandemia (left shift) present in 69% to 93% of cases. Normal WBC count does not rule out diverticulitis.
  - Inflammatory Biomarkers: Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR). Elevated procalcitonin suggests severe sepsis or complicated perforated disease.
  - Serum Chemistry & Renal Function: BUN and serum creatinine evaluate hydration status and renal function. Liver function tests and serum lipase/amylase help rule out hepatobiliary or pancreatic pathologies.
  - Urinalysis: Sterile pyuria or microscopic hematuria may be present if pericolonic inflammation abuts the bladder or ureter.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Abdominal and Pelvic Computed Tomography (CT) with Intravenous and Oral/Rectal Contrast (Diagnostic Imaging Modality of Choice): CT is the gold standard diagnostic imaging tool with sensitivity (97%), specificity (100%), and overall accuracy (99%). Classic CT findings include: (1) Pericolic fat stranding (98%); (2) Colonic diverticula (84%); (3) Segmental colonic wall thickening >4 mm (70%); (4) Phlegmon or fluid collection / abscess formation (35%); (5) Arrowhead sign (focal thickening pointing to inflamed diverticulum); (6) Extraluminal air / pneumoperitoneum (indicates perforation); (7) Obstructive hydronephrosis due to ureteral compression.
  - Ultrasonography: Graded compression ultrasonography is a safe alternative (sensitivity 85%-90%, specificity 80%-90%), showing thickened colonic wall >4 mm, non-compressible inflamed diverticulum, pericolic fat halo, or abscess. Highly operator-dependent and limited by overlying bowel gas.
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): High sensitivity (86%-94%) and specificity (88%-92%) without ionizing radiation; useful in pregnancy or severe contrast allergy.
  - Plain Abdominal Radiographs: May show free intraperitoneal air beneath the diaphragm (pneumoperitoneum in free perforation), bowel distention, or soft tissue mass effect.
  - Colonoscopy and Barium Enema STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED in Acute Setting: Colonoscopy and double-contrast barium enemas are STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED during acute diverticulitis due to high risk of converting a microperforation into a free macroperforation; colonoscopy should be deferred for 6 to 8 weeks after resolution to rule out underlying colon carcinoma.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Blood Cultures: Recommended in hospitalized patients with severe illness, high fever, or signs of systemic sepsis (positive in a subset of complicated cases).
  - Intraoperative / Percutaneous Abscess Fluid Cultures: Gram stain and quantitative aerobic/anaerobic cultures of purulent fluid obtained during percutaneous catheter drainage or operative intervention guide targeted antibiotic therapy.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Abdominal and Pelvic Contrast-Enhanced Computed Tomography (CT) represents the gold standard diagnostic test for confirming acute diverticulitis, establishing severity, staging per Hinchey criteria, and guiding interventional management.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Triage & Outpatient vs Inpatient Decision:
    - Outpatient Management: Suitable for mild, uncomplicated diverticulitis in reliable patients who can tolerate oral hydration, have mild pain, and lack significant comorbidities or severe systemic toxicity (success rate 94% to 97%).
    - Inpatient Admission Indications: Complicated diverticulitis (abscess, phlegmon, perforation, obstruction), inability to tolerate oral fluids, severe pain requiring IV narcotics, high fever, advanced age, immunosuppression, or failure of outpatient therapy after 48 to 72 hours.
  - Supportive Inpatient Measures: Bowel rest (NPO or clear liquid diet), IV fluid resuscitation with balanced crystalloids, correction of electrolyte disturbances, IV analgesics, and nasogastric tube decompression if ileus or obstruction is present.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Selective Antibiotic Use in Uncomplicated Diverticulitis: Current guidelines (AGA, SIS, WSES) recommend SELECTIVE rather than routine use of antibiotics in mild, uncomplicated acute diverticulitis in low-risk immunocompetent patients, as conservative management without antibiotics yields equivalent resolution without increasing complications. Antibiotics ARE mandatory for complicated disease, high-risk patients, immunocompromised hosts, or severe illness.
  - Outpatient Oral Antimicrobial Regimens (7 to 10 Days Duration):
    1. Combination Oral Regimens: Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO bid PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg PO tid OR Levofloxacin 500 mg PO daily PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg PO tid OR Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) 160/800 mg PO bid PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg PO tid OR Cefdinir 300 mg PO bid PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg PO tid.
    2. Single-Agent Oral Regimen: Amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg PO bid (or 1000 mg PO bid) OR Moxifloxacin 400 mg PO daily.
  - Inpatient Parenteral Regimens for Mild-to-Moderate Community-Acquired Diverticulitis:
    1. Single-Agent Parenteral: Ertapenem 1 g IV q24h OR Moxifloxacin 400 mg IV q24h.
    2. Combination Parenteral: Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Cefotaxime 2 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - High-Risk / Severe / Healthcare-Associated / Immunocompromised Regimens:
    - Single-Agent: Piperacillin-tazobactam 3.375 g to 4.5 g IV q6h OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Imipenem-cilastatin 500 mg IV q6h OR Doripenem 500 mg IV q8h.
    - Combination: Cefepime 2 g IV q8-12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Aztreonam 2 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h PLUS Vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg IV q8-12h.
  - Duration of Antibiotic Therapy: 7 to 10 days for uncomplicated cases managed with antibiotics; 4 to 7 days postoperatively / post-drainage for complicated cases once source control is achieved.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Percutaneous Image-Guided Catheter Drainage: Preferred initial interventional management for large peridiverticular or pelvic abscesses (>3 to 5 cm in diameter; Hinchey Stage II). CT- or ultrasound-guided catheter placement drains purulence, controls sepsis, and converts an emergency two-stage operation into a safer elective one-stage resection.
  - Emergency Surgical Resection: Indicated for generalized purulent or fecal peritonitis (Hinchey III/IV), uncontrolled sepsis, free perforation, large un-drainable abscesses, or failure of medical/percutaneous management.
    - Two-Stage Hartmann Procedure: Resection of diseased bowel, creation of an end colostomy, and closure of the rectal stump (the classic procedure for free perforation or severe peritonitis).
    - Primary Resection with Immediate Anastomosis (with or without diverting loop ileostomy): Performed in selected stable patients after intraoperative colonic lavage.
  - Elective Sigmoid Colectomy: Indicated for recurrent symptomatic episodes, persistent fistulas, chronic stricture/obstruction, or high-risk immunocompromised patients after resolution of acute inflammation.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Peridiverticular or Pelvic Abscess Formation (occurs in ~15% to 30% of complicated cases).
  - Colonic Perforation and Diffuse Suppurative or Fecal Peritonitis (Hinchey III/IV).
  - Acute Mechanical Colonic Obstruction (secondary to inflammatory edema, spasm, or pericolic phlegmon).
  - Suppurative Pylephlebitis (septic thrombophlebitis of the portal venous system) and Pyogenic Liver Abscesses.
  - Septic Shock and Multiorgan Failure.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Chronic Colonic Stricture and Intestinal Stenosis (secondary to transmural scarring and fibrosis following recurrent inflammation).
  - Internal and External Fistula Formation: Colovesical fistula (most common, 65%), colovaginal fistula, colocutaneous fistula, and enterocolic fistula.
  - Recurrent Diverticulitis: Occurs in 20% to 30% of patients following a first episode managed medically.
  - Post-Diverticulitis Irritable Bowel Syndrome / Chronic Abdominal Pain.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Left-Sided Appendicitis: Historical clinical descriptor for acute sigmoid diverticulitis due to identical clinical features mirrored on the left side of the abdomen.
  - Arrowhead Sign: CT imaging descriptor showing triangular accumulation of contrast pointing toward an inflamed diverticulum.
  - Pericolic Fat Stranding: Hazy linear density attenuation in pericolic fat on CT, pathognomonic for active pericolonic inflammation.
  - Pneumaturia & Fecaluria: Pathognomonic clinical signs indicating a colovesical fistula.
  - Hartmann Procedure: Classic emergency two-stage operation (sigmoid resection, end colostomy, rectal stump closure).
  - Hinchey Staging System: Four-tier pathologic classification system for complicated perforated diverticulitis (I to IV).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Selective Antibiotic Omission Rule: Antibiotics are NOT routinely required for mild, uncomplicated diverticulitis in low-risk, immunocompetent adults; conservative management (hydration, symptom control) achieves equivalent cure rates.
  - Acute Endoscopy Contraindication: Diagnostic colonoscopy and barium enemas are STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED during acute diverticulitis due to high risk of iatrogenic free perforation (colonoscopy must be delayed 6 to 8 weeks post-resolution).
  - Nuts and Popcorn Myth: Consumption of popcorn, nuts, and corn does NOT increase the risk of diverticulitis or diverticular bleeding (and nuts/popcorn may actually be protective).
  - Right-Sided Asian Diverticulitis: Right-sided (cecal) diverticulitis is common in Asian populations, involves true diverticula (all mural layers), affects younger individuals, and mimics acute appendicitis.
  - Hematochezia Incompatibility: Gross lower GI bleeding (hematochezia) rarely occurs simultaneously with active acute diverticulitis; presentation of heavy bleeding should prompt evaluation for diverticular hemorrhage without diverticulitis or alternative pathology.
  - Percutaneous Drainage Cutoff: Abscesses <=3 to 5 cm can be treated with IV antibiotics alone, whereas abscesses >3 to 5 cm require percutaneous image-guided catheter drainage.
