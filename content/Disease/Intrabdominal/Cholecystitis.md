# Cholecystitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Cholecystitis is an acute or chronic inflammatory and infectious condition involving the gallbladder, most commonly triggered by obstruction of the cystic duct or neck of the gallbladder by an impacted gallstone, leading to increased intraluminal pressure, gallbladder wall edema, ischemia, mucosal injury, and secondary bacterial or fungal infection.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Acute Calculous Cholecystitis: Acute inflammation of the gallbladder initiated by gallstone impaction in the cystic duct or Hartmann pouch; accounts for 85% to 98% of all cholecystitis cases.
  - Acute Acalculous Cholecystitis: Severe inflammation of the gallbladder occurring in the absence of gallstones; accounts for 2% to 15% of cases, typically arising in critically ill, traumatized, burned, or septic patients experiencing bile stasis, gallbladder ischemia, and systemic inflammation.
  - Gangrenous Cholecystitis: Advanced ischemic and necrotizing form of acute cholecystitis characterized by mural necrosis, mucosal sloughing, intramural hemorrhage, and high risk of perforation.
  - Emphysematous Cholecystitis: A severe, life-threatening variant characterized by gas formation within the gallbladder wall, lumen, or pericholecystic tissues caused by gas-producing organisms (Clostridium species, E. coli, Klebsiella species); predominantly affects elderly diabetic males and carries high mortality.
  - Chronic Cholecystitis: Persistent, low-grade, or recurrent mucosal and mural inflammation of the gallbladder secondary to repeated episodes of biliary colic or mechanical irritation from gallstones, characterized histologically by mural fibrosis, mucosal atrophy, and thickening.
  - Gallbladder Empyema / Suppurative Cholecystitis: Accumulation of frank purulent exudate (pus) under pressure within a distended, obstructed gallbladder cavity.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Prevalence & Disease Burden: Tens of millions of individuals in the United States have gallstones (cholelithiasis), and 1% to 3% of symptomatic gallstone patients develop acute cholecystitis each year. Approximately 120,000 cholecystectomies are performed annually in the United States for acute cholecystitis.
  - Risk Factors for Calculous Cholecystitis: Female sex, advancing age (peak incidence between 40 and 60 years), obesity, rapid weight loss, pregnancy, multiparity, oral contraceptive or estrogen replacement therapy, hypertriglyceridemia, total parenteral nutrition (TPN), terminal ileal disease or resection (disrupts enterohepatic bile acid circulation), and chronic Salmonella Typhi gallbladder carriage.
  - Risk Factors for Acalculous Cholecystitis: Critical illness, major surgery, severe trauma, extensive burns, prolonged mechanical ventilation, sepsis, prolonged fasting or parenteral nutrition, multiple blood transfusions, atherosclerosis, diabetes mellitus, advanced HIV/AIDS, and vasculitis.
  - Parasitic Risk Factors: Endemic hepatobiliary parasitic flukes and roundworms including Ascaris lumbricoides (mechanical obstruction of cystic or common bile duct), Clonorchis sinensis, Opisthorchis species, and Fasciola hepatica.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Infection as a Secondary Event: Secondary bacterial contamination occurs in 20% to 50% of acute cholecystitis cases overall (positive bile cultures obtained in 46% of acute cholecystitis surgical cases compared to 22% in uninflamed symptomatic cholelithiasis).
  - Facultative Gram-Negative Bacilli: Escherichia coli (most common isolate, 31% to 44%), Klebsiella species (9% to 20%), Enterobacter species (5% to 9%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (0.5% to 19%), Proteus mirabilis, Citrobacter species, and Serratia species.
  - Gram-Positive Cocci: Enterococcus species (Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium, isolated in 3% to 34% of bile cultures, often part of polymicrobial infection), Streptococcus species (2% to 10%), and Staphylococcus aureus (0% to 4%).
  - Anaerobes: Recovered in 4% to 20% of acute cholecystitis cases, including Bacteroides fragilis, Bacteroides species, Clostridium species (notably Clostridium perfringens in emphysematous cholecystitis), Fusobacterium species, and Peptostreptococcus species (isolated more frequently in patients with prior biliary surgery or biliary-enteric anastomoses).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children & Adolescents: Viral etiologies (Epstein-Barr virus, Hepatitis A/B, Adenovirus), Streptococcal species, E. coli, and Salmonella species; acalculous cholecystitis can occur during acute systemic viral or bacterial infections (such as Kawasaki disease, typhoid fever, or scarlet fever).
  - Adults & Elderly: Enteric gram-negative bacilli (E. coli, Klebsiella spp., Enterobacter spp.) and Enterococcus species dominate; Clostridium species and anaerobes increase in frequency in elderly, diabetic, or emphysematous cholecystitis patients.
  - Endemic Parasitic Regions: Ascaris lumbricoides, Clonorchis sinensis, Opisthorchis species, and Fasciola hepatica.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Bacteria & Mycobacteria: Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi and Paratyphi (cause acute/chronic typhoid cholecystitis and long-term asymptomatic gallbladder carriage); Listeria monocytogenes; Leptospira species; Mycobacterium tuberculosis; and Brucella species.
  - Viruses (in Immunocompromised Hosts): Cytomegalovirus (CMV; causing ulcerative acalculous cholecystitis in advanced AIDS or transplant recipients) and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV).
  - Protozoa & Parasites: Cryptosporidium parvum and Microsporidia (causing acalculous cholecystitis and AIDS cholangiopathy in HIV patients with CD4 <100 cells/mm3); Cystoisospora belli; Entamoeba histolytica; and Giardia lamblia.
  - Fungi: Candida albicans and Candida glabrata (in critically ill or TPN-dependent patients).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Cystic Duct Obstruction & Intraluminal Pressure: Impaction of a gallstone in the neck of the gallbladder or cystic duct halts normal gallbladder emptying. Secretion of fluid by the gallbladder mucosa into the obstructed lumen increases intraluminal pressure, causing distention.
  - Inflammatory Cascade & Ischemia: Distention impairs venous and lymphatic drainage, producing gallbladder wall edema and localized ischemia. Mucosal damage exposes wall tissue to concentrated bile salts and lysophosphatidylcholine. Local inflammation is mediated by prostaglandins, specifically prostaglandin I2 (PGI2) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2).
  - Secondary Bacterial Superinfection: Biliary stasis permits ascending microbial entry from the duodenum or translocation via portal venous drainage, leading to secondary bacterial multiplication in 20% to 50% of cases.
  - Acalculous Pathophysiology: Driven by severe gallbladder ischemia (hypoperfusion during shock, heart failure, or vasopressor therapy), endothelial injury, bile stasis (lack of CCK-mediated gallbladder contraction during prolonged fasting/TPN), hyperviscous bile sludge, and direct mucosal toxicity.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Suppurative Mural Inflammation: The gallbladder wall undergoes intense neutrophilic infiltration, transmural edema, microvascular thrombosis, and mucosal ulceration.
  - Ischemic Necrosis & Gangrene: Unrelieved intraluminal pressure and thrombosis lead to patchy or transmural gangrenous necrosis of the gallbladder wall (occurring in 10% to 15% of cases).
  - Perforation & Abscess Formation: Transmural gangrene leads to gallbladder wall perforation, manifesting as: (1) Contained perforation resulting in a pericholecystic phlegmon or abscess; (2) Free perforation into the peritoneal cavity causing secondary bacterial peritonitis; or (3) Erosion into an adjacent hollow viscus (duodenum, jejunum, or colon) forming a cholecystenteric fistula.
  - Emphysematous Gas Production: Infection by gas-producing anaerobic or facultative bacteria (Clostridium perfringens, E. coli) leads to intra-mural and intra-luminal gas accumulation, microvascular gangrene, and high risk of rapid perforation.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Continuous Right Upper Quadrant Pain: Right upper quadrant (RUQ) or epigastric abdominal pain that is characteristically continuous and persistent (lasting >6 hours, distinguishing it from self-limited, short-duration biliary colic). Pain often radiates to the right infrascapular region, right shoulder, or back.
  - Postprandial Trigger: Frequently precipitated by ingestion of a fatty meal 1 to several hours prior to symptom onset.
  - Associated Gastrointestinal Symptoms: Anorexia, nausea, vomiting, fever, chills, and abdominal bloating.
  - Subtle Presentation in Acalculous / Elderly Cases: Critically ill, intubated, or elderly patients may lack localized abdominal complaints, presenting with unexplained fever, leukocytosis, feeding intolerance, sepsis, or subtle abdominal distention.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Localized Right Upper Quadrant Tenderness: Tenderness to palpation over the right upper quadrant or epigastrium.
  - Murphy Sign: Inhibition of deep inspiration by sudden severe pain when the physician palpates the right upper quadrant directly over the gallbladder fossa while the patient inhales deeply (the clinical hallmark of acute cholecystitis).
  - Palpable Gallbladder Mass: A distended, tender gallbladder or pericholecystic inflammatory mass / phlegmon is palpable in up to 20% of cases.
  - Systemic Signs: Fever (typically mild-to-moderate, 38°C to 38.5°C; high fever >39°C suggests gangrene, empyema, or perforation), tachycardia, and mild jaundice (scleral icterus present in ~20% to 25% of cases due to adjacent biliary inflammation, extrinsic common bile duct compression, or passage of gravel/sludge).
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Murphy Sign (Clinical or Sonographic) + RUQ Pain + Fever: The clinical combination of Murphy sign, localized right upper quadrant tenderness, fever, and gallstones on imaging is highly diagnostic for acute cholecystitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis (typically 12,000 to 15,000 cells/uL) with a neutrophilic shift and bandemia (left shift). Leukocytosis >20,000 cells/uL suggests complicated disease (gangrene, empyema, or perforation).
  - Inflammatory Biomarkers: Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and serum procalcitonin (PCT).
  - Liver Function Tests: Mild elevations in serum aminotransferases (AST, ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and bilirubin. While significant jaundice suggests choledocholithiasis or cholangitis, up to 25% of patients with acute cholecystitis WITHOUT common bile duct obstruction exhibit elevated total serum bilirubin (often up to 2 to 4 mg/dL) due to transient sludge passage, direct parenchymal inflammation, or Mirizzi syndrome. Serum amylase and lipase may be mildly elevated.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Transabdominal Ultrasonography (First-Line Initial Imaging Modality of Choice): High sensitivity (88% to 90%) and specificity (80% to 88%). Key diagnostic findings include: (1) Sonographic Murphy Sign (localized maximal pain when ultrasound transducer presses directly on the visualized gallbladder); (2) Gallbladder wall thickening >4 mm; (3) Pericholecystic fluid collection; (4) Gallstones or sludge, particularly impacted in the gallbladder neck or cystic duct; (5) Gallbladder distention (luminal diameter >4 cm); (6) Striated mucosal halo or intramural hypoechoic gas/sloughing.
  - Radionuclide Cholescintigraphy (HIDA Scan / Technetium-99m Iminodiacetic Acid Scan): The most sensitive imaging modality for acute cholecystitis (sensitivity >95%, specificity >90%). Intravenously administered 99mTc-HIDA is excreted into bile. Non-visualization of the gallbladder within 60 to 120 minutes with prompt visualization of the common bile duct and duodenum confirms cystic duct obstruction and acute cholecystitis.
  - Computed Tomography (CT) of Abdomen: Useful for evaluating severe complications (gangrene, emphysematous gas, perforation, abscess, or pancreatitis). CT findings include gallbladder distention, wall thickening, pericholecystic fat stranding, intramural gas, and high-attenuation fluid.
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) / MRCP: Highly sensitive for detecting gallbladder wall edema, pericholecystic fluid, and concomitant choledocholithiasis.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Bile Cultures: Performed on bile aspirated intraoperatively or during percutaneous cholecystostomy. Tokyo Guidelines (TG18) and IDSA recommend routine bile/tissue cultures for moderate-to-severe (Grade II/III) acute cholecystitis or healthcare-associated cases.
  - Blood Cultures: Routine blood cultures are NOT recommended for mild community-acquired cholecystitis. Blood cultures ARE mandatory for moderate-to-severe cholecystitis, immunocompromised hosts, severely toxic patients, or those with septic shock.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Radionuclide Cholescintigraphy (HIDA Scan) showing non-visualization of the gallbladder due to cystic duct obstruction represents the functional imaging gold standard diagnostic test (while histopathologic confirmation of transmural neutrophilic inflammation post-cholecystectomy represents the pathologic gold standard).

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Medical Stabilization: Hospital admission, strict NPO (nothing by mouth) status, intravenous fluid resuscitation with balanced crystalloids to correct volume depletion, correction of electrolyte imbalances, and antiemetics.
  - Analgesia & Anti-inflammatory Therapy: Intravenous nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs, e.g., ketorolac or tenoxicam) to inhibit prostaglandin synthesis (reducing gallbladder intraluminal pressure and smooth muscle spasm), or IV opioids (meperidine, fentanyl) for severe pain.
  - Stratification of Severity (Tokyo Guidelines TG18): Stratified into Grade I (Mild; uncomplicated), Grade II (Moderate; marked leukocytosis >18,000, palpable mass, duration >72 hours, or severe local inflammation), and Grade III (Severe; accompanied by organ dysfunction).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Role of Antibiotics in Uncomplicated Cholecystitis: Uncomplicated mild cholecystitis can remit spontaneously; randomized trials demonstrate that postoperative antibiotics can be safely discontinued within 24 hours after successful cholecystectomy for acute uncomplicated cholecystitis without increasing infection risk. Antibiotics are mandatory for complicated, severe, elderly, diabetic, immunocompromised, or conservatively managed patients.
  - Mild-to-Moderate Community-Acquired Acute Cholecystitis (Tokyo Guidelines TG18 / IDSA Regimens):
    1. Cephalosporin-Based Monotherapy or Combination: Cefazolin 1 to 2 g IV q8h OR Cefuroxime 1.5 g IV q8h OR Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h. (Metronidazole addition is not routinely required for mild community-acquired cases unless a biliary-enteric anastomosis is present).
    2. Beta-Lactam / Beta-Lactamase Inhibitor Combinations: Ampicillin-sulbactam 1.5 to 3 g IV q6h (where E. coli resistance is low) OR Piperacillin-tazobactam 3.375 g IV q6h.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Regimens for Severe, Healthcare-Associated, Post-Biliary-Anastomosis, or High-Risk Cholecystitis:
    1. Single-Agent Carbapenems: Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Imipenem-cilastatin 500 mg IV q6h OR Doripenem 500 mg IV q8h OR Ertapenem 1 g IV q24h.
    2. Combination Regimens: Cefepime 2 g IV q8-12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h.
    3. Addition of Antienterococcal / Anti-MRSA Coverage: Add Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h (or Linezolid / Daptomycin if VRE risk) for healthcare-associated cases, severe illness, liver transplant recipients, or known VRE colonization.
  - Duration of Antimicrobial Therapy: Discontinue within 24 hours post-cholecystectomy for acute uncomplicated cholecystitis. Continue for a total of 4 to 7 days for complicated cholecystitis (gangrene, perforation) once source control is established.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Cholecystectomy (Definitive Curative Treatment): Surgical removal of the gallbladder, performed via Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy (preferred method) or Open Cholecystectomy. Early Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy (performed within 24 to 72 hours of admission or within 7 days of symptom onset) is superior to delayed surgery, significantly reducing total hospital stay, complication rates, and healthcare costs without increasing conversion rates.
  - Percutaneous Cholecystostomy (Tube Drainage): Indicated for critically ill, unstable, severely septic, or elderly high-surgical-risk patients (especially those with acute acalculous cholecystitis) who cannot tolerate immediate general anesthesia. Ultrasound- or CT-guided placement of a percutaneous drainage catheter into the gallbladder lumen decompresses the gallbladder, drains infected purulent bile, and serves as a lifesaving temporizing or definitive measure.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Gangrenous Cholecystitis (ischemic wall necrosis occurring in 10% to 15% of cases).
  - Gallbladder Perforation: Occurs in 2% to 12% of cases, leading to localized pericholecystic abscess, free secondary peritonitis, or cholecystenteric fistula formation.
  - Gallbladder Empyema (suppurative infection filling the gallbladder with frank pus).
  - Emphysematous Cholecystitis (intra-mural gas formation with high mortality).
  - Pyogenic Liver Abscess (secondary to direct invasion or hematogenous seeding).
  - Sepsis, Bacteremia, and Septic Shock.
  - Cholecystenteric Fistula and Gallstone Ileus: Erosion of a large gallstone through the gallbladder wall into the adjacent duodenum or colon, causing mechanical small bowel obstruction (gallstone ileus, classic Rigler triad).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Post-cholecystectomy Syndrome (persistent right upper quadrant abdominal pain or dyspepsia following gallbladder removal).
  - Chronic Cholecystitis and Porcelain Gallbladder (calcification of the gallbladder wall secondary to chronic inflammation, associated with increased risk of gallbladder adenocarcinoma).
  - Gallbladder Carcinoma (increased long-term risk in chronic Salmonella Typhi carriers and porcelain gallbladders).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Murphy Sign: Inhibition of deep inspiration by sudden severe pain upon palpation of the right upper quadrant over the gallbladder fossa.
  - Sonographic Murphy Sign: Direct pressure of the ultrasound transducer over the visualized gallbladder eliciting maximal tenderness.
  - HIDA Scan Non-Visualization: Failure of radiotracer accumulation in the gallbladder within 60 to 120 minutes confirming cystic duct obstruction.
  - Acalculous Cholecystitis: Gallbladder inflammation without gallstones in critically ill, burned, or traumatized patients.
  - Emphysematous Cholecystitis: Intramural gas on imaging in diabetic males caused by Clostridium species or E. coli.
  - Rigler Triad: Pneumobilia, small bowel obstruction, and ectopic gallstone in gallstone ileus.
  - Porcelain Gallbladder: Extensive calcification of the gallbladder wall secondary to chronic cholecystitis.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Postoperative Antibiotic Discontinuation Within 24 Hours: Antimicrobial therapy can be safely stopped within 24 hours post-cholecystectomy for acute uncomplicated calculous cholecystitis; extending postoperative antibiotics offers no clinical benefit.
  - Bilirubin Elevation Without Duct Obstruction: Up to 25% of patients with acute cholecystitis WITHOUT common bile duct stones exhibit elevated serum bilirubin (up to 2 to 4 mg/dL) due to transient sludge passage, direct liver inflammation, or Mirizzi syndrome.
  - Enterococcal Coverage Omission in Mild Cases: Despite Enterococcus species being isolated from bile in up to 34% of cases, empiric antienterococcal antibiotics are UNNECESSARY for community-acquired mild-to-moderate cholecystitis (clearing primary pathogens resolves infection), but ARE MANDATORY in liver transplant recipients or healthcare-associated cases.
  - Metronidazole Omission in Mild Cases: Empiric anaerobic coverage (metronidazole) is NOT required for routine mild community-acquired cholecystitis unless a biliary-enteric anastomosis is present or gangrene/perforation is suspected.
  - Early vs Delayed Surgery Rule: Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy (within 24-72 hours of admission) is superior to delaying surgery for 6 to 12 weeks, lowering complication rates, hospital stay, and cost.
