# Pancreatitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Acute pancreatitis is an acute inflammatory condition of the pancreas characterized by severe upper abdominal pain, elevated pancreatic enzymes, and autodigestion of pancreatic parenchyma by prematurely activated proteolytic digestive enzymes, with variable local peripancreatic or remote organ system involvement. Pancreatic infection is a major, life-threatening complication of acute pancreatitis that develops in tissue damaged by ischemic necrosis, or less commonly from direct primary microbial invasion of the gland.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Morphologic / Atlanta Classification (1992, Revised 2012):
    - Interstitial Edematous Pancreatitis: Acute inflammatory swelling of the pancreas without parenchymal necrosis; accounts for 80% of acute pancreatitis cases and usually resolves with supportive care.
    - Necrotizing Pancreatitis: Inflammation associated with pancreatic parenchymal necrosis, peripancreatic necrosis, or both; accounts for 15% to 20% of acute pancreatitis cases.
    - Acute Peripancreatic Fluid Collection (APFC): Non-encapsulated peripancreatic fluid collection occurring within the first 4 weeks of interstitial edematous pancreatitis without peripancreatic necrosis.
    - Acute Necrotic Collection (ANC): Collection containing variable amounts of both fluid and necrosis associated with necrotizing pancreatitis during the first 4 weeks of illness.
    - Pancreatic Pseudocyst: Encapsulated collection of fluid with a well-defined inflammatory wall usually outside the pancreas with minimal or no necrosis, occurring more than 4 weeks after onset of interstitial edematous pancreatitis.
    - Walled-Off Necrosis (WON): Mature, encapsulated collection of pancreatic and/or peripancreatic necrosis that has developed a well-defined inflammatory wall, occurring more than 4 weeks after onset of necrotizing pancreatitis.
  - Infectious Complications Classifications:
    - Infected Pancreatic Necrosis: Bacterial or fungal superinfection of necrotic pancreatic or peripancreatic tissue, occurring in up to 30% to 70% of patients with necrotizing pancreatitis.
    - Pancreatic Abscess: Circumscribed collection of pus in or near the pancreas with minimal or no necrotic tissue, usually arising more than 4 to 6 weeks after acute pancreatitis or trauma (associated with lower mortality of 12% to 22% compared to 26% to 32% for infected necrosis).
    - Primary Pancreatic Infection: Direct microbial infection of the pancreas by specific viruses, bacteria, fungi, or parasites without antecedent noninfectious acute pancreatitis.
  - Clinical Severity Categories: Mild Acute Pancreatitis (absence of organ failure and absence of local or systemic complications), Moderately Severe Acute Pancreatitis (presence of transient organ failure lasting less than 48 hours or local/systemic complications), and Severe / Critical Acute Pancreatitis (persistent organ failure lasting more than 48 hours, frequently accompanied by infected pancreatic necrosis).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Disease Burden & Frequency: Approximately 275,000 hospital admissions for acute pancreatitis occur annually in the United States. Up to 15% to 20% of cases develop significant pancreatic necrosis (approximately 40,000 to 55,000 cases), and up to 30% to 70% of patients with pancreatic necrosis develop secondary pancreatic infection.
  - Etiologic Risk Factors for Acute Pancreatitis: Gallstones (cholelithiasis / choledocholithiasis, the leading cause accounting for 40% to 50%) and alcohol abuse (the leading cause of chronic and recurrent pancreatitis, 30% to 40%). Other causes include hypertriglyceridemia, hypercalcemia, ERCP procedures, abdominal trauma, anatomic variants (pancreas divisum), autoimmune pancreatitis, pancreatic carcinoma, ischemia, and drugs (including antimicrobials such as tetracyclines, pentamidine, sulfonamides, didanosine, erythromycin, nitrofurantoin, and metronidazole).
  - Risk Factors for Secondary Pancreatic Infection: Extent of pancreatic necrosis (infection rate is less than 10% with under 30% gland necrosis, but rises to over 40% to 70% with greater than 50% necrosis); duration of necrosis (highest risk between 2 and 4 weeks into illness, peaking at 15 to 21 days); presence of persistent organ failure / SIRS; impaired mucosal gut barrier; parenteral nutrition; and invasive procedural interventions. Advanced HIV/AIDS is associated with opportunistic pancreatic infections (CMV, Mycobacterium avium, Pneumocystis jirovecii) and drug-induced pancreatitis.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Gastrointestinal Bacterial Microbiota (translocating from the gut): Polymicrobial in up to 30% to 50% of infected necrosis cases.
  - Aerobic Gram-Negative Bacilli: Escherichia coli (the single most common gram-negative isolate), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter species, Proteus species, and Citrobacter species. (Biliary pancreatitis shows a higher proportion of enteric gram-negative bacilli than alcoholic pancreatitis).
  - Gram-Positive Cocci: Enterococcus faecalis, Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Coagulase-negative staphylococci (Staphylococcus epidermidis, frequently associated with central venous catheters or hospital-acquired contamination), Streptococcus species, and Viridans group streptococci.
  - Obligate Anaerobes: Bacteroides fragilis, Peptostreptococcus species, and Clostridium species (isolated in 10% to 20% of infected necrosis cases).
  - Fungi: Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, Candida tropicalis, and Candida krusei (fungal superinfection occurs in 10% to 36% of severe necrotizing pancreatitis cases, often selected by broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children: Viral etiologies (Mumps virus, Coxsackievirus B, Varicella-zoster virus, Epstein-Barr virus), Ascaris lumbricoides (biliary/pancreatic ascariasis causing mechanical ductal obstruction), blunt abdominal trauma, and congenital structural anomalies.
  - Adults & Elderly: Enteric gram-negative bacilli (E. coli, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas), Enterococcus faecalis, and anaerobes secondary to gallstones, alcohol, or invasive biliary procedures.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Viruses (Definite or Probable Direct Causes of Acute Pancreatitis): Coxsackievirus B (serotypes B3, B4), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Varicella-zoster virus (VZV), Herpes simplex virus 2 (HSV-2), Mumps virus, Hepatitis B virus (HBV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), Rubella virus, Rubeola virus, Adenovirus, and Vaccinia virus.
  - Bacteria & Fastidious Organisms: Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Leptospira interrogans, Legionella pneumophila, Salmonella enterica serotype Typhi, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Mycobacterium avium-intracellulare complex (MAC), Actinomyces israelii, and Nocardia asteroides.
  - Fungi: Aspergillus species, Cryptococcus neoformans, Histoplasma capsulatum, Coccidioides immitis, Paracoccidioides brasiliensis, and Mucormycosis.
  - Parasites & Protozoa: Toxoplasma gondii, Cryptosporidium parvum, Ascaris lumbricoides (adult worm invasion into the pancreatic duct), Clonorchis sinensis, Entamoeba histolytica, Echinococcus granulosus, Leishmania donovani, Paragonimus westermani, Schistosoma haematobium, and Strongyloides stercoralis.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Initial Enzyme Activation & Microvascular Injury: Noninfectious insult (gallstone obstruction, ethanol toxicity) causes premature intra-acinar zymogen activation (conversion of trypsinogen to trypsin mediated by cathepsin B). Active proteolytic enzymes (trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase, phospholipase A2) and vasoactive kinins cause acinar cell injury, autodigestion, and microvascular ischemia. In 15% to 20% of cases, microvascular thrombosis leads to pancreatic parenchymal and peripancreatic fat necrosis.
  - Intestinal Barrier Breakdown & Bacterial Translocation: Ischemia and hypovolemia during early acute pancreatitis impair the intestinal mucosal epithelial barrier, increasing gut permeability and enterocyte damage. Endogenous gut microbiota translocate across the compromised mucosal barrier into mesenteric lymph nodes, portal circulation, or peritoneal cavity, hematogenously or lymphatically seeding devitalized necrotic pancreatic tissue.
  - Timing & Maturation of Infection: Bacterial contamination of necrotic tissue is rare in the first 48 hours, present in approximately 24% within 1 week, 36% within 2 weeks, and peaks at 71% between 15 and 21 days (weeks 2 to 4). Liquefied sterile necrosis can mature into pseudocysts (after 4 weeks), while infected necrosis matures into walled-off necrosis (WON) or pancreatic abscesses.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Inflammatory Cascade & SIRS: Local tissue injury triggers massive recruitment of neutrophils and monocytes, releasing TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, platelet-activating factor (PAF), and PMN elastase. This drives Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS), causing capillary leak, third-space fluid sequestration into retroperitoneum and peritoneum, hypovolemic shock, ARDS, acute kidney injury, and DIC.
  - Double Burden on Mortality: Early mortality (within 1 to 2 weeks) is driven by SIRS, severe hypovolemia, and multiorgan failure (MODS). Late mortality (after 2 to 4 weeks) is driven by infected pancreatic necrosis, retroperitoneal phlegmon, secondary sepsis, and late organ failure. When organ failure AND infected pancreatic necrosis coexist, mortality risk doubles (reaching 40% to 67%).

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Severe Abdominal Pain: Abrupt onset of persistent, severe, agonizing epigastric or periumbilical pain that characteristically radiates straight through to the back (in 50% of cases). Pain reaches peak intensity within 30 to 60 minutes, is exacerbated by lying supine or coughing, and is partially relieved by leaning forward with knees flexed.
  - Associated Gastrointestinal Symptoms: Nausea, profuse vomiting (which does not relieve the pain), anorexia, abdominal distention, and obstipation or paralytic ileus.
  - Clinical Manifestations of Pancreatic Infection: Develops 2 to 4 weeks after initial pancreatitis onset in a patient who initially stabilized or deteriorated, manifesting with secondary temperature spiking / hectic fever (38.5°C to 40°C), rigors, worsening abdominal pain, leukocytosis, tachycardia, re-emergence of organ failure, or a palpable tender retroperitoneal mass.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Epigastric Tenderness & Guarding: Marked tenderness to palpation over the epigastrium and upper abdomen; voluntary guarding; distended abdomen with hypoactive or absent bowel sounds (paralytic ileus). Peritoneal signs (rigidity/rebound) occur if free fluid or secondary peritonitis develops.
  - Vital Signs & Systemic Signs: Fever (38°C to 39°C), tachycardia, tachypnea, hypotension, and scleral icterus (in 20% to 30% of cases, indicating biliary etiology or common duct compression).
  - Hemorrhagic / Ecchymotic Signs (Rare, less than 1% to 3%, indicating severe necrotizing pancreatitis with retroperitoneal hemorrhage):
    - Cullen Sign: Blue-purple ecchymosis around the umbilicus.
    - Grey Turner Sign: Blue-red-brown discoloration along the flanks.
    - Fox Sign: Ecchymosis over the anterior thigh or inguinal ligament.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Severe Constant Epigastric Pain Radiating to Back + 3-Fold Serum Amylase/Lipase Elevation: Classic diagnostic clinical combination for acute pancreatitis.
  - Gas Bubbles within Pancreatic Necrosis on CT: Pathognomonic imaging feature confirming infected pancreatic necrosis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Pancreatic Enzymes (Core Diagnostic Hallmark): Serum lipase (more specific and remains elevated longer, 8 to 14 days) and serum amylase elevated greater than 3 times the upper limit of normal within 6 to 12 hours of symptom onset.
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis (12,000 to 20,000 cells/uL or higher) with a neutrophilic left shift. Hemoconcentration (hematocrit greater than 44%) on admission is an independent risk factor for pancreatic necrosis.
  - Inflammatory Biomarkers: Serum C-reactive protein (CRP greater than 150 mg/L at 48 hours indicates severe necrotizing pancreatitis; CRP less than 81 mg/L and WBC less than 13x10^9/L indicates a 1.4% risk of infected necrosis). Elevated serum procalcitonin (PCT greater than 0.5 to 2.0 ng/mL) is a promising marker for predicting infected pancreatic necrosis.
  - Metabolic Profile & Organ Function: Elevated BUN (greater than 20 to 25 mg/dL) and creatinine (indicates renal failure and mortality risk); hypocalcemia; hyperglycemia; elevated AST/ALT (greater than 3-fold elevation of ALT >150 U/L has a 95% positive predictive value for gallstone pancreatitis); hyperbilirubinemia; and arterial blood gas showing hypoxemia (PaO2 less than 60 mmHg) or metabolic acidosis.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Contrast-Enhanced Computed Tomography (CECT) of Abdomen (Diagnostic Imaging Modality of Choice): Performed after 72 hours of symptom onset (early CT before 48 to 72 hours may underestimate extent of necrosis). Demonstrates pancreatic parenchymal non-enhancement (perfusion defect indicating necrosis), peripancreatic fluid collections (APFC, ANC), pseudocysts, or walled-off necrosis (WON).
  - Gas Within Pancreatic Necrosis on CT: Detection of extraluminal gas bubbles within necrotic tissue or peripancreatic space is highly specific for infected pancreatic necrosis.
  - Transabdominal Ultrasonography: First-line imaging on admission to detect gallstones, choledocholithiasis, or biliary ductal dilation.
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) / MRCP: Superior for visualizing non-enhancing debris within fluid collections, characterization of WON versus pseudocysts, and detecting choledocholithiasis.
  - PET/CT with 18F-FDG Labeled Leukocytes: Non-invasive nuclear imaging method helpful in distinguishing infected from uninfected collections.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - CT-Guided Fine-Needle Aspiration (FNA) & Culture: CT-guided FNA of necrotic tissue or fluid collections with Gram stain, aerobic, and anaerobic bacterial/fungal cultures provides definitive diagnostic proof of infected pancreatic necrosis (high sensitivity and specificity with minimal risk of contamination). Indicated for patients with necrotizing acute pancreatitis and persistent fever, SIRS, or organ failure after 7 to 14 days. (Recent guidelines suggest FNA can be omitted if gas is present on CT or if step-up catheter drainage is planned).
  - Blood Cultures: Mandatory prior to antibiotic therapy in toxic/septic patients to detect secondary bacteremia or central line sepsis.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** CT-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA) yielding purulent aspirate with positive Gram stain or bacterial/fungal culture (or gas bubbles in necrotic tissue on contrast-enhanced CT) in a patient with acute necrotizing pancreatitis represents the gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Aggressive Fluid Resuscitation: Immediate, goal-directed IV fluid hydration with balanced crystalloids (Lactated Ringer's preferred over normal saline at 200 to 500 mL/h or 2,500 to 4,000 mL in the first 24 hours) to restore intravascular volume, maintain microvascular perfusion, and prevent necrosis progression.
  - Early Enteral Nutrition (Core Prevention & Supportive Standard): Enteral nutrition via nasogastric (NG) or nasojejunal (NJ) tube started within 24 to 72 hours of admission is superior to total parenteral nutrition (TPN), significantly reducing mucosal permeability, bacterial translocation, infectious complications, surgical interventions, and mortality.
  - Sepsis & Organ Support: Intensive care unit (ICU) admission for severe acute pancreatitis; mechanical ventilation for ARDS; vasopressors (norepinephrine) for shock; hemodynamic monitoring; analgesia (IV opioids); and avoidance of routine prophylactic probiotics (probiotics in severe acute pancreatitis increased bowel ischemia and mortality in the PROPATRIA trial).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Policy Against Routine Preemptive Antibiotics: Modern guidelines (ACG, IAP/APA, Canadian, IDSA) strongly advise AGAINST routine prophylactic/preemptive antibiotic administration in mild or severe acute pancreatitis, as randomized controlled trials (Dellinger et al., Isenmann et al.) showed no reduction in infected necrosis or mortality. Antibiotics ARE indicated ONLY for confirmed or strongly suspected infected necrosis (gas on CT, positive FNA, or persistent SIRS/toxicity after 7 to 14 days), or extrapancreatic infections (cholangitis, bacteremia, UTI).
  - Empiric Regimens for Confirmed / Suspected Infected Pancreatic Necrosis (Must use agents with proven pancreatic tissue penetration and activity against enteric gram-negative bacilli and anaerobes):
    1. Carbapenem Monotherapy (Highest Pancreatic Penetration Efficacy Factor ~0.98): Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Imipenem-cilastatin 500 mg IV q6h OR Ertapenem 1 g IV q24h.
    2. Fluoroquinolone-Based Combination Regimens: Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h.
    3. Cephalosporin / Beta-Lactam Combination Regimens: Cefepime 2 g IV q8-12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Alternative & Antifungal Regimens:
    - Addition of Targeted Antifungal Therapy: Fluconazole 400 mg IV/PO daily (loading dose 800 mg) OR Echinocandins (Caspofungin 50 mg IV daily, Micafungin 100 mg IV daily) for confirmed Candida superinfection in necrotic collections. Routine prophylactic antifungals are NOT recommended.
    - Addition of Anti-MRSA / Antienterococcal Coverage: Add Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h or Linezolid 600 mg IV q12h for suspected healthcare-associated MRSA or VRE superinfection.
  - Duration of Antibiotic Therapy: 14 to 21 days for infected pancreatic necrosis, adjusted based on clinical response, repeat imaging, and source control.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Minimally Invasive "Step-Up" Approach (Current Gold Standard Interventional Strategy):
    - Delayed Intervention Paradigm: Defer procedural intervention (catheter drainage or necrosectomy) for at least 4 weeks until necrotic collections mature into encapsulated walled-off necrosis (WON), allowing better demarcation and reducing surgical hemorrhage and mortality.
    - Step 1: Image-Guided Percutaneous (CT/US) or Endoscopic Transgastric Catheter Drainage of infected fluid collection. Catheter drainage alone successfully resolves infected necrosis in 35% to 50% of patients without requiring necrosectomy.
    - Step 2: If no clinical improvement after 72 hours, step-up to minimally invasive necrosectomy: Video-Assisted Retroperitoneal Débridement (VARD), Percutaneous Endoscopic Necrosectomy, or Endoscopic Transgastric Necrosectomy.
  - Open Surgical Necrosectomy: Reserved as a last resort for failed minimally invasive step-up interventions or acute abdominal catastrophes (free perforation, massive retroperitoneal bleeding).

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Infected Pancreatic Necrosis and Walled-Off Necrosis (WON).
  - Sepsis, Septic Shock, and Multiorgan Dysfunction Syndrome (MODS involving pulmonary/ARDS, renal failure, cardiovascular collapse).
  - Gastrointestinal Bleeding and Retroperitoneal Hemorrhage (secondary to splenic artery or gastroduodenal artery pseudoaneurysm erosion or rupture).
  - Splenic / Portal / Superior Mesenteric Vein Thrombosis (pylephlebitis) and Variceal Bleeding.
  - Pancreatic Ascites and Pancreatic Pleural Effusions (secondary to pancreatic duct disruption).
  - Abdominal Compartment Syndrome (increased intraabdominal pressure greater than 20 mmHg causing organ dysfunction).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Exocrine Pancreatic Insufficiency (steatorrhea, malabsorption, weight loss requiring pancreatic enzyme replacement).
  - Endocrine Pancreatic Insufficiency / Pancreatogenic Diabetes Mellitus (Type 3c diabetes due to islet cell loss).
  - Pancreatic Pseudocysts and External Pancreatic Cutaneous Fistulas (frequently complicating percutaneous drainage).
  - Chronic Pancreatitis and Recurrent Acute Pancreatitis (especially in uncorrected gallstones or ongoing alcohol intake).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Atlanta Classification (1992, Revised 2012): International consensus system defining pancreatic collections (APFC, ANC, Pseudocyst, WON).
  - Step-Up Approach: Minimally invasive strategy starting with catheter drainage followed by VARD or endoscopic necrosectomy.
  - Gas Within Necrosis: CT hallmark pathognomonic for infected pancreatic necrosis.
  - Walled-Off Necrosis (WON): Encapsulated necrotic collection maturing greater than 4 weeks post-acute pancreatitis.
  - Cullen Sign & Grey Turner Sign: Periumbilical and flank ecchymosis indicating retroperitoneal necrotizing hemorrhage.
  - PROPATRIA Trial: Famous Dutch probiotic study showing increased bowel ischemia and mortality from prophylactic probiotics.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Preemptive Antibiotic Prophylaxis Disproval: Despite widespread historical practice in the 1990s and 2000s, multiple double-blind RCTs (Dellinger et al.) and meta-analyses proved that routine prophylactic antibiotics DO NOT reduce infected necrosis or mortality in acute pancreatitis and are STRICTLY NOT RECOMMENDED.
  - Delayed Surgery Rule (Greater Than 4 Weeks): Immediate surgical necrosectomy in early acute pancreatitis (under 2 to 3 weeks) carries extremely high mortality (30% to 50%); delaying intervention until greater than 4 weeks when necrosis becomes walled-off (WON) markedly improves survival and reduces bleeding.
  - Enteral Nutrition Superiority over Parenteral: Early enteral nutrition via NG or NJ tube within 24 to 72 hours reduces bacterial translocation and infectious complications compared to total parenteral nutrition (TPN).
  - Probiotic Mortality Warning: Probiotic prophylaxis in predicted severe acute pancreatitis is STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED due to high risks of splanchnic ischemia, bowel necrosis, and death.
  - Catheter Drainage Success Without Surgery: Up to 35% to 50% of infected pancreatic necrosis cases can be completely cured by image-guided percutaneous or endoscopic catheter drainage ALONE without surgical necrosectomy.
