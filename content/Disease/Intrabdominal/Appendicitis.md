# Appendicitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Appendicitis is an acute inflammation of the vermiform appendix, a tubular structure arising 2 to 3 cm below the terminal ileum along the medial posterior wall of the cecum. It is often initiated or accompanied by luminal obstruction or microbial dysbiosis, and can progress to ischemic necrosis, gangrene, perforation, peritonitis, or intraabdominal abscess formation.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Uncomplicated (Simple) Acute Appendicitis: Inflammation confined to the appendiceal wall without gangrene, perforation, or periappendiceal phlegmon/abscess.
  - Complicated Acute Appendicitis: Inflammation accompanied by gangrene, perforation (free or contained), periappendiceal phlegmon, walled-off periappendiceal abscess, or secondary peritonitis.
  - Chronic / Recurrent / Subacute Appendicitis: Indolent or episodic right lower quadrant abdominal pain associated with chronic inflammation, often mimicking or related to granulomatous processes (such as tuberculosis, actinomycosis, histoplasmosis, or Crohn disease).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Incidence and Lifetime Risk: Appendectomy is among the most common surgical emergencies of the abdomen, with more than 300,000 (or over 330,000 nonincidental) appendectomies performed annually in the United States, accounting for over 1 million inpatient hospital days. The lifetime risk for appendicitis is 8.6% in men and 6.7% in women (overall male-to-female rate ratio of 1.4 to 1).
  - Age Distribution: Appendicitis is rare in infants, steadily increases through childhood, reaches a peak incidence at 15 to 25 years of age, and declines through adulthood (less than 25% of cases occur in individuals older than 45 years).
  - Risk Factors: Low dietary fiber Western diet (thought to alter stool transit time or intraluminal pressure); fecalith formation; familial susceptibility or genetic predisposition (history of appendicitis in first-degree relatives); lymphoid hyperplasia (triggered by viral or bacterial enteric infections); and physical luminal blockage by foreign bodies, parasites, strictures, or tumors (such as cecal adenocarcinoma, carcinoid tumor, or lymphoma).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Polymicrobial Infection: Conventional cultures of inflamed, gangrenous, or perforated appendices typically yield 10 to 14 different organisms per specimen, representing a complex mixture of colonic facultative and obligate anaerobic bacteria.
  - Facultative Gram-Negative Bacilli: Escherichia coli (the single most common facultative isolate), Klebsiella species, Enterobacter species, Proteus species, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (recovered in 4% to 15% of cases).
  - Obligate Anaerobes: Bacteroides fragilis (the most common anaerobic isolate), non-fragilis Bacteroides species, Prevotella species (including pigmented Prevotella species and Prevotella melaninogenica), Fusobacterium species (notably enriched in molecular studies), Bilophila wadsworthia (found in one-third of gangrenous and half of perforated cases), Peptostreptococcus species, Parvimonas species, Eubacterium species, and Clostridium species (including C. perfringens).
  - Aerobic and Microaerophilic Streptococci: Streptococcus anginosus group (viridans streptococci) and Enterococcus species.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children and Adolescents: Polymicrobial flora dominated by E. coli, Bacteroides fragilis, Fusobacterium species (such as Fusobacterium nucleatum and Fusobacterium necrophorum, which are markedly expanded in pediatric appendicitis microbiome studies), Peptostreptococcus species, and Parvimonas species. Mesenteric lymphadenitis / pseudoappendicitis mimicking appendicitis in children is frequently caused by Yersinia enterocolitica, Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, Campylobacter jejuni, nontyphoidal Salmonella enterica, Adenovirus, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), Parvovirus B19, and Measles virus.
  - Adults and Elderly: Polymicrobial colonic flora including E. coli, B. fragilis, Klebsiella species, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and enterococci. Older adults have a higher risk of appendiceal obstruction secondary to cecal adenocarcinoma, carcinoid tumors, or fecaliths.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Fastidious / Granulomatous Bacteria: Yersinia enterocolitica and Yersinia pseudotuberculosis (can directly cause true granulomatous appendicitis as well as ileocecitis/mesenteric adenitis); Mycobacterium tuberculosis (appendiceal/ileocecal TB); Actinomyces israelii (appendiceal actinomycosis causing chronic indurated masses and sinus tracts).
  - Parasites: Entamoeba histolytica (amebic appendicitis/colitis and ameboma); Enterobius vermicularis (pinworm causing luminal mechanical obstruction); Ascaris lumbricoides; Taenia species; Schistosoma species (schistosomiasis causing bilharzioma); and Angiostrongylus costaricensis.
  - Viruses (in Immunocompromised Hosts): Cytomegalovirus (CMV appendicitis, particularly in AIDS patients or organ transplant recipients); Epstein-Barr virus (EBV); Adenovirus; Parvovirus B19; and Measles virus.
  - Fungi: Histoplasma capsulatum and Candida species.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Classic Luminal Obstruction Hypothesis: The appendix typically measures 5 to 10 cm in length and arises 2 to 3 cm below the terminal ileum. Physical obstruction of the narrow appendiceal lumen by a fecalith (inspissated fecal mass), lymphoid hyperplasia (secondary to viral or bacterial infection), foreign bodies, strictures, or neoplasms leads to continuous accumulation of intraluminal mucus secreted by appendiceal goblet cells.
  - Pressure Elevation, Ischemia, and Microbial Invasion: As intraluminal pressure rises above venous and lymphatic pressure, lymphatic and venous drainage becomes compromised, causing mucosal ischemia, congestion, and breakdown of the mucosal epithelial barrier. Endogenous commensal colonic bacteria inside the appendiceal lumen invade the ischemic appendiceal wall. Unchecked tissue ischemia and bacterial multiplication lead to transmural gangrene, necrosis, and ultimate appendiceal perforation.
  - Modern Microbial Dysbiosis Hypothesis: Recent studies demonstrate that fecaliths are present in only a minority of cases, and 16S rRNA sequencing reveals that acute appendicitis is strongly associated with an appendiceal dysbiosis (enrichment of oral commensals and periodontal pathogens, particularly Fusobacterium, Peptostreptococcus, and Parvimonas), suggesting primary microbial invasion or inflammatory dysregulation without requiring physical obstruction.
  - Normal Appendiceal Physiology and Reservoir Function: The healthy appendix houses a diverse commensal microbiome residing within a mucus-rich biofilm (shedding ~3 mL per day) that serves as a protected reservoir to repopulate the gut after acute diarrheal illnesses and supports gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) immune maturation.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Transmural Neutrophilic Infiltration: The pathologic hallmark of acute appendicitis is the presence of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (neutrophils) infiltrating the muscularis propria and mucosa of the appendiceal wall, accompanied by severe mural edema, mucosal ulceration, microvascular thrombosis, and suppuration.
  - Peritonitis and Abscess Progression: Once perforation occurs, bacterial spillage into the peritoneal cavity produces localized phlegmon, periappendiceal abscess (commonly in the right lower quadrant, pelvis, or subphrenic space), or diffuse secondary peritonitis. B. fragilis and E. coli act synergistically: E. coli drives early mortality via endotoxemia/bacteremia, while B. fragilis and capsular polysaccharides stimulate IL-17-producing CD4+ T cells to drive intraabdominal abscess formation.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Migrating Abdominal Pain: Visceral periumbilical or epigastric colicky pain that gradually evolves over 6 to 24 hours into constant, localized somatic right lower quadrant (RLQ) abdominal pain as inflammation spreads to involve the parietal peritoneum. (Migration of pain to the RLQ is the single most predictive symptom of acute appendicitis).
  - Associated Gastrointestinal Symptoms: Anorexia (loss of appetite is almost universally present), nausea, vomiting (typically follows the onset of pain, not preceding it), mild fever, and constipation or mild diarrhea.
  - Atypical Pain Locations Based on Anatomical Variations:
    - Retrocecal Appendix (most common variant): Pain remains poorly localized or dull, migration to RLQ is delayed/absent, and flank or back pain may predominate.
    - Pelvic Appendix: Pain is localized to the pelvis, suprapubic region, or left lower quadrant, often accompanied by urinary frequency, dysuria, or tenesmus due to bladder/rectal irritation.
    - Third-Trimester Pregnancy or Malrotation: Pain is displaced upward to the right upper quadrant or right flank due to displacement of the cecum by the gravid uterus.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - McBurney Point Tenderness: Maximal tenderness on localized palpation over McBurney point (located two to three fingerbreadths above the right anterior superior iliac spine on a line joining the umbilicus).
  - Peritoneal Signs & Classical Eponymous Signs:
    - Guarding and Involuntary Muscle Rigidity in the right lower quadrant.
    - Rebound Tenderness: Localized pain upon sudden release of deep abdominal pressure.
    - Rovsing Sign: Pain elicited in the right lower quadrant during deep palpation of the left lower quadrant.
    - Psoas Sign: Right lower quadrant pain elicited by passive hyperextension of the right hip (indicates an inflamed retrocecal appendix irritating the psoas muscle).
    - Obturator Sign: Right lower quadrant pain elicited by passive internal rotation of the flexed right hip (indicates an inflamed pelvic appendix irritating the internal obturator muscle).
    - Palpable Right Lower Quadrant Mass: Indicates a walled-off periappendiceal phlegmon or abscess, or underlying cecal carcinoma.
  - Signs in Special Populations: Elderly patients often present with muted or generalized pain, lack of fever, longer symptom duration, and higher rates of early perforation. Children under 5 years may present with irritability, lethargy, vomiting, and high fever.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Migrating Periumbilical Pain to McBurney Point with Localized Guarding: Classic, highly predictive clinical sequence for acute appendicitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Mild to moderate leukocytosis (typically 11,000 to 16,000 cells/uL) with a neutrophilic predominance and a left shift (bandemia >10%). Marked leukocytosis (>18,000/uL) suggests appendiceal gangrene or perforation.
  - Inflammatory Markers: Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP). Elevated procalcitonin (PCT) is not routine for diagnosis but strongly suggests complicated appendicitis (gangrene or perforation).
  - Urinalysis: Usually normal, but mild sterile pyuria or microscopic hematuria may occur if inflammation abuts the right ureter or bladder.
  - Serum Pregnancy Test (beta-hCG): MANDATORY in all women of childbearing age to rule out ectopic pregnancy or intrauterine pregnancy.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Computed Tomography (CT) of Abdomen/Pelvis (Diagnostic Modality of Choice in Adults): Overall diagnostic accuracy >95% (sensitivity 94%-98%, specificity 95%-98%). Key CT findings include an enlarged appendiceal diameter >6 mm with a blind-ending tubular structure, appendiceal wall thickening and hyperenhancement, periappendiceal fat stranding, appendicolith / fecalith (calcified intraluminal mass), appendiceal wall gas or defect (perforation), and localized fluid collection or phlegmon.
  - Ultrasonography (Modalities of Choice in Children, Pregnant Women, and Young Females): Non-invasive, avoids ionizing radiation. Diagnostic findings on graded compression sonography include a non-compressible, blind-ending tubular structure >6 mm in outer diameter in the right iliac fossa, target sign in cross-section, periappendiceal fluid, and hyperemia on color Doppler. Fails to visualize the appendix in ~10% of cases.
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): Highly accurate (sensitivity 96%-97%, specificity 95%-99%) using rapid unenhanced protocols. Preferred second-line imaging in pregnant women when ultrasound is equivocal, avoiding radiation and contrast.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Intraoperative Peritoneal / Pus Cultures: Routine Gram stain and culture of peritoneal fluid in uncomplicated appendicitis is NOT routinely recommended in community-acquired cases because the flora is predictable and responds well to standard empiric regimens. However, culture and susceptibility testing ARE recommended in high-risk, healthcare-associated, or tertiary peritonitis cases, severe complicated appendicitis, or immunocompromised hosts.
  - Molecular 16S rRNA Sequencing: Research tool identifying unculturable anaerobes and Fusobacterium expansion.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Histopathologic Examination of the resected appendix showing polymorphonuclear neutrophilic infiltration throughout the muscularis propria represents the definitive gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Preoperative Stabilization: Intravenous fluid resuscitation with balanced crystalloids (Lactated Ringer's or Normal Saline) to correct dehydration, correction of electrolyte disturbances, NPO (nothing by mouth) status, antipyretics, and IV analgesics.
  - Stratified Preoperative Decision Algorithm: Clinical risk scoring (e.g., Alvarado score) combined with selective imaging (ultrasound in children/pregnancy, CT in adults) guides decisions between immediate surgery, active observation, or conservative management.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Perioperative Prophylaxis for Acute Uncomplicated Appendicitis: Single preoperative IV dose administered within 60 minutes prior to surgical incision: Cefoxitin 2 g IV OR Cefotetan 2 g IV OR Cefazolin 2 g IV plus Metronidazole 500 mg IV OR Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV. (Postoperative antibiotics are NOT indicated for uncomplicated non-perforated appendicitis).
  - Empiric Regimens for Complicated Appendicitis (Gangrene, Perforation, Abscess, Peritonitis):
    - Mild-to-Moderate Community-Acquired Regimens:
      1. Single-Agent Parenteral: Piperacillin-tazobactam 3.375 g IV q6h OR Ertapenem 1 g IV q24h.
      2. Combination Parenteral: Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Cefotaxime 2 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - High-Risk / Severe / Healthcare-Associated Complicated Appendicitis Regimens:
    - Cefepime 2 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Imipenem-cilastatin 500 mg IV q6h OR Doripenem 500 mg IV q8h OR Aztreonam 2 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h PLUS Vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg IV q8-12h.
  - Duration of Antibiotic Therapy in Complicated Appendicitis: Intravenous therapy for 3 to 5 days postoperatively until fever resolves, leukocytosis normalizes, and bowel function returns (recent RCTs confirm 3 days of postoperative antibiotics is as effective as 5 days).
  - "Antibiotics-First" Primary Nonoperative Strategy for Uncomplicated Appendicitis: Primary antibiotic therapy (e.g., IV ertapenem or ceftriaxone plus metronidazole for 1 to 3 days, followed by oral amoxicillin-clavulanate or ciprofloxacin plus metronidazole to complete 10 days) may be considered in carefully selected adult patients with uncomplicated appendicitis who strongly prefer to avoid surgery or are poor surgical candidates. (Carries a ~27% to 39% recurrence rate requiring appendectomy within 1 to 5 years).
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Appendectomy (Standard Definitive Curative Therapy): Surgical removal of the appendix, performed via Laparoscopic Appendectomy (preferred in most patients, offering reduced wound infections, less pain, shorter hospital stay, and faster recovery) or Open Appendectomy.
  - Percutaneous Image-Guided Catheter Drainage: Indicated for complicated appendicitis presenting with a large, well-defined periappendiceal abscess (>3 to 5 cm). Catheter drainage combined with IV antibiotics stabilizes the patient, allowing an Interval Appendectomy to be performed 6 to 8 weeks later after inflammation subsides (though routine interval appendectomy is increasingly omitted in asymptomatic patients).

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Appendiceal Perforation: Occurs in 20% to 30% of overall cases (higher in elderly >50% and young children).
  - Periappendiceal Phlegmon and Intraabdominal Abscess Formation (right lower quadrant, pelvic, subphrenic, or interloop abscesses).
  - Secondary Diffuse Peritonitis and Septic Shock.
  - Suppurative Portal Vein Thrombophlebitis (Pyelophlebitis) and Pyogenic Liver Abscesses (rare in the modern antibiotic era).
  - Postoperative Wound Infection / Surgical Site Infection (higher in open appendectomy than laparoscopic).
  - Postoperative Intraabdominal Abscess (slightly higher in laparoscopic appendectomy than open).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Late Recurrence of Appendicitis: Occurs in up to 39% of patients treated with an "antibiotics-first" nonoperative approach within 5 years.
  - Adhesive Small Bowel Obstruction (secondary to postoperative peritoneal adhesions).
  - Inadvertent Delay in Diagnosing Appendiceal / Cecal Malignancies (carcinoid tumor, cecal adenocarcinoma, or mucinous cystadenoma discovered on pathology).
  - Protective Immunologic Association: Appendectomy is paradoxically associated with a long-term reduced risk / protection against developing Ulcerative Colitis.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - McBurney Point: Location two-thirds of the distance from umbilicus to right anterior superior iliac spine.
  - Rovsing Sign: RLQ pain on LLQ palpation.
  - Psoas Sign: RLQ pain on passive right hip extension.
  - Obturator Sign: RLQ pain on internal rotation of flexed right hip.
  - Fecalith / Appendicolith: Calcified stone-like intraluminal mass triggering obstruction.
  - Negative Appendectomy Rate: Historical 8% to 25% surgical removal rate of normal appendices, significantly reduced by modern CT/ultrasound.
  - Target Sign: Ultrasonographic cross-sectional halo of acute appendicitis.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Fecalith Not Mandatory Exception: Physical luminal obstruction by a fecalith is found in only a minority of acute appendicitis cases; many cases represent primary appendiceal dysbiosis involving Fusobacterium expansion.
  - Antibiotics-First Nonoperative Strategy Exception: While appendectomy remains the standard of care, uncomplicated acute appendicitis can be successfully treated with antibiotics alone in up to 60-70% of selected patients without immediate surgery, though ~39% recur within 5 years.
  - Postoperative Antibiotic Omission Rule: Postoperative antibiotics are STRICTLY UNNECESSARY for simple uncomplicated non-perforated appendicitis after successful appendectomy; single preoperative prophylactic dose suffices.
  - Diagnostic Caution in Childbearing Females: Women of childbearing age have the highest negative appendectomy rate (up to 20-30%) due to mimicking gynecologic pathologies (tubo-ovarian abscess, ovarian cyst rupture, ectopic pregnancy, pelvic inflammatory disease).
  - Third-Trimester Pain Displacement: In late pregnancy, the enlarged uterus displaces the appendix superiorly and laterally, causing appendicitis pain to manifest in the right upper quadrant or right flank.
  - Protective Effect Against Ulcerative Colitis: Appendectomy performed for true appendicitis early in life confers significant long-term protection against developing Ulcerative Colitis.
