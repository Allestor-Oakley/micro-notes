# Peritonitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Peritonitis is defined as an acute or chronic inflammatory response of the peritoneal cavity lining resulting from contamination with microorganisms, chemical irritants (such as bile, gastric acid, or pancreatic enzymes), or both. Infection within the peritoneal cavity encompasses a broad spectrum of localized or diffuse entities that can involve any intraabdominal organ, peritoneal recess, or potential space.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Primary Peritonitis (Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis / SBP): Infection of the peritoneal cavity without an evident intraabdominal surgically treatable focus of mucosal breach or visceral perforation. Recognized SBP variants include: (1) Classic SBP (ascitic PMN count >=250 cells/mm3 with positive monomicrobial culture); (2) Monomicrobial Nonneutrocytic Bacterascites (positive culture with ascitic PMNs <250 cells/mm3); and (3) Culture-Negative Neutrocytic Ascites (ascitic PMNs >=250 cells/mm3 with negative culture in the absence of antibiotic therapy or pancreatitis).
  - Secondary Peritonitis: The most common intraabdominal infection (accounting for 80% to 90% of cases), resulting from microbial or chemical contamination of the sterile peritoneal space due to loss of mucosal barrier integrity from hollow viscus perforation, bowel ischemia/gangrene, severe inflammatory bowel disease, trauma, or operative disruption. Subclassified into community-acquired (low-risk vs high-risk) and healthcare-associated (complicating prior elective or emergency abdominal operations).
  - Tertiary Peritonitis: Persistent or recurrent intraabdominal infection without a surgically treatable focus that persists or recurs >=48 hours after adequate surgical source control of secondary peritonitis, characterized by host immune dysfunction and colonization by low-grade or multidrug-resistant nosocomial pathogens.
  - Continuous Ambulatory Peritoneal Dialysis (CAPD) Peritonitis: Major complication of peritoneal dialysis introduced via catheter touch contamination, exit-site/subcutaneous tunnel infection, or transmural bacterial migration across the gut wall.
  - Anatomical & Complexity Classifications: Classified as Diffuse (generalized) vs Localized (phlegmon/abscess), and Uncomplicated (intramural inflammation confined to a single organ) vs Complicated (extension into the sterile peritoneal space with peritonitis or abscess formation).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Prevalence & Burden: Primary peritonitis accounts for approximately 1% of all peritonitis cases, while secondary peritonitis accounts for 80% to 90%. Intraabdominal infections are ~80% community-acquired and ~20% healthcare-associated. CAPD peritonitis recurs in 20% to 30% of peritoneal dialysis patients and represents the primary cause for switching to hemodialysis.
  - Risk Factors for Primary Peritonitis / SBP: Advanced cirrhosis with high-volume ascites (prevalence of SBP in hospitalized cirrhotic ascites is 10% to 30%, especially Child-Turcotte-Pugh class C or MELD score >15); dilute ascitic fluid with low protein concentration (<1.0 to 1.5 g/dL, reflecting depleted opsonins); concurrent gastrointestinal hemorrhage; prior history of SBP; elevated serum bilirubin (>2.5 to 3.0 mg/dL); impaired renal function (creatinine >1.2 mg/dL, BUN >25 mg/dL); and use of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs). In children, SBP occurs in 2% of those with nephrotic syndrome or postnecrotic cirrhosis.
  - Risk Factors for Secondary & Tertiary Peritonitis: Gastrointestinal tract disease/perforation (peptic ulcer disease, appendicitis, diverticulitis, inflammatory bowel disease, typhoid/amebic/CMV intestinal ulceration); abdominal trauma; prior elective or emergency abdominal operations; surgical anastomosis dehiscence; biliary tract obstruction/cholecystitis; severe necrotizing pancreatitis; immunocompromise; and prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure.
  - Risk Factors for CAPD Peritonitis: Manual dialysate bag exchange touch contamination; nasal Staphylococcus aureus carriage; catheter exit-site or subcutaneous tunnel infections; chronic constipation; depression; and recent endoscopic procedures (colonoscopy, hysteroscopy, cystoscopy) with biopsy.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Primary Peritonitis (SBP): Characteristically monomicrobial (>90% of cases). Enteric gram-negative bacilli cause >60% of cases in cirrhotic adults (Escherichia coli is the predominant pathogen, followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae and Enterobacter species). Gram-positive cocci cause 20% to 35% of cases (Streptococcus pneumoniae, viridans group streptococci, Group A streptococci, and Enterococcus species). Staphylococcus aureus is rare (2% to 4%), usually linked to umbilical hernia erosion.
  - Secondary Peritonitis: Characteristically polymicrobial (averaging 4.5 species per case: 2.5 anaerobes and 2 facultative/aerobic species). Facultative gram-negative bacilli include E. coli (most common overall), Klebsiella species, Proteus species, Enterobacter species, Citrobacter species, and Enterococcus species. Obligate anaerobes are present in 80% to 95% of lower GI sources, dominated by Bacteroides fragilis (most prevalent anaerobe), non-fragilis Bacteroides species, Prevotella melaninogenica, Fusobacterium species, Bilophila wadsworthia, Peptostreptococcus species, Clostridium perfringens, and Eubacterium species.
  - CAPD Peritonitis: Gram-positive bacteria cause 60% to 80% of cases (Staphylococcus epidermidis / coagulase-negative staphylococci predominant, S. aureus, Streptococcus species, and Corynebacterium/diphtheroids). Gram-negative bacteria cause 15% to 30% (E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter species). Fungi cause 2% to 10%.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children: Primary peritonitis in children is predominantly caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, Group A streptococci, or Gram-negative coliforms (especially in nephrotic syndrome or postnecrotic cirrhosis). In appendicitis with perforation, polymicrobial flora including E. coli, B. fragilis, Fusobacterium species, Bilophila wadsworthia, and Prevotella predominate.
  - Adults & Elderly: Cirrhotic adult SBP is dominated by E. coli and K. pneumoniae; secondary/tertiary peritonitis in older adults involves complex polymicrobial colonic flora or resistant nosocomial organisms.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Nosocomial & Multidrug-Resistant Organisms (Tertiary / Healthcare-Associated): Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Acinetobacter baumannii, Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae, Carbapenem-Resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE / KPC producers), Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococci (VRE, e.g., Enterococcus faecium).
  - Mycobacteria & Granulomatous Agents: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (tuberculous peritonitis causing doughy abdomen, lymphocytic pleocytosis, and peritoneal nodules); Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (Mycobacterium fortuitum, Mycobacterium chelonae in CAPD); and Coccidioides immitis (coccidioidal granulomatous peritonitis).
  - Fungi: Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, Candida tropicalis, Candida parapsilosis, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Fusarium species (Candida is present in 20% of GI perforations, CAPD peritonitis, and post-surgical tertiary peritonitis).
  - Sexually Transmitted & Gynecologic Agents: Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis (causing pelvic peritonitis and Fitz-Hugh-Curtis perihepatitis syndrome).
  - Parasites: Entamoeba histolytica (amebic dysentery perforation or ruptured amebic liver abscess); Strongyloides stercoralis (hyperinfection transmural larval migration); and Cytomegalovirus (CMV enterocolitis perforation in AIDS).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Routes of Entry in Primary Peritonitis (SBP): (1) Bacterial Translocation: Enteric bacteria cross the intact or congested intestinal mucosal barrier into mesenteric lymph nodes and systemic circulation via the thoracic duct; (2) Impaired Reticuloendothelial Clearance: Cirrhotic portal hypertension and portosystemic shunts bypass hepatic Kupffer cell filtration, perpetuating bacteremia and seeding low-protein, low-opsonin ascitic fluid; (3) Direct Transmural Migration across the intact intestinal wall into ascites.
  - Mechanism of Secondary Peritonitis: Mucosal barrier disruption along the gastrointestinal or genitourinary tract spills endogenous mucosal microbiota and chemical irritants into the peritoneal cavity. Bacterial density varies by site: stomach/duodenum contains sparse salivary flora (10^3 CFU/mL), ileum contains 10^5 to 10^8 CFU/mL (E. coli, enterococci, B. fragilis), and colon contains dense sludge (10^11 to 10^12 bacteria/gram feces) where anaerobes outnumber aerobes 1,000:1 to 10,000:1.
  - Biphasic Pathogenesis of Secondary Infection: Inoculation of colonic flora produces a classic two-stage course: early acute phase driven by facultative gram-negative bacilli (E. coli) causing bacteremia, SIRS, and early mortality; followed by a late chronic phase driven by obligate anaerobes (B. fragilis) causing localized intraabdominal abscess formation (promoted by B. fragilis zwitterionic capsular polysaccharide A / PSA stimulating IL-17 CD4 T cells).
  - Mechanism of CAPD Peritonitis: Luminal or periluminal contamination of the peritoneal catheter during bag exchanges (touch contamination), progression from catheter exit-site or subcutaneous tunnel infections, or transmural migration from constipated bowel.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Massive Exudative Response & Third-Spacing: Peritoneal irritation triggers rapid vasodilation, hyperpermeability, and outpouring of protein-rich exudate (>3 g/dL) containing polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs). Fibrinogen in exudate polymerizes into fibrinous plaques that coat peritoneal surfaces, gluing together omentum and bowel loops to wall off infection. Third-spacing of massive fluid volumes into the peritoneal cavity causes severe hypovolemic shock.
  - Compartmentalized Cytokine Storm: Macrophages and mesothelial cells release massive localized amounts of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6, IFN-gamma, and chemokines) directly into peritoneal fluid (at levels far exceeding systemic circulation). This cytokine storm increases plasma renin, suppresses effective arterial blood volume, and drives hepatorenal syndrome and acute kidney injury (occurring in ~30% of SBP cases).
  - Chemical Peritonitis Escalation: Escape of unbuffered gastric acid, pancreatic enzymes (enzymatic auto-digestion), or toxic bile salts causes severe chemical peritonitis, destruction of peritoneal mesothelium, and secondary bacterial superinfection.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Acute Secondary Peritonitis: Sudden onset of severe, agonizing, persistent abdominal pain that is aggravated by any motion, coughing, or deep inspiration. Accompanied by anorexia, nausea, profuse vomiting, fever, chills, thirst, oliguria, obstipation (inability to pass feces or flatus), and abdominal distention. Rapid spread of pain reflects uncontained perforation (e.g., peptic ulcer rupture spreading in minutes), whereas gradual spread reflects localized leak (e.g., appendicitis).
  - Primary Peritonitis (SBP in Cirrhotic Adults): Acute or insidious onset of fever (37.8°C to 40°C, present in 50% to 80% of cases), diffuse abdominal pain, tenderness, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. Can present subtly as unexplained clinical decompensation in stable cirrhosis (worsening hepatic encephalopathy, altered mental status, acute kidney injury, or variceal bleeding) or may be completely clinically silent.
  - Tuberculous & Granulomatous Peritonitis: Subacute or chronic insidious course with low-grade fever, weight loss, night sweats, malaise, and abdominal distention.
  - CAPD Peritonitis: Cloudy or turbid dialysate effluent (the earliest and most constant sign) accompanied by abdominal pain/tenderness (60% to 80%), nausea/vomiting (30% to 50%), fever (25% to 50%), and diarrhea (10%).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Patient Appearance & Posture: Patient lies motionless in the supine position with knees flexed and shallow rapid intercostal respirations to minimize peritoneal movement; alert but restless early, becoming apathetic, delirious, or lethargic as sepsis progresses.
  - Abdominal Examination Signs: Diffuse or localized abdominal tenderness; involuntary muscle rigidity ("board-like abdomen"); guarding; exquisite rebound tenderness; hypoactive or completely absent bowel sounds (paralytic ileus); and abdominal distention. Palpation of a "doughy" abdomen is characteristic of tuberculous peritonitis.
  - Vital Signs & Systemic Signs: High fever (up to 42°C) or hypothermia (<35°C, a grave sign in chemical peritonitis or late septic shock); tachycardia with weak thready pulses; tachypnea; hypotension; oliguria; jaundice; and cool, clammy, mottled extremities in septic shock.
  - Specialized Gynecologic Signs: Right upper quadrant tenderness, guarding, and a hepatic friction rub in Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome (gonococcal or chlamydial perihepatitis).
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Cloudy Dialysate Effluent with >100 WBCs/mm3 (predominantly PMNs) in a Peritoneal Dialysis Patient: Confirms CAPD peritonitis.
  - Ascitic Fluid PMN Count >=250 cells/mm3 in a Cirrhotic Host without Surgical Source: Confirms primary peritonitis / SBP.
  - Free Intraperitoneal Air Under Diaphragm (Pneumoperitoneum) + Sudden Severe Abdominal Rigidity: Indicates perforated hollow viscus with secondary acute peritonitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Marked leukocytosis (>11,000 to 25,000 cells/uL) with a strong neutrophilic left shift (bandemia >10%). Leukopenia (<5,000 cells/uL) with immature forms indicates severe sepsis or peritoneal mobilization of circulating leukocytes. Hemoconcentration (elevated hematocrit) reflects dehydration.
  - Serum Chemistry & Renal Function: Elevated BUN (>25 to 30 mg/dL) and creatinine indicating acute kidney injury / hepatorenal syndrome; electrolyte imbalances (hyponatremia, hypokalemia); metabolic acidosis with elevated lactate; and elevated liver enzymes.
  - Coagulation Profile: Elevated PT/INR, PTT, or D-dimer in DIC / severe sepsis.
  - Pancreatic & Biliary Markers: Elevated serum amylase and lipase (>3-fold elevation specific for acute pancreatitis, though mild elevations occur in viscus perforation or peritonitis).
  - Serum Pregnancy Test (beta-hCG): Mandatory in female patients of childbearing age to exclude ectopic pregnancy.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Abdominal and Pelvic Computed Tomography (CT) with Oral and Intravenous Contrast (Diagnostic Imaging Modality of Choice): Preferred initial diagnostic study for secondary peritonitis. CT identifies extraluminal gas / pneumoperitoneum, free fluid collections, soft tissue phlegmons, wall thickening, abscesses, and pinpoints the precise anatomical site of visceral perforation.
  - Transabdominal Ultrasonography: Useful bedside tool for detecting ascitic fluid, guiding paracentesis, and evaluating right upper quadrant, pelvic, or retroperitoneal disease. Limited by overlying bowel gas, obesity, and dressings.
  - Plain Abdominal & Upright Chest Radiographs: Demonstrates free intraperitoneal air beneath the diaphragm (pneumoperitoneum in ~50% to 75% of hollow viscus perforations), dilated bowel loops with air-fluid levels (paralytic ileus), or visceral displacement.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Ascitic / Peritoneal Fluid Paracentesis Analysis (Core Diagnostic Standard for SBP):
    - Paracentesis Cell Count: Ascitic PMN leukocyte count >=250 cells/mm3 is diagnostic of SBP (if bloody, subtract 1 PMN per 250 RBCs/mm3).
    - Bedside Inoculation into Blood Culture Bottles: Inoculating 10 to 20 mL of ascitic fluid directly into blood culture bottles at the bedside increases positivity rates from 50% to 80%-100%.
    - Biochemical Differentiation (Primary vs Secondary Peritonitis): Secondary peritonitis is indicated by ascitic fluid total protein >1.0 g/dL, glucose <50 mg/dL, LDH elevated above upper limit of normal for serum, multiple bacterial species on Gram stain, CEA >5 ng/mL, or alkaline phosphatase >240 U/L. Serum-Ascites Albumin Gradient (SAAG) >1.1 g/dL indicates portal hypertension.
  - CAPD Dialysate Analysis: Dialysate WBC >100 cells/mm3 (with >50% PMNs) and positive culture (positive in 90% to 95% of cases).
  - Intraoperative Peritoneal Exudate / Abscess Culture: Gram stain and quantitative aerobic/anaerobic cultures guide targeted antibiotic adjustments in secondary/tertiary peritonitis.
  - Blood Cultures: Positive in 75% of primary peritonitis cases (aerobic bacteremia) and 20% to 30% of secondary peritonitis cases.
  - Molecular Testing: PCR testing on peritoneal tissue/fluid for Mycobacterium tuberculosis or Coccidioides immitis.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** For Primary Peritonitis / SBP: Paracentesis yielding ascitic fluid PMN count >=250 cells/mm3 with monomicrobial isolation (or clinical response) in the absence of an intraabdominal surgical source. For Secondary Peritonitis: Abdominal CT or direct exploratory laparotomy/laparoscopy confirming intraabdominal mucosal breach, free purulence, or perforation.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Hemodynamic Resuscitation: Immediate, aggressive early goal-directed IV volume resuscitation with balanced crystalloids (Lactated Ringer's or Normal Saline) or albumin to restore effective arterial blood volume, correct metabolic acidosis, and preserve renal perfusion (Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines).
  - Intravenous Albumin Infusion in SBP (Mandatory SBP Adjunct): Intravenous albumin (1.5 g/kg body weight within 6 hours of diagnosis, followed by 1.0 g/kg on day 3) combined with antibiotics significantly reduces acute kidney injury (from 31% down to 8%) and overall mortality (from 35% down to 16%) in cirrhotic patients with SBP (especially if creatinine >1 mg/dL, BUN >30 mg/dL, or bilirubin >4 mg/dL). Nonselective beta-blockers MUST BE DISCONTINUED in SBP as they increase mortality and hepatorenal syndrome risk.
  - Supportive Measures: NPO status; continuous gastric or small bowel tube decompression for ileus/distention; vasopressor support (norepinephrine) for septic shock; analgesia; and avoidance of nephrotoxic drugs (aminoglycosides, NSAIDs).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Primary Peritonitis / SBP Empiric Regimens (5 Days Total Duration):
    1. Extended-Spectrum Third-Generation Cephalosporins (First-Choice Standard): Cefotaxime 2 g IV q8h OR Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h. (As effective as ampicillin plus aminoglycosides, avoiding nephrotoxicity).
    2. Beta-Lactam / Beta-Lactamase Inhibitor Alternatives: Piperacillin-tazobactam 3.375 g to 4.5 g IV q6h OR Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h.
    3. Fluoroquinolones (for beta-lactam allergy, if no prior quinolone prophylaxis used): Levofloxacin 750 mg IV/PO daily OR Moxifloxacin 400 mg IV/PO daily.
    4. Carbapenems (for high ESBL risk or healthcare-associated SBP): Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Imipenem-cilastatin 500 mg IV q6h.
  - Secondary Peritonitis Empiric Regimens (Community-Acquired, Mild-to-Moderate, 4 to 7 Days Duration):
    1. Single-Agent Regimens: Ertapenem 1 g IV q24h OR Moxifloxacin 400 mg IV/PO q24h.
    2. Combination Regimens: Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Cefotaxime 2 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h.
  - CAPD Peritonitis First-Line Regimens (Intraperitoneal Route Preferred, 10 to 21 Days Duration):
    - Intraperitoneal Vancomycin (or Cefazolin) PLUS Intraperitoneal Gentamicin, Tobramycin, Ceftazidime, or Cefepime administered directly in dialysate exchanges.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - High-Risk Community-Acquired, Healthcare-Associated, or Tertiary Peritonitis Regimens:
    1. Single-Agent Carbapenems: Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Imipenem-cilastatin 500 mg IV q6h OR Doripenem 500 mg IV q8h OR Meropenem-vaborbactam 2 g/1 g IV q8h.
    2. Combination Regimens: Cefepime 2 g IV q8-12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Ceftazidime-avibactam 2.5 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Ceftolozane-tazobactam 1.5 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Aztreonam 2 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h PLUS Vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg IV q8-12h.
    3. Addition of Antifungal Coverage (for Candida peritonitis): Echinocandins (Caspofungin 50 mg IV daily, Micafungin 100 mg IV daily, or Anidulafungin 100 mg IV daily) as first-choice; or Fluconazole 800 mg loading dose then 400 mg IV/PO daily in non-critically ill patients without prior azole exposure.
  - Duration of Antimicrobial Therapy: 5 days for SBP; 24 hours for sterile peritonitis post-resection of unperforated viscus; 4 to 7 days for secondary peritonitis with adequate source control; 10 to 21 days for CAPD peritonitis.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Surgical Source Control in Secondary Peritonitis (DEFINITIVE MANDATORY INTERVENTION): Immediate emergency operative intervention (laparotomy or laparoscopy) within hours of presentation to: (1) Resect diseased or necrotic viscus; (2) Repair or divert bowel perforations (e.g., Hartmann procedure); (3) Evacuate purulence, feces, bile, and blood; and (4) Perform peritoneal lavage. Postponing source control beyond 24 hours markedly increases mortality. Operative intervention is STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED in primary peritonitis (SBP).
  - Percutaneous Image-Guided Catheter Drainage: Indicated for localized, unilocular intraabdominal or pelvic abscesses as an alternative to surgery.
  - CAPD Catheter Removal Indications: Mandatory removal of peritoneal dialysis catheter for: (1) Refractory peritonitis (failure to respond after 5 days); (2) Fungal or nontuberculous mycobacterial peritonitis; (3) P. aeruginosa peritonitis; (4) Relapsing peritonitis with same organism within 4 weeks; (5) Persistent exit-site or subcutaneous tunnel infection; or (6) Intraabdominal abscess.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Septic Shock, Multiorgan Dysfunction Syndrome (MODS), and Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS).
  - Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) and Hepatorenal Syndrome (occurring in ~30% of SBP cases, serving as the leading predictor of mortality).
  - Intraabdominal and Pelvic Abscess Formation.
  - Abdominal Compartment Syndrome (increased intraabdominal pressure >20 mmHg requiring decompression laparotomy).
  - Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC).
  - Superimposed Nosocomial or Fungal Tertiary Peritonitis.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - High Recurrence Rate of SBP (70% 1-year probability of recurrence in cirrhotic survivors without prophylaxis).
  - High Long-Term Mortality in Cirrhotic SBP (1-year mortality 70%, 2-year mortality 80%; indication for liver transplantation evaluation).
  - Extensive Intraabdominal Adhesions and Late Adhesive Small Bowel Obstruction.
  - Incisional Hernias and Enterocutaneous / Colocutaneous Fistulas (especially following open temporary abdominal closure or planned relaparotomy).
  - Permanent Loss of Peritoneal Dialysis Membrane Function requiring permanent transition to hemodialysis.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Board-Like Abdomen: Involuntary abdominal muscle rigidity pathognomonic for acute diffuse secondary peritonitis.
  - Serum-Ascites Albumin Gradient (SAAG): Difference >1.1 g/dL confirming portal hypertension-related ascites.
  - Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP): Monomicrobial infection of ascitic fluid without an intraabdominal surgical source.
  - Monomicrobial Nonneutrocytic Bacterascites & Culture-Negative Neutrocytic Ascites: Recognized clinical variants of SBP.
  - Fitz-Hugh-Curtis Syndrome: Gonococcal or chlamydial perihepatitis causing RUQ pain and violin-string adhesions.
  - Mannheim Peritonitis Index (MPI) & APACHE II: Stratification scoring systems predicting individual peritonitis mortality.
  - Intravenous Albumin SBP Rule: Co-administration of IV albumin with antibiotics reducing SBP renal failure and mortality.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Surgery Contraindication in Primary Peritonitis: Exploratory laparotomy is STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED in primary peritonitis (SBP) as it carries high mortality in cirrhotic hosts; SBP is managed medically with IV antibiotics and albumin. Conversely, secondary peritonitis MANDATES surgical or interventional source control.
  - 24-Hour Antibiotic Rule for Nonperforated Viscus: Only 24 hours of perioperative antibiotics are required for sterile secondary peritonitis resulting from an unperforated inflamed viscus (simple appendicitis or cholecystitis) following surgical resection.
  - Nonselective Beta-Blocker SBP Warning: Nonselective beta-blockers MUST BE STOPPED immediately in cirrhotic patients who develop SBP, as they significantly increase hepatorenal syndrome and death.
  - Primary Prophylaxis Criteria in Cirrhosis: Long-term daily oral antibiotic prophylaxis (TMP-SMX DS, norfloxacin, or ciprofloxacin) is MANDATORY for cirrhotic ascites patients with: (1) Prior SBP episode; (2) GI bleeding (7 days IV ceftriaxone); (3) Ascitic protein <1.0 g/dL; or (4) Ascitic protein <1.5 g/dL with renal impairment (creatinine >1.2, BUN >25, Na <=130) or CTP score >=9 with bilirubin >3.
  - Leukocyte Esterase Strip Screening: Urinary leukocyte esterase dipsticks provide high negative predictive value for rapid preliminary screening of SBP in ascitic fluid.
  - Drotrecogin Alfa Withdrawal: Recombinant human activated protein C was voluntarily withdrawn from the global market after the PROWESS-SHOCK trial demonstrated no survival benefit in severe sepsis of abdominal origin.
