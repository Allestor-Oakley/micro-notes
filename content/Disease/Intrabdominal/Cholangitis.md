# Cholangitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Cholangitis is an acute or chronic inflammatory and infectious condition involving the intrahepatic and/or extrahepatic biliary ducts (the biliary tree). It typically occurs when mechanical or functional obstruction of the bile ducts causes biliary stasis, elevated intraluminal biliary pressure, and secondary bacterial or parasitic infection, leading to localized mucosal inflammation, purulent exudate within the ducts, and potential systemic bacteremia/sepsis.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Acute / Ascending Cholangitis: Acute bacterial infection of the obstructed biliary tree; ranges from mild, self-limited disease to severe life-threatening sepsis.
  - Acute Suppurative Cholangitis: Severe form characterized by gross pus under pressure within the obstructed biliary tree, high systemic toxicity, hypotension, and altered mental status.
  - Recurrent Pyogenic Cholangitis (RPC) / Oriental Cholangiohepatitis: Endemic disease in East and Southeast Asia, characterized by recurrent episodes of bacterial cholangitis, intrahepatic pigment stone formation (pigment stones composed of calcium bilirubinate), biliary strictures, and hepatic parenchymal destruction; strongly associated with hepatobiliary parasitic flukes.
  - Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis (PSC): Noninfectious autoimmune chronic progressive disease characterized by inflammation, fibrotic strictures, and obliterative destruction of intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts, predisposing to recurrent secondary bacterial cholangitis episodes and cholangiocarcinoma.
  - Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC): Autoimmune destruction of small intrahepatic interlobular bile ducts (formerly Primary Biliary Cirrhosis).
  - AIDS Cholangiopathy: Biliary disease occurring in advanced HIV/AIDS (CD4 <100 cells/mm3) presenting with papillary stenosis, sclerosing cholangitis, or acalculous cholecystitis, caused by opportunistic infection with Cryptosporidium parvum, Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Microsporidia, or Cystoisospora belli.
  - Iatrogenic Post-ERCP Cholangitis: Biliary infection triggered by endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) during attempts to decompress or instrument an obstructed bile duct.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Biliary Obstruction Causes: Biliary duct obstruction is the primary driver of cholangitis. Choledocholithiasis (common bile duct stones resulting from gallstones) is the most common cause, accounting for 60% to 80% of cases. Benign biliary strictures, malignant biliary obstruction (pancreatic adenocarcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, ampullary tumors, metastatic lymphadenopathy), occluded biliary stents or drains, biliary-enteric anastomoses, and biliary strictures secondary to primary sclerosing cholangitis.
  - Parasitic Risk Factors: High prevalence in endemic regions of Asia and Latin America due to hepatobiliary parasitic infections including Clonorchis sinensis, Opisthorchis felineus, Opisthorchis viverrini, Fasciola hepatica, and Ascaris lumbricoides (migration of adult Ascaris worms into the common bile duct causing acute obstruction).
  - Demographic & Host Factors: Middle-aged and elderly adults (peak incidence in 5th to 7th decades of life, mirroring the prevalence of gallstone disease and biliary malignancies); immunosuppression; advanced HIV/AIDS; diabetes mellitus; previous biliary surgery or sphincterotomy; and indwelling biliary stents.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Facultative Gram-Negative Bacilli: Escherichia coli (31% to 44% of isolates; the single most common pathogen), Klebsiella species (9% to 20%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (0.5% to 19%), Enterobacter species (5% to 9%), Proteus species, Citrobacter species, and Serratia species.
  - Gram-Positive Cocci: Enterococcus species (Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium, isolated in 3% to 34% of cases, often in polymicrobial infections or healthcare-associated disease), Streptococcus species (2% to 10%), and Staphylococcus aureus (0% to 4%).
  - Anaerobes: Recovered in 4% to 20% of acute cholangitis cases (most frequently Bacteroides fragilis, Fusobacterium species, and Clostridium species); isolated with higher frequency in patients with prior biliary tract surgery or biliary-intestinal (biliary-enteric) anastomoses.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Adults & Elderly: Enteric gram-negative bacilli (E. coli, Klebsiella spp., Enterobacter spp., P. aeruginosa) and Enterococcus species dominate; anaerobes increase in frequency in older adults with prior biliary interventions or biliary-enteric anastomoses.
  - Endemic Parasitic Regions: Parasitic flukes (Clonorchis sinensis, Opisthorchis species, Fasciola hepatica) and roundworms (Ascaris lumbricoides) in children and adults.
  - Immunocompromised / HIV/AIDS Patients: Opportunistic pathogens causing AIDS cholangiopathy, including Cryptosporidium hominis/parvum, Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Microsporidia (Enterocytozoon bieneusi, Encephalitozoon intestinalis), and Cystoisospora belli.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Parasites & Flukes: Clonorchis sinensis, Opisthorchis felineus, Opisthorchis viverrini, Fasciola hepatica, Ascaris lumbricoides, and Echinococcus granulosus (biliary obstruction from hydatid cyst mass effect or daughter cyst rupture into bile ducts).
  - Fungi: Candida species (Candida albicans, Candida glabrata) in critically ill, immunocompromised, broad-spectrum antibiotic-treated, or post-procedural patients with biliary drains.
  - Mycobacteria: Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC causing biliary obstruction/adenopathy).
  - Viruses: Cytomegalovirus (CMV) and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Dual Requirement for Infection: Development of acute cholangitis requires two key factors: (1) Biliary duct obstruction causing mechanical stasis; and (2) Increased intraluminal biliary pressure coupled with bacterial contamination of bile.
  - Normal Biliary Sterility & Defense Mechanisms: In healthy state, bile in the biliary tree is sterile due to continuous bile flow, bactericidal bile salts, anatomical integrity of the sphincter of Oddi (which prevents retrograde entry of duodenal contents), and mucosal secretory IgA.
  - Biliary Stasis, Increased Intraluminal Pressure & Bacterial Entry: When the common bile duct becomes obstructed (by stones, strictures, or tumors), intrabiliary pressure rises above normal limits (>15 to 25 cm H2O). Bacteria enter the biliary tree primarily via ascending migration from the duodenum across a disrupted or bypassed sphincter of Oddi (or via biliary-enteric anastomoses/stents), or less commonly via portal venous bacteremia.
  - Translocation & Cholangiovenous Reflux: Elevated intrabiliary pressure disrupts tight junctions between biliary epithelial cells (cholangiocytes) and damages hepatic reticuloendothelial clearance. This leads to "cholangiovenous" and "cholangiolymphatic" reflux, flushing bacteria and endotoxins directly into the hepatic veins and systemic circulation, resulting in rapid bacteremia, sepsis, and septic shock.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Suppurative Inflammatory Response: Biliary duct mucosal hyperemia, edema, and intense neutrophilic infiltration. As bacteria multiply, purulent exudate fills the extrahepatic and intrahepatic biliary ducts.
  - Microabscess Formation & Hepatic Parenchymal Damage: High intraluminal pressure and suppurative exudate cause focal biliary epithelial necrosis, microperforations, and extension of infection into adjacent hepatic parenchyma, producing multiple small biliary pyogenic liver abscesses.
  - Biliary Stricture & Pigment Stone Genesis: Chronic or recurrent bacterial infection and parasite presence lead to bacterial beta-glucuronidase secretion, which hydrolyzes conjugated bilirubin to free bilirubin. Free bilirubin combines with calcium to form insoluble calcium bilirubinate intrahepatic pigment stones (the hallmark of Recurrent Pyogenic Cholangitis), driving progressive intrahepatic duct strictures, fibrosis, and secondary biliary cirrhosis.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Symptom Triad / Pentad: Right upper quadrant (RUQ) or epigastric abdominal pain, fever with shaking chills/rigors, and jaundice (yellowing of skin and sclera). Pain is typically continuous (distinguishing it from transient biliary colic) and may radiate to the right infrascapular region.
  - Systemic & Associated Symptoms: High fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, dark urine (bilirubinuria), pale or clay-colored stools (acholic stools due to complete biliary obstruction), and generalized pruritus.
  - Severe / Septic Presentation: Confusion, lethargy, disorientation, weakness, and collapse secondary to uroseptic shock.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Charcot Triad: The classic clinical triad of acute ascending cholangitis consisting of (1) Right upper quadrant abdominal pain, (2) Fever or chills/rigors, and (3) Jaundice. Present in approximately 50% to 70% of patients.
  - Reynolds Pentad: The classic pentad of severe acute suppurative cholangitis consisting of Charcot triad PLUS (4) Hypotension (septic shock) and (5) Altered mental status (confusion/lethargy). Reported in less than 14% of patients.
  - Physical Examination Signs: Right upper quadrant tenderness on palpation; positive Murphy sign (inhibition of deep inspiration due to pain on RUQ/gallbladder palpation); icterus / jaundice of sclera and mucous membranes; fever (temperature >38.5°C); tachycardia; tachypnea; and hypotension/shock in severe suppurative cases.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Charcot Triad (RUQ Pain + Fever + Jaundice) / Reynolds Pentad (Charcot Triad + Hypotension + Altered Mental Status): Classic, highly specific clinical presentations indicating acute ascending/suppurative cholangitis in the setting of biliary obstruction.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Marked leukocytosis (typically 12,000 to 20,000 cells/uL or higher) with a left shift (bandemia >10%). Leukopenia (<4,000 cells/uL) may be seen in severe uroseptic shock and portends a poor prognosis.
  - Inflammatory Biomarkers: Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and serum procalcitonin (PCT).
  - Cholestatic Liver Function Tests (Core Diagnostic Hallmark): Marked elevation of serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total and conjugated (direct) bilirubin, and gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT).
  - Hepatocellular & Pancreatic Markers: Moderate to marked elevations in serum transaminases (AST and ALT), reflecting hepatic parenchymal inflammation/microabscesses. Elevated serum amylase and lipase if concomitant gallstone pancreatitis is present.
  - Renal Function & Coagulation: Elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (indicates acute kidney injury secondary to dehydration/sepsis); elevated PT/INR in severe hepatic dysfunction or DIC.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Transabdominal Ultrasonography (First-Line Initial Imaging Modality): Bedside, rapid, non-invasive. Demonstrates biliary tree obstruction (common bile duct dilation >6 to 8 mm), choledocholithiasis (common bile duct stones), intrahepatic ductal dilation, gallbladder wall thickening, or pericholecystic fluid.
  - Computed Tomography (CT) of Abdomen/Pelvis with Contrast: Superior to ultrasound for evaluating distal common bile duct obstruction, pancreatic/ampullary tumors, intrahepatic ductal strictures, pneumobilia (gas in biliary tree), and detection of complications such as pyogenic liver abscesses or acute pancreatitis.
  - Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP): Highly accurate non-invasive imaging modality that provides detailed 3D visualization of the intrahepatic and extrahepatic biliary tree, detecting radiolucent stones, strictures, and anatomical variants without radiation or iodinated contrast.
  - Endoscopic Ultrasound (EUS): Highly sensitive for detecting small distal common bile duct stones or occult ampullary neoplasms.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Blood Cultures (MANDATORY IN ALL CASES): Two or more sets of blood cultures obtained prior to antibiotic administration; positive in 30% to 40% of acute cholangitis cases. Demonstrates high concordance with bile cultures.
  - Bile Cultures (Core Diagnostic Standard): Obtained during diagnostic/therapeutic ERCP, percutaneous drainage, or surgery. Positive in 59% to 93% of patients. Aerobic and anaerobic cultures must be processed.
  - Stool Examination / Parasite Diagnostics: Stool microscopy for ova and parasites (detecting Clonorchis, Opisthorchis, Fasciola eggs, or Ascaris) in endemic areas or suspected parasitic cholangitis; stool PCR/antigen testing for Cryptosporidium in HIV/AIDS.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Direct Cholangiography via Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) demonstrating biliary obstruction, purulent bile aspiration, and positive quantitative bile/blood cultures in a patient with cholestasis and systemic inflammation (Tokyo Guidelines TG18 criteria) represents the gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Hemodynamic Resuscitation: Immediate admission and aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation with balanced crystalloids (Lactated Ringer's or Normal Saline) to restore intravascular volume, correct hypotension, and maintain renal perfusion; correction of electrolyte abnormalities.
  - Monitoring & Sepsis Support: Intensive care unit (ICU) admission for severe cholangitis (Reynolds pentad or organ dysfunction); vasopressor support (norepinephrine) for septic shock refractory to fluid resuscitation; NPO (nothing by mouth) status; antipyretics and analgesics.
  - Assessment of Disease Severity (Tokyo Guidelines TG18): Severity stratified into Grade I (Mild), Grade II (Moderate), and Grade III (Severe; accompanied by organ dysfunction). Severity dictates the timing of biliary decompression.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Empiric Antibiotic Principles: Antibiotic therapy is mandatory but serves as a TEMPORIZING MEASURE; definitive cure requires mechanical decompression of the obstructed biliary tree. Empiric spectrum must cover enteric gram-negative bacilli (E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter) and Enterococcus species.
  - Mild-to-Moderate Community-Acquired Acute Cholangitis (Tokyo Guidelines TG18 / IDSA Regimens):
    1. Beta-Lactam / Beta-Lactamase Inhibitor Monotherapy: Piperacillin-tazobactam 3.375 g to 4.5 g IV q6h.
    2. Cephalosporin-Based Combination Therapy: Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Cefotaxime 2 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Cefepime 2 g IV q8-12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h.
    3. Carbapenem Monotherapy: Ertapenem 1 g IV q24h OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Imipenem-cilastatin 500 mg IV q6h OR Doripenem 500 mg IV q8h.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Fluoroquinolone-Based Alternative Regimens (Mild-to-Moderate, only if local E. coli resistance <10%): Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Moxifloxacin 400 mg IV q24h (addition of metronidazole is necessary due to rising Bacteroides resistance to fluoroquinolones).
  - Regimens for Severe, Healthcare-Associated, Post-Biliary-Enteric Anastomosis, or High-Risk Cholangitis:
    - Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h OR Cefepime 2 g IV q8h PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h OR Imipenem-cilastatin 500 mg IV q6h.
    - Addition of Vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg IV q8-12h (or Linezolid / Daptomycin if VRE risk) for healthcare-associated cases, severe illness, or known enterococcal colonization.
  - Duration of Antimicrobial Therapy: Antibiotics are administered for a total of 4 to 7 days ONCE BILIARY DRAINAGE / SOURCE CONTROL IS ACHIEVED and fever resolves (longer duration required if bacteremia, pyogenic liver abscesses, or persistent obstruction are present).
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Biliary Decompression / Drainage (DEFINITIVE MANDATORY TREATMENT): Mechanical relief of biliary obstruction is the single most critical intervention in acute cholangitis.
  - Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP - Preferred First-Line Drainage Procedure): Endoscopic biliary drainage with endoscopic sphincterotomy, stone extraction (using balloon/basket), or placement of a biliary stent / nasobiliary drainage tube. ERCP is successful in >90% of cases, carries lower morbidity than surgery, and is the gold standard decompression method.
  - Timing of ERCP Biliary Drainage:
    - Grade III (Severe) Cholangitis: URGENT / EMERGENCY ERCP biliary drainage within 24 hours (or immediately upon stabilization).
    - Grade II (Moderate) Cholangitis: Early ERCP biliary drainage within 24 to 48 hours.
    - Grade I (Mild) Cholangitis: Elective or initial trial of conservative medical therapy with ERCP drainage if no improvement after 24 to 48 hours.
  - Percutaneous Transhepatic Biliary Drainage (PTBD): Second-line decompression option when ERCP is unsuccessful, unavailable, or anatomically precluded (e.g., prior Roux-en-Y gastric bypass or duodenal obstruction).
  - Open Surgical Biliary Decompression (Choledochotomy with T-Tube Placement): Reserved as a last resort for failed endoscopic and percutaneous drainage attempts, or when concurrent emergency laparotomy is required for intraabdominal catastrophe.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Severe Bacteremic Urosepsis / Biliary Sepsis and Septic Shock (carrying high mortality if biliary drainage is delayed).
  - Multiple Pyogenic Liver Abscesses (biliary origin, characteristically polymicrobial).
  - Acute Suppurative Cholangitis with biliary empyema and ductal microperforation.
  - Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) and Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN) secondary to septic shock.
  - Concomitant Acute Gallstone Pancreatitis.
  - Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Recurrent / Relapsing Bacterial Cholangitis (common in strictures, RPC, or retained stones).
  - Intrahepatic and Extrahepatic Biliary Strictures (fibrotic luminal narrowing secondary to chronic inflammation).
  - Secondary Biliary Cirrhosis and Portal Hypertension (resulting from chronic long-standing biliary obstruction and recurrent cholangitis).
  - Cholangiocarcinoma (biliary tract cancer; long-term risk markedly increased in chronic Clonorchis sinensis / Opisthorchis infection, primary sclerosing cholangitis, and recurrent pyogenic cholangitis).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Charcot Triad: Right upper quadrant pain + fever + jaundice.
  - Reynolds Pentad: Charcot triad + hypotension + altered mental status (indicates suppurative cholangitis).
  - Tokyo Guidelines (TG18): International consensus criteria for diagnosis, severity assessment, and management of acute cholangitis.
  - Cholangiovenous Reflux: Elevation of intrabiliary pressure flushing bacteria into hepatic veins and systemic circulation.
  - Recurrent Pyogenic Cholangitis / Oriental Cholangiohepatitis: Endemic East Asian disease with intrahepatic calcium bilirubinate stones and strictures.
  - AIDS Cholangiopathy: Opportunistic biliary infection (Cryptosporidium, CMV) in advanced HIV.
  - Endoscopic Sphincterotomy & Stenting: Primary therapeutic ERCP procedures for biliary decompression.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Antibiotics Alone Are Temporizing Exception: Antibiotic therapy ALONE cannot cure acute cholangitis without mechanical decompression of the obstructed biliary tree; biliary drainage (via ERCP, PTBD, or surgery) is MANDATORY.
  - Reynolds Pentad Rarity: Although classic in medical education, Reynolds pentad is present in less than 14% of patients with acute cholangitis.
  - Charcot Triad Incompleteness: Charcot triad is present in only 50% to 70% of acute cholangitis cases; absence of the full triad does NOT exclude the diagnosis.
  - ERCP Paradox: ERCP is the primary therapeutic intervention for acute cholangitis, yet ERCP itself is a major iatrogenic cause of acute cholangitis when used to instrument an obstructed, uninfected bile duct.
  - Anaerobic Coverage Necessity: Although anaerobes are isolated from bile in less than 20% of simple acute cholangitis cases, anaerobic coverage (metronidazole) is MANDATORY if a biliary-enteric anastomosis is present or in severe/healthcare-associated cases.
  - Enterococcal Coverage Rule: Despite frequent enterococcal isolation from bile, routine empiric antienterococcal coverage is NOT required for mild-to-moderate community-acquired cholangitis, but IS MANDATORY for healthcare-associated cases, liver transplant recipients, or severe septic shock.
