# Diarrhea

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Diarrhea is defined as the passage of three or more loose, liquid, or unformed stools in a 24-hour period (or a daily stool weight exceeding 200 g/day). It results from an imbalance in intestinal absorption and secretion of water and electrolytes, direct mucosal invasion, or the action of enterotoxins, cytotoxins, or neurotoxins on gastrointestinal mucosal cells or the enteric nervous system.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - By Temporal Duration: (1) Acute Diarrhea (lasting 14 days or less; typically self-limited, caused by viruses, bacteria, or preformed toxins); (2) Persistent Diarrhea (lasting 14 to 29 days; often associated with parasites, EAEC, EPEC, malabsorption, or underlying immune deficiency); (3) Chronic Diarrhea (lasting 30 days or longer; associated with parasites, Environmental Enteric Dysfunction / Tropical Sprue, C. difficile, HIV enteropathy, or noninfectious causes).
  - By Pathophysiologic / Clinical Syndrome: (1) Acute Watery / Noninflammatory Diarrhea (enterotoxin-mediated or viral villous damage, large volume watery stools without gross blood or fecal leukocytes, low or absent fever, e.g., Rotavirus, Norovirus, ETEC, Vibrio cholerae, C. perfringens, B. cereus); (2) Acute Dysentery / Inflammatory Diarrhea (frequent small-volume stools with gross blood, mucus, and pus, severe abdominal cramps, tenesmus, fever, and abundant fecal PMNs, e.g., Shigella, EIEC, C. jejuni, NTS, E. histolytica); (3) Hemorrhagic Colitis (grossly bloody diarrhea with severe abdominal cramps and minimal or low-grade fever, e.g., Shiga toxin-producing E. coli / STEC such as O157:H7 and O104:H4); (4) Profuse Purging / Rice-Water Stools (copious isotonic watery fluid loss leading to rapid hypovolemic shock, e.g., V. cholerae O1/O139, ETEC); (5) Foodborne Poisoning / Intoxication (acute gastroenteritis acquired via contaminated food/water, divided by incubation period into preformed neurotoxin/emetic toxin <1-6 hours (S. aureus, B. cereus), enterotoxin in vivo 8-16 hours (C. perfringens, B. cereus), or invasive/tissue phase 1-7 days (Salmonella, C. jejuni, STEC, Listeria)); (6) Enteric / Typhoidal Fever (systemic illness with persistent fever, abdominal pain, hepatosplenomegaly, and variable bowel habits (constipation or pea-soup diarrhea), caused by S. enterica serovars Typhi and Paratyphi A, B, C); (7) Healthcare-Associated / Nosocomial Diarrhea (diarrhea developing after the 3rd hospital day, dominated by Clostridioides difficile); (8) Traveler's Diarrhea / Turista (acquired during international travel to resource-limited regions, predominantly caused by ETEC, EAEC, Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:** An estimated 2.4 billion episodes of gastroenteritis occur worldwide annually, causing >1.3 million deaths globally (fourth leading cause of death in children under 5 years, accounting for >500,000 deaths per year, concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia). In the United States, an estimated 179 million outpatient visits, 48 million foodborne illnesses, nearly 500,000 hospitalizations, and >5,000 deaths occur annually. Total diarrhea-attributable US mortality increased by 36.8% between 2005 and 2015, driven by healthcare-associated C. difficile in older adults. Risk factors include absence of safe piped water and sanitation infrastructure; international travel to Latin America, Africa, South/Southeast Asia (traveler's diarrhea affects up to 40% of travelers); age extremes (weanling diarrhea in infants 6 to 24 months due to loss of maternal breast milk antibodies and exposure to contaminated complementary foods/soil; elderly >65 years at high risk for severe STEC/HUS, C. difficile, and Salmonella bacteremia); undernutrition, stunting, and micronutrient deficiencies (zinc, vitamin A); reduced gastric acidity (proton pump inhibitors, H2-blockers, antacids, or gastrectomy increase susceptibility to Salmonella, C. jejuni, V. cholerae, C. difficile); broad-spectrum antibiotic exposures (disrupts normal protective gut microbiota, predisposing to C. difficile, Klebsiella oxytoca, and Candida overgrowth); daycare center attendance or institutional living (promotes person-to-person spread of low-inoculum pathogens like Shigella, Norovirus, Cryptosporidium, Giardia); male sex having sex with men (MSM; risks proctitis/colitis via N. gonorrhoeae, HSV, C. trachomatis LGV, T. pallidum, Shigella, E. histolytica); and immunocompromise (HIV/AIDS with CD4 <100 cells/uL, solid organ/stem cell transplant, cancer chemotherapy, hypogammaglobulinemia, predisposes to severe/chronic infection with Cryptosporidium, Cystoisospora, Cyclospora, Microsporidia, CMV, MAC, Listeria, Strongyloides hyperinfection, and persistent Norovirus).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Viruses: Norovirus (the single most common cause of medically attended acute gastroenteritis and foodborne/waterborne outbreaks across all ages in the US), Rotavirus (group A; historically the leading global cause of severe dehydrating diarrhea in infants/young children <5 years, now declining in vaccinated areas), Enteric Adenoviruses (serotypes 40 and 41), Astroviruses, and Sapoviruses.
  - Bacteria: Nontyphoidal Salmonella enterica (leading bacterial cause of foodborne hospitalizations/deaths in the US), Campylobacter jejuni (leading bacterial cause of diarrheal gastroenteritis overall in high-income nations; associated with poultry), Shigella species (S. sonnei, S. flexneri, S. dysenteriae type 1, S. boydii; leading global bacterial cause of dysentery), Diarrheagenic Escherichia coli pathotypes (Enterotoxigenic E. coli / ETEC—most common cause of traveler's diarrhea; Shiga toxin-producing E. coli / STEC / EHEC including O157:H7 and O104:H4; Enteropathogenic E. coli / EPEC; Enteroaggregative E. coli / EAEC; Enteroinvasive E. coli / EIEC), Clostridioides difficile (leading cause of nosocomial and antibiotic-associated diarrhea), Vibrio species (V. cholerae O1 and O139, non-O1/non-O139 V. cholerae, V. parahaemolyticus, V. vulnificus, V. mimicus), Yersinia enterocolitica, Bacillus cereus, Clostridium perfringens type A, Staphylococcus aureus (preformed enterotoxin food poisoning), and Listeria monocytogenes.
  - Protozoa & Parasites: Cryptosporidium hominis and Cryptosporidium parvum (leading protozoal cause of childhood diarrhea in low-income countries and waterborne outbreaks), Giardia lamblia (G. intestinalis; associated with contaminated stream water), Cyclospora cayetanensis (fresh produce outbreaks), Entamoeba histolytica (amebic dysentery and amebic liver abscess), Cystoisospora belli, and Microsporidia.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Infants & Young Children (<5 years): Rotavirus (leading global cause in unimmunized infants), Norovirus GII (leading cause in rotavirus-vaccinated infants), Cryptosporidium hominis/parvum, Enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), Enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), Enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC; associated with persistent diarrhea and malnutrition), Shigella species (leading bacterial cause of dysentery in children 1 to 4 years), Campylobacter jejuni, Salmonella enterica, Enteric Adenoviruses 40/41, Sapoviruses, Astroviruses, and Human Parechovirus type 3. In low-income countries (GEMS/MAL-ED studies), 6 pathogens drive moderate-to-severe childhood diarrhea: Shigella, Rotavirus, Adenovirus 40/41, ETEC, Cryptosporidium, and C. jejuni.
  - Older Children & Adolescents: Norovirus, Campylobacter jejuni, Nontyphoidal Salmonella, Shigella, STEC (O157:H7, highest incidence of HUS in children <5 to 10 years), Yersinia enterocolitica (causes pseudoappendicular syndrome / ileocecitis in older children), and Cyclospora.
  - Adults & Elderly (>65 years): Norovirus (leading cause of epidemic gastroenteritis in long-term care facilities/retirement communities), Clostridioides difficile (leading cause of diarrhea-related death in elderly), Nontyphoidal Salmonella (high risk for bacteremia and mycotic aortic aneurysm), Campylobacter jejuni, ETEC (traveler's diarrhea), STEC (high mortality 33% in elderly with HUS and 2% with hemorrhagic colitis alone), Listeria monocytogenes (febrile gastroenteritis and invasive listeriosis), Vibrio parahaemolyticus (seafood exposure), and C. perfringens.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Opportunistic Pathogens in Immunocompromised Hosts (HIV/AIDS CD4 <100 cells/uL, Transplant, Chemotherapy): Cryptosporidium hominis/parvum, Cystoisospora belli, Cyclospora cayetanensis, Microsporidia (Enterocytozoon bieneusi, Encephalitozoon intestinalis), Cytomegalovirus (CMV enteritis/colitis with mucosal ulceration and perforation), Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC enteritis), Chronic Norovirus / Sapovirus / Rotavirus infection (lasting months to years), Strongyloides stercoralis hyperinfection syndrome (causing enteric Gram-negative bacteremia/sepsis), Listeria monocytogenes, Salmonella enterica (high-grade recurrent bacteremia), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida albicans.
  - Rare / Fastidious / Zoonotic / Toxin-Producing Organisms: Vibrio vulnificus (fulminant sepsis and bullous skin lesions following raw oyster ingestion in cirrhotic/hemochromatosis patients), Clostridium perfringens type C (causing Enteritis Necroticans / Pigbel / Darmbrand), Cytotoxic Klebsiella oxytoca (antibiotic-associated hemorrhagic colitis), Aeromonas hydrophila, Plesiomonas shigelloides, Edwardsiella tarda, Yersinia pseudotuberculosis (appendicitis-like syndrome), Tropheryma whipplei (Whipple disease), Helicobacter cinaedi / Helicobacter fennelliae (proctocolitis in MSM), Arcobacter species, Capillaria philippinensis, Angiostrongylus costaricensis, Anisakis simplex (acute gastric/intestinal vomiting illness from raw fish), Trichinella spiralis, Fasciola hepatica (hepatic fluke migration causing fever, RUQ pain, and high eosinophilia >1000-3000/mm3), Sarcocystis, Brainerd diarrhea agent (idiopathic chronic watery diarrhea lasting months/years linked to unpasteurized raw milk or unchlorinated water), and Zaire Ebolavirus (causing voluminous cholera-like diarrhea and vomiting).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Ingestion & Inoculum Dose: Transmission occurs via the fecal-oral route through ingestion of contaminated water, food, or person-to-person contact/fomites. Pathogens vary widely in required infectious dose: (1) Low Infectious Inoculum (10 to 100 organisms/cysts; highly contagious via person-to-person spread): Shigella species, STEC, Entamoeba histolytica, Giardia lamblia, Cryptosporidium, and Norovirus (as few as 18 viral particles); (2) High Infectious Inoculum (10^5 to 10^8 organisms; requires significant multiplication in food/water): Vibrio cholerae, Enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), Nontyphoidal Salmonella, and Clostridium perfringens.
  - Gastric Acid Barrier & Colonization: Ingested pathogens must survive the protective gastric acid barrier (pH <2.0). Gastric bypass, PPIs, or achlorhydria significantly reduce the required infectious dose. Surviving organisms adhere to intestinal mucus and brush border epithelial cells using surface fimbriae/pili (e.g., EPEC bundle-forming pilus (Bfp), ETEC colonization factor antigens (CFAs), EAEC aggregative adherence fimbriae (AAF), V. cholerae toxin-coregulated pilus (TCP)).
  - Enterotoxins & Secretory Diarrhea Mechanisms: Toxins act directly on enterocytes without structural tissue destruction. Prototypical Cholera Toxin (V. cholerae O1/O139) and ETEC Heat-Labile Toxin (LT) are AB5 subunit enterotoxins; the B pentamer binds enterocyte GM1 ganglioside receptors, delivering the active A subunit into the cytoplasm to ADP-ribosylate Gs-alpha, permanently activating adenylate cyclase, elevating intracellular cAMP, opening apical CFTR chloride channels, and driving massive, watery, isotonic fluid/electrolyte secretion into the intestinal lumen. ETEC Heat-Stable Toxin (STa) binds apical guanylate cyclase C, elevating intracellular cGMP to inhibit NaCl absorption and induce fluid secretion. Rotavirus NSP4 enterotoxin acts as a viral enterotoxin, inducing intracellular calcium mobilization, activating the enteric nervous system, and triggering secretory diarrhea.
  - Cytotoxins & Cytoskeletal Disruption: Pathogens produce cytotoxins that destroy enterocytes or disrupt tight junctions: Shiga Toxins (Stx1, Stx2 produced by S. dysenteriae type 1 and STEC/EHEC) are AB5 toxins whose B pentamer binds cell-surface globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) receptors (abundant on intestinal microvascular endothelium and renal tubular cells), undergoes retrograde transport, and uses its A subunit to cleave specific adenine residues from 28S rRNA of 60S ribosomes, halting protein synthesis and causing endothelial microvascular necrosis, hemorrhagic colitis, and HUS. C. difficile Toxin A (TcdA) and Toxin B (TcdB) are glucosyltransferases that target Rho/Rac/Cdc42 GTPases, disaggregating F-actin, disrupting tight junctions, and triggering massive neutrophilic inflammation, pseudomembrane formation, and mucosal necrosis. EPEC and STEC produce Attaching and Effacing (A/E) lesions on enterocytes using a Type III Secretion System (T3SS) to translocate Tir (Translocated Intimin Receptor) into host cell membranes; Intimin binding triggers actin polymerization into pedestals beneath bound bacteria, destroying absorptive microvilli.
  - Epithelial Invasion & Intracellular Spread: Invasive pathogens (Shigella, EIEC, Salmonella, C. jejuni, Yersinia) invade intestinal epithelial cells. Shigella and EIEC selectively enter M cells overlying Peyer's patches, transcytose to the basolateral surface, escape phagosomes, and use a T3SS-encoded actin-polymerizing motor (IcsA) to propel themselves laterally through enterocyte cytoplasm from cell to cell without entering the extracellular space, causing extensive mucosal ulceration and microabscesses. Salmonella uses T3SS-1 (SPI-1) to induce host cell membrane ruffling and macropinocytosis, replicating within specialized phagosomal vacuoles.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Noninflammatory vs. Inflammatory Mucosal Injury:
    - Noninflammatory (Small Bowel Secretory): Minimal structural damage or cellular infiltrate; villus tip blunting or microvillus disruption in upper small intestine (Norovirus, Rotavirus, Giardia); fluid/electrolyte secretion without blood, mucus, or fecal PMNs.
    - Inflammatory (Colonic & Distal Ileal Dysentery): Acute mucosal invasion or cytotoxic necrosis triggers intense mucosal inflammation. Recruitment of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (neutrophils) into the lamina propria and epithelial crypts (cryptitis and crypt abscesses), sloughing of necrotic enterocytes, superficial exudative ulcerations (Shigella, C. jejuni, NTS) or deep, flask-shaped, undermined mucosal ulcers (Entamoeba histolytica), and formation of fibrinopurulent yellow-white pseudomembranes (C. difficile).
  - Malabsorption, Environmental Enteric Dysfunction (EED) & Tropical Sprue: Chronic or repeated enteric infections damage the protective mucin layer and mucosal architecture, causing villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, decreased brush border disaccharidase activity (lactase deficiency), small bowel bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), impaired nutrient absorption, increased mucosal permeability (elevated lactulose:mannitol ratio), and systemic microbial translocation. This chronic mucosal inflammation drives Environmental Enteric Dysfunction (EED) and Tropical Sprue, resulting in growth faltering, stunting, and cognitive impairment in children in resource-limited settings.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Acute Watery Diarrhea: Abrupt onset of frequent, large-volume, watery, non-bloody stools; accompanied by nausea, vomiting, abdominal bloating/cramping, anorexia, and thirst. Minimal or low-grade fever. (Norovirus features acute prominent vomiting ("winter vomiting disease"); ETEC causes traveler's diarrhea starting 5-15 days post-arrival; V. cholerae causes sudden, painless, voluminous, non-bloody "rice-water" stool purging leading to rapid dehydration within hours).
  - Acute Dysentery / Inflammatory Diarrhea: Frequent, small-volume stools containing gross blood, mucus, and pus; accompanied by high fever, severe colicky abdominal pain, tenesmus (painful, ineffective straining to defecate), and toxic appearance (Shigella, C. jejuni, NTS, EIEC, E. histolytica).
  - Hemorrhagic Colitis (STEC / EHEC): Severe abdominal cramping and initial watery diarrhea that rapidly becomes grossly bloody within 24 to 48 hours ("all blood and no stool"). Characteristically accompanied by minimal or NO fever (fever absent in >70% of cases!).
  - Enteric / Typhoid Fever: Insidious onset of rising step-ladder fever (>39°C), headache, malaise, anorexia, nonproductive cough, myalgias, abdominal pain, and variable bowel habits (constipation in 20%, diarrhea / "pea-soup" loose stools in 20%).
  - Food Poisoning Syndromes: (1) Rapid onset 1 to 6 hours post-meal: prominent nausea and vomiting with minimal diarrhea (S. aureus, B. cereus emetic toxin); (2) Intermediate onset 8 to 16 hours post-meal: severe abdominal cramps and watery diarrhea with minimal vomiting/fever (C. perfringens, B. cereus diarrheal toxin); (3) Delayed onset >16 to 48+ hours: febrile gastroenteritis or dysentery (Salmonella, C. jejuni, Vibrio, Listeria).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Assessment of Volume Status & Dehydration: (1) Mild Dehydration (3-5% weight loss): dry mucous membranes, increased thirst; (2) Moderate Dehydration (6-9% weight loss): tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension, sunken eyes, decreased skin turgor (tenting), oliguria, irritability; (3) Severe Dehydration (>=10% weight loss): thready/absent peripheral pulses, hypotension / hypovolemic shock, cold clammy cyanotic extremities, delayed capillary refill (>3 seconds), lethargy, stupor, or coma.
  - Abdominal Examination Findings: Hyperactive bowel sounds in acute watery diarrhea; hypoactive or absent bowel sounds (paralytic ileus) in severe peritonitis/toxic megacolon. Diffuse abdominal tenderness; localized tenderness in right lower quadrant / ileocecitis mimicking acute appendicitis (pseudoappendicular syndrome caused by Yersinia enterocolitica, C. jejuni, or Salmonella).
  - Systemic & Cutaneous Physical Signs:
    - Typhoid Fever: Coated tongue with red margins/tip; Rose Spots (1 to 4 mm blanching erythematous macules on trunk/abdomen during 2nd week, <5% in ambulatory cases); relative bradycardia (pulse-temperature dissociation, Faget sign); hepatomegaly and splenomegaly.
    - C. difficile Colitis: Abdominal distention, lower quadrant tenderness, fever, leukocytosis, and elevated fecal lactoferrin.
    - Vibrio vulnificus: Bullous skin lesions, necrotizing vasculitis, and hemorrhagic bullae on lower extremities in patients with liver disease.
    - Tropical Sprue / EED: Physical signs of micronutrient deficiencies: cheilosis, angular stomatitis, glossitis (folate/B12 deficiency), koilonychia (iron deficiency), dermatitis/hyperkeratosis (zinc/vitamin A), peripheral edema (hypoalbuminemia), and linear stunting/wasting.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Rice-Water Stools: Copious, pale, gray, odorless, non-bloody watery stools with flecks of mucus, pathognomonic for epidemic Vibrio cholerae infection.
  - Rose Spots & Coated Tongue: Transient 1 to 4 mm blanching pink macules on the chest/abdomen paired with a white/yellowish-brown coated tongue sparing the edges, classic for Typhoid Fever (S. Typhi).
  - "All Blood and No Stool" with Minimal Fever: Grossly bloody diarrheal fluid with severe cramps and absence of fever pathognomonic for Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC O157:H7) hemorrhagic colitis.
  - Pseudomembranes on Endoscopy: Raised, 2 to 8 mm yellow-white inflammatory plaques overlying erythematous colonic mucosa pathognomonic for Clostridioides difficile colitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis with a left shift in acute bacterial dysentery, C. difficile colitis (leukemoid reaction WBC >30,000-50,000/mm3 indicates severe CDI), and neutropenic enterocolitis; Leukopenia or normal WBC count with bandemia in Typhoid fever and viral gastroenteritis. Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia with helmet cells/schistocytes, severe thrombocytopenia (<100,000/mm3), and acute renal failure (elevated BUN/creatinine) indicate Hemolytic-Uremic Syndrome (HUS) following STEC or S. dysenteriae type 1. Hemoconcentration (elevated hematocrit) reflects severe dehydration.
  - Electrolytes & Acid-Base Panel: Hyponatremia, hypokalemia, and hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis (low HCO3- due to fecal bicarbonate loss) in severe watery diarrhea / cholera.
  - Focal & Fecal Inflammatory Markers: Fecal Leukocytes (microscopic methylene blue / Gram stain demonstrating sheets of polymorphonuclear leukocytes); Fecal Lactoferrin (neutrophil granule protein immunoassay) and Fecal Calprotectin. Positive fecal leukocytes or elevated lactoferrin/calprotectin differentiate inflammatory colitis (Shigella, C. jejuni, NTS, C. difficile) from noninflammatory secretory/viral diarrhea.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Abdominal Radiography (Plain Film): Evaluates for toxic megacolon (colonic dilation >6 cm), paralytic ileus, pneumoperitoneum (free intraperitoneal air from intestinal perforation in typhoid fever or C. difficile), or pneumatosis intestinalis.
  - Computed Tomography (CT) of Abdomen/Pelvis: Modality of choice for complicated gastroenteritis, neutropenic enterocolitis, diverticulitis, or intra-abdominal abscess. Demonstrates bowel wall thickening (>4 mm in cecum/ascending colon in typhlitis/neutropenic enterocolitis), "accordion sign" (diffuse colonic wall thickening and submucosal edema in C. difficile colitis), pericolic fat stranding, appendiceal/ileocecal inflammation (Yersinia ileocecitis), mesenteric lymphadenopathy, or intramural air/abscess.
  - Endoscopy (Sigmoidoscopy / Colonoscopy) & Biopsy: Indicated in chronic/persistent diarrhea, suspected C. difficile failing non-invasive tests, amebic colitis, or immunocompromised hosts. Findings: Raised yellow-white pseudomembranous plaques (C. difficile); shallow 3 to 7 mm diffuse mucosal ulcers with purulent exudate (Shigella); discrete small, flask-shaped ulcers with undermined edges and relatively normal intervening mucosa (Entamoeba histolytica; PAS stain confirms amebic trophozoites with ingested RBCs); mucosal cytomegaly with intranuclear inclusion bodies (CMV colitis).
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Stool Culture (Bacterial Enteric Panel): Indicated for severe illness, fever, bloody diarrhea, immunocompromised hosts, public health surveillance, or foodborne outbreaks. Routine stool culture isolates Nontyphoidal Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter jejuni (requires selective Campy-BAP medium at 42°C in microaerophilic atmosphere), and E. coli O157:H7 (requires Sorbitol-MacConkey / SMAC agar yielding sorbitol-negative white colonies). Specific notification/special media required for Vibrio species (TCBS agar), Yersinia enterocolitica (cold enrichment), or Aeromonas/Plesiomonas.
  - Culture-Independent Diagnostic Tests (CIDTs) & Multiplex PCR Panels: Commercially available multiplex PCR panels (e.g., BioFire FilmArray GI Panel, Verigene, Luminex xTAG) simultaneously test for 14 to 22 targets (bacteria: Salmonella, Shigella, C. jejuni, ETEC, STEC, EPEC, EAEC, EIEC, V. cholerae, Yersinia, C. difficile; viruses: Norovirus GI/GII, Rotavirus A, Adenovirus 40/41, Sapovirus, Astrovirus; parasites: Giardia, Cryptosporidium, E. histolytica, Cyclospora). Rapid (1-2 hours) and highly sensitive; positive bacterial results should be followed by reflex culture for antimicrobial susceptibility testing and outbreak subtyping (PulseNet).
  - C. difficile Testing Algorithm: Stool testing indicated for acute unformed stools in patients with recent antibiotic or hospital exposure. Preferred two-step algorithm: Glutamate Dehydrogenase (GDH) antigen assay OR Nucleic Acid Amplification Test (NAAT / PCR for tcdB gene) PLUS C. difficile Toxin A/B Enzyme Immunoassay (EIA).
  - Parasitology (Microscopy & Special Stains): Stool Ova and Parasites (O&P) wet mount for Giardia trophozoites/cysts and E. histolytica. Modified Acid-Fast Stain (Kinyoun) for Cryptosporidium (4-6 um pink oocysts), Cyclospora (8-10 um oocysts), and Cystoisospora belli (20-30 um oocysts).
  - Blood Cultures: Indicated in enteric/typhoid fever (positive in 40-80% of cases), infants <12 months, immunocompromised hosts, severe sepsis, or suspected bacteremia from NTS, C. fetus, Yersinia, or V. vulnificus.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Stool Microbiological Culture demonstrating isolation and antimicrobial susceptibility of the specific bacterial pathogen (or diagnostic NAAT / multiplex PCR detection of pathogen-specific nucleic acid) combined with characteristic clinical findings remains the gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Fluid & Electrolyte Resuscitation (Cornerstone of Management):
    1. Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT): Mainstay of treatment for mild-to-moderate dehydration in all ages. Reduced-Osmolarity Oral Rehydration Salts (WHO-ORS solution: 75 mEq/L sodium, 75 mmol/L glucose, total osmolarity 245 mOsm/L) effectively treats >90% of acute diarrheal episodes. Continued feeding with easily digestible age-appropriate foods should be maintained.
    2. Intravenous Fluid Resuscitation: Mandatory for severe dehydration, hypovolemic shock, intractable vomiting, or stupor. Administer rapid IV boluses of balanced crystalloids (Lactated Ringer's or Plasma-Lyte preferred over 0.9% Normal Saline; 20 mL/kg boluses in children, 1-2 L in adults) until peripheral pulses and perfusion normalize.
  - Supplemental Nutritional Therapy: Zinc supplementation (20 mg/day oral zinc for 10-14 days; 10 mg/day for infants <6 months) significantly reduces diarrhea duration, severity, and 6-month recurrence in children in low-income settings. Vitamin A supplementation in acute shigellosis/diarrhea in malnourished children.
  - Antimotility Agent Restrictions: Loperamide (2-4 mg PO) is safe and effective ONLY in mild-to-moderate non-bloody traveler's diarrhea or watery diarrhea WITHOUT fever. CONTRAINDICATED in children <2 years (risk of ileus/lethargy), STEC/EHEC infection (increases Shiga toxin absorption and HUS risk), and severe bloody dysentery / C. difficile colitis (risk of toxic megacolon).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Indications for Empiric Antibiotics: Empiric antimicrobial therapy is NOT recommended for uncomplicated mild acute watery diarrhea in immunocompetent hosts. Indicated ONLY for: (1) Severe traveler's diarrhea; (2) Acute febrile dysentery / bloody diarrhea; (3) High-risk hosts (infants <12 months, adults >50 years, immunocompromised, severe sepsis, cardiac/joint prostheses, or severe atherosclerosis); (4) Suspected Cholera or Shigellosis.
  - Empiric Regimens for Acute Febrile Dysentery / Severe Traveler's Diarrhea:
    - Adult Oral Regimen: Azithromycin 1000 mg PO single dose OR 500 mg PO daily for 3 days (PREFERRED first-line agent globally, especially for travel to South/Southeast Asia due to high fluoroquinolone resistance in Campylobacter, Shigella, and ETEC).
    - Alternative Adult Regimen (Low-Resistance Areas): Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO bid x 3 days OR Levofloxacin 500 mg PO daily x 1-3 days OR Rifaximin 200 mg PO tid x 3 days (for non-invasive ETEC traveler's diarrhea ONLY; ineffective for invasive Campylobacter/Shigella).
    - Pediatric Oral Regimen: Azithromycin 10 mg/kg PO daily (max 500 mg) x 3 days.
    - Severe Hospitalized / Invasive Dysentery Regimen: Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h (100 mg/kg/day in children) OR Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Pathogen-Directed Targeted Therapies:
    - Shigella Species: Azithromycin 500 mg PO daily x 3 days OR Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO bid x 3 days (if susceptible) OR Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h x 3 days. (Reduces duration, fever, and shedding).
    - Campylobacter Jejuni: Azithromycin 500 mg PO daily x 3 days (indicated for severe/prolonged illness or immunocompromised; modest 1.3-day symptom reduction).
    - Nontyphoidal Salmonella (NTS): Antibiotics NOT indicated for uncomplicated gastroenteritis in healthy hosts (prolongs stool shedding). Indicated ONLY for high-risk patients (infants <12 months, adults >50 years, immunocompromised, prostheses): Azithromycin 500 mg PO daily x 7 days OR Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h x 7-14 days OR Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO bid x 7-10 days.
    - Shiga Toxin-Producing E. coli (STEC / EHEC): ANTIBIOTICS ARE CONTRAINDICATED! Antibiotics (especially fluoroquinolones and beta-lactams) induce Stx-encoding bacteriophages, markedly increase Shiga toxin production/release, and increase HUS risk by 17-fold! Supportive volume expansion is the primary therapy.
    - Vibrio Cholerae: Doxycycline 300 mg PO single dose (adults) OR Azithromycin 1000 mg PO single dose (pregnant women/children) OR Ciprofloxacin 1000 mg PO single dose. (Shortens volume loss and shedding).
    - Clostridioides Difficile Colitis: Oral Fidaxomicin 200 mg PO bid x 10 days OR Oral Vancomycin 125 mg PO qid x 10 days (first-line agents). Metronidazole 500 mg PO tid x 10-14 days is reserved ONLY for initial non-severe CDI when vancomycin/fidaxomicin are unavailable. Severe fulminant CDI: Oral Vancomycin 500 mg PO/NG qid PLUS IV Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h +/- Vancomycin retention enema.
    - Giardia Lamblia: Metronidazole 250 mg PO tid x 5-7 days OR Tinidazole 2 g PO single dose OR Nitazoxanide 500 mg PO bid x 3 days.
    - Cryptosporidium: Nitazoxanide 500 mg PO bid x 3 days in immunocompetent hosts; ART optimization + Paromomycin + Azithromycin in HIV/AIDS.
    - Entamoeba Histolytica: Metronidazole 750 mg PO tid x 7-10 days (tissue amebicide) FOLLOWED BY Paromomycin 30 mg/kg/day PO in 3 divided doses x 7 days (luminal amebicide to prevent relapse).
    - Typhoid Fever (S. Typhi): Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h x 7-14 days OR Azithromycin 1000 mg PO daily x 7 days (for fluoroquinolone-resistant / DCS strains). Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO bid x 7-14 days (for fully susceptible strains). Adjunctive High-Dose Dexamethasone (3 mg/kg IV load, then 1 mg/kg q6h x 48 hours) for severe typhoid encephalopathy and shock.
    - Tropical Sprue: Tetracycline 250 mg PO qid PLUS Folic Acid 5 mg PO daily for 1 to 6 months.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Indications for Surgical Intervention:
    1. Intestinal Perforation: Emergency laparotomy with segmental bowel resection, primary repair, or stoma creation for free ileal perforation in Typhoid fever (median onset 9 days post-fever) or perforated C. difficile colitis / amebic colitis.
    2. Toxic Megacolon & Fulminant C. difficile Colitis: Emergency subtotal colectomy with end ileostomy (or loop ileostomy with intraoperative colonic lavage and anterograde vancomycin flushes) for fulminant CDI with megacolon, severe shock, or rising serum lactate >5 mmol/L.
    3. Drainage of Intra-abdominal / Amebic Abscesses: Percutaneous ultrasound/CT-guided catheter drainage or needle aspiration for pyogenic liver abscesses, large amebic liver abscesses (>5 cm, left lobe risk of pericardial rupture, or failing 48-72 hours of metronidazole), or diverticular abscesses >3-4 cm.
    4. Surgical Debridement for Neutropenic Enterocolitis: Operative resection indicated for intestinal perforation, uncontrolled GI hemorrhage, or persistent clinical deterioration despite maximum broad-spectrum medical therapy.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Severe Hypovolemic Shock & Electrolyte Collapse (acute kidney injury, severe metabolic acidosis, hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, death within hours in cholera).
  - Hemolytic-Uremic Syndrome (HUS): Triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute renal failure occurring ~1 week post-diarrhea in 6% of STEC cases (15% in children <5 years) and S. dysenteriae type 1; overall mortality 3% in children, 33% in elderly.
  - Intestinal Perforation & Intestinal Hemorrhage: Severe life-threatening complications of Typhoid fever (ileal Peyer's patch necrosis) and severe amebic or C. difficile colitis.
  - Toxic Megacolon & Septic Shock: Rapid colonic dilation >6 cm with systemic toxicity in C. difficile, Shigella, or E. histolytica colitis.
  - Bacteremia & Metastatic Seeding: Nontyphoidal Salmonella bacteremic seeding causing mycotic aortic aneurysms (in adults >50 years with atherosclerosis), osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, or endocarditis; Yersinia or C. fetus bacteremia.
  - Extraintestinal Neurological Complications: Seizures, meningismus, and encephalopathy in shigellosis (associated with Shiga neurotoxin); toxic encephalopathy in typhoid fever.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Postinfectious Irritable Bowel Syndrome (PI-IBS): Chronic abdominal pain, bloating, and altered bowel habits developing in 10% to 30% of patients following acute bacterial gastroenteritis (C. jejuni, Shigella, Salmonella).
  - Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS): Acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy caused by molecular mimicry between Campylobacter jejuni lipooligosaccharide (LOS) and human peripheral nerve gangliosides (GM1, GD1a); C. jejuni triggers 20% to 40% of all GBS cases worldwide.
  - Reactive Arthritis (formerly Reiter Syndrome): Sterile HLA-B27-associated asymmetric oligoarthritis, conjunctivitis, and urethritis occurring 2 to 4 weeks after infection with Shigella flexneri, Salmonella enterica, Campylobacter jejuni, Yersinia enterocolitica, or C. difficile.
  - Chronic Environmental Enteric Dysfunction (EED) & Stunting: Persistent mucosal enteropathy, villous atrophy, and malabsorption leading to growth faltering, physical stunting, and cognitive impairment in children in developing nations.
  - Chronic Biliary / Intestinal Typhoid Carriage: Asymptomatic persistence of S. Typhi in the gallbladder (often associated with gallstones/biofilms) for >1 year in 2% to 5% of infected individuals, serving as an epidemic reservoir (e.g., "Typhoid Mary").

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Rice-Water Stools: Pale, flecked, watery diarrheal purging in epidemic Vibrio cholerae.
  - Rose Spots: Transient 1-4 mm blanching pink macules on trunk in Typhoid fever.
  - Pea-Soup Stools: Characteristic watery green-brown diarrhea in severe Typhoid fever.
  - Pseudomembranes: Yellow-white raised mucosal plaques pathognomonic for C. difficile colitis.
  - Ecthyma Gangrenosum-like Bullae / Vibriosis: Bullous skin lesions in V. vulnificus sepsis from raw oysters.
  - Attaching and Effacing (A/E) Lesions: Intimin/Tir-mediated actin pedestals beneath EPEC and STEC.
  - Brainerd Diarrhea: Idiopathic chronic watery diarrhea outbreak lasting >6 months linked to raw milk or unchlorinated water.
  - Pigbel / Darmbrand: Enteritis Necroticans caused by Clostridium perfringens type C beta-toxin in sweet potato/pork feasts.
  - Faget Sign / Relative Bradycardia: Pulse-temperature dissociation in Typhoid fever.
  - "All Blood and No Stool": Grossly bloody diarrheal fluid in STEC O157:H7 hemorrhagic colitis.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Antibiotic Contraindication in STEC / EHEC: Giving antibiotics (fluoroquinolones, beta-lactams) to patients with STEC O157:H7 increases Shiga toxin production via SOS response activation and INCREASES the risk of HUS by 17-fold; antibiotics MUST be withheld!
  - Absence of Fever in STEC Hemorrhagic Colitis: Unlike other dysenteric enteropathogens (Shigella, Salmonella, C. jejuni), STEC hemorrhagic colitis characteristically presents with MINIMAL OR NO FEVER in >70% of cases!
  - Antibiotic Avoidance in Uncomplicated Nontyphoidal Salmonella: Giving antibiotics for uncomplicated NTS gastroenteritis in healthy hosts does NOT shorten symptom duration and PROLONGS stool carriage/excretion of Salmonella; antibiotics are restricted to high-risk hosts!
  - Loperamide Contraindication in STEC & Dysentery: Antimotility agents (loperamide) are STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED in STEC (increases Shiga toxin absorption and HUS risk) and in febrile bloody dysentery / C. difficile (risk of toxic megacolon)!
  - Fluoroquinolone Resistance in Traveler's Diarrhea: Ciprofloxacin is NO LONGER first-line empiric self-treatment for traveler's diarrhea to South/Southeast Asia (e.g., Thailand) due to >80-90% fluoroquinolone resistance in Campylobacter and ETEC; Azithromycin is the preferred first-line agent!
  - Normal Stool Testing in Brainerd Diarrhea: Brainerd diarrhea produces severe watery diarrhea lasting >6-12 months without any identifiable pathogen on routine or specialized stool testing!
  - Chloramphenicol Reversible vs Irreversible Bone Marrow Toxicity: Chloramphenicol causes dose-dependent reversible bone marrow suppression, but uncommonly causes fatal idiosyncratic aplastic anemia (1 in 10,000 to 40,000)!
