# Dysentery

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Dysentery is an acute or chronic inflammatory infection of the gastrointestinal tract, specifically involving the colonic mucosa and distal ileum, clinically characterized by frequent, small-volume stools containing gross blood, mucus, and inflammatory exudate (pus), accompanied by fever, severe colicky abdominal cramps, and tenesmus (painful, distressing, ineffective straining to defecate). It represents a destructive, invasive, or cytotoxic enteropathy that destroys the intestinal epithelial architecture and disrupts normal mucosal fluid and electrolyte absorption and secretion.
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. By Etiologic / Pathologic Type: (a) Bacillary Dysentery (Infectious bacterial invasion and cytotoxicity causing intense superficial mucosal inflammation and ulceration; predominantly caused by Shigella species (S. flexneri, S. sonnei, S. dysenteriae type 1, S. boydii), Enteroinvasive E. coli (EIEC), Campylobacter jejuni, Nontyphoidal Salmonella enterica, and Yersinia enterocolitica); (b) Amebic Dysentery (Invasive parasitic disease caused by Entamoeba histolytica, characterized by deeper, penetrating, flask-shaped mucosal ulcers with undermined margins and minimal surrounding neutrophilic response); (c) Ciliary Dysentery (Parasitic dysentery caused by Balantidium coli, a large ciliated protozoan acquired via swine/pig exposure); (d) Viral Dysentery / Colitis (Severe inflammatory enteritis in immunocompromised hosts, e.g., Cytomegalovirus (CMV) colitis).
  2. By Clinical Presentation & Severity: (a) Acute Febrile Dysentery (Acute onset of bloody/mucoid diarrhea with high fever, systemic toxicity, severe cramps, and tenesmus); (b) Hemorrhagic Colitis / Minimal-Fever Dysentery (Grossly bloody diarrhea with severe abdominal cramps but characteristically absent or minimal fever, prototypical for Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC / EHEC) such as O157:H7 and O104:H4); (c) Anorectal Dysentery / Proctitis (Inflammation limited to the rectum and distal colon, frequently acquired via venereal/anal intercourse, e.g., Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV), Chlamydia trachomatis (LGV serotypes L1-L3), Treponema pallidum); (d) Chronic or Relapsing Dysentery (Symptoms persisting for >2 to 4 weeks or recurring, seen in amebiasis, gastrointestinal tuberculosis, Schistosomiasis (bilharzioma), or noninfectious mimics like Inflammatory Bowel Disease (Ulcerative Colitis, Crohn's disease)).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Global Burden: Shigella species alone infect >200 million people and cause approximately 650,000 deaths worldwide each year, predominantly affecting children in resource-limited regions of Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. In the US, nontyphoidal Salmonella, Campylobacter, Yersinia enterocolitica, and STEC O157 are the leading reported bacterial causes of bloody diarrhea in young children.
  - Low Infectious Inoculum & Person-to-Person Transmission: Shigella species and Entamoeba histolytica require an exceptionally small infectious dose (as few as 10 to 100 bacterial cells or 10 amebic cysts). Consequently, direct person-to-person spread is common in daycare centers, custodial institutions, crowded residential settings, and refugee camps.
  - Dietary & Environmental Exposures: Consumption of unpasteurized milk or raw dairy (Listeria, Campylobacter, Salmonella, STEC); undercooked poultry (Campylobacter jejuni); undercooked ground beef or contaminated produce (STEC O157:H7, Cyclospora); raw/undercooked pork or chitterlings (Yersinia enterocolitica); raw/undercooked seafood or saltwater exposure (Vibrio parahaemolyticus); untreated/unchlorinated water or contaminated well water (E. histolytica, Cryptosporidium, Brainerd diarrhea agent).
  - High-Risk Populations & Host Factors: Malnourished children (high risk for severe shigellosis, enteric protein loss, and stunting); men who have sex with men (MSM; increased risk of venereal proctitis/colitis via N. gonorrhoeae, HSV, C. trachomatis, T. pallidum, and oral-anal transmission of Shigella, E. histolytica, and C. jejuni); recent antibiotic use or hospitalization (Clostridioides difficile colitis); iron-overload states, hemochromatosis, or cirrhosis (predisposes to severe invasive Yersinia enterocolitica bacteremia); and advanced HIV/AIDS or immunosuppression (predisposes to CMV colitis, MAC, persistent Cryptosporidium, and recurrent Salmonella bacteremia).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Shigella species: S. flexneri and S. sonnei are the most prevalent worldwide; S. dysenteriae type 1 (producer of Shiga toxin) causes severe epidemic dysentery; S. boydii is concentrated in the Indian subcontinent. (Genomic studies show Shigella and EIEC form a single E. coli pathovar).
  - Campylobacter species: C. jejuni and C. coli (leading bacterial cause of diarrheal illness in developed nations; associated with poultry).
  - Nontyphoidal Salmonella enterica (NTS): S. Typhimurium, S. Enteritidis, and other serovars.
  - Enteroinvasive Escherichia coli (EIEC) and Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC / EHEC, e.g., O157:H7, O104:H4).
  - Entamoeba histolytica (the primary protozoal cause of amebic dysentery and amebic liver abscess).
  - Clostridioides difficile (toxigenic strains producing Toxin A and Toxin B).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Infants & Children (<5 years): In low-income countries (GEMS and MAL-ED studies), Shigella species and Campylobacter species are the primary bacterial drivers of dysentery in the 2nd year of life (12-23 months), while Rotavirus, Norovirus GII, Adenovirus 40/41, and Cryptosporidium dominate the 1st year of life. In the US, the leading bacterial agents in children <5 years are Nontyphoidal Salmonella, followed by Campylobacter spp., Yersinia enterocolitica, and E. coli O157:H7 (children <5 years have the highest incidence of STEC-associated HUS).
  - Older Children & Adolescents: Shigella flexneri/sonnei, Campylobacter jejuni, Nontyphoidal Salmonella, STEC, Yersinia enterocolitica (frequently presenting as pseudoappendicular syndrome / ileocecitis).
  - Adults & Elderly (>65 years): Clostridioides difficile (leading cause of hospital-acquired inflammatory colitis and diarrhea mortality in the elderly), Campylobacter jejuni, Nontyphoidal Salmonella (high risk for bacteremia), STEC (high mortality in elderly with HUS (33%) or hemorrhagic colitis alone (2%)), Entamoeba histolytica, and Vibrio parahaemolyticus.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Bacteria & Spirochetes: Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi and Paratyphi (cause enteric/typhoid fever, which can present with inflammatory diarrhea and Peyer's patch ulceration/hemorrhage in 10-20% of cases); Vibrio parahaemolyticus and non-O1/non-O139 Vibrio cholerae; Aeromonas hydrophila; Plesiomonas shigelloides; Clostridium perfringens type C (causes Enteritis Necroticans / Pigbel / Darmbrand, marked by segmental ischemic necrosis of the small bowel); Cytotoxic Klebsiella oxytoca (antibiotic-associated hemorrhagic colitis); Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia trachomatis (LGV serotypes L1-L3), Treponema pallidum, and Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1/2) causing infectious proctitis; Mycobacterium tuberculosis (gastrointestinal TB causing ulcerative/hypertrophic ileocecal disease); Spirillum species and human intestinal spirochetes (Treponema hyodysenteriae-like agents); and Listeria monocytogenes.
  - Protozoa & Parasites: Balantidium coli (ciliary dysentery from pig contact); Cryptosporidium hominis/parvum; Cystoisospora belli; Cyclospora cayetanensis; Microsporidia (Enterocytozoon bieneusi, Encephalitozoon intestinalis); Schistosoma mansoni and Schistosoma japonicum (intestinal schistosomiasis causing mucosal bilharziomas); Strongyloides stercoralis hyperinfection; and Angiostrongylus costaricensis.
  - Viruses (Immunocompromised Hosts): Cytomegalovirus (CMV colitis with deep mucosal ulceration and perforation in HIV/AIDS with CD4 <50 cells/uL or transplant recipients).
  - Noninfectious Mimics & Enigmatic Syndromes: Inflammatory Bowel Disease (Ulcerative Colitis, Crohn's disease), Ischemic Colitis, Radiation Enteritis, Allergic / Eosinophilic Gastroenteritis, and Brainerd Diarrhea (idiopathic chronic watery/inflammatory diarrhea lasting >4 weeks to months linked to unpasteurized milk or raw water).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Route & Inoculum Dose: Ingestion of contaminated food or water or direct fecal-oral person-to-person transfer. Low inoculum pathogens (Shigella, E. histolytica, STEC requiring only 10 to 100 organisms/cysts) easily breach host defenses and spread rapidly. High inoculum pathogens (Salmonella, V. parahaemolyticus requiring 10^5 to 10^8 organisms) require multiplication in food vehicles.
  - Epithelial Transcytosis & Basolateral Invasion: Invasive bacteria (Shigella, EIEC) cross the intestinal barrier predominantly via microfold (M) cells overlying Peyer's patches in the distal ileum and colon. Shigella transcytoses through M cells, enters underlying macrophages, induces macrophage apoptosis via caspase-1 activation, and escapes into the basolateral interstitial space.
  - Type III Secretion Systems (T3SS) & Intracellular Propulsion: Shigella and EIEC use a T3SS (encoded on a large virulence plasmid) to inject effector proteins into basolateral membranes of colonic epithelial cells, inducing actin membrane ruffling and macropinocytosis. Once inside the enterocyte cytoplasm, Shigella lyses the phagosomal membrane, replicates cytosolically, and uses its outer membrane protein IcsA (VirG) to catalyze host cell F-actin polymerization at one bacterial pole. This propels the bacteria laterally through the cytoplasm into adjacent enterocytes without entering the extracellular environment, evading humoral antibodies.
  - Cytotoxin Action:
    1. Shiga Toxin (Stx / Verotoxin) produced by S. dysenteriae type 1 and STEC (O157:H7, O104:H4): An AB5 subunit toxin whose B pentamer binds cell-surface globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) receptors (abundant on microvascular endothelial cells in gut mucosa and renal glomeruli). The A subunit is internalized, cleaves an adenine residue from 28S rRNA of the 60S ribosomal subunit, halts eukaryotic protein synthesis, and induces microvascular endothelial cell apoptosis, microvascular thrombosis, hemorrhagic colitis, and systemic Hemolytic-Uremic Syndrome (HUS).
    2. C. difficile Toxins A (TcdA) and B (TcdB): Glucosyltransferases that covalently modify Rho/Rac/Cdc42 GTPases, causing F-actin depolymerization, disruption of tight junctions, massive neutrophilic infiltration, epithelial cell death, and pseudomembrane formation.
  - Amebic Invasion (Entamoeba histolytica): Cysts excyst in the small intestine to release trophozoites. Trophozoites adhere to colonic mucin and epithelial cells via Gal/GalNAc lectins, secrete pore-forming amoebapores and cysteine proteinases, lyse enterocytes and neutrophils upon direct contact (contact-dependent cytolysis), degrade extracellular matrix, and penetrate deep into the submucosa.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Superficial Inflammatory Colitis (Bacillary Dysentery): Shigella, EIEC, C. jejuni, and NTS induce an intense acute neutrophilic inflammatory reaction in the mucosa and submucosa. Neutrophil infiltration (crypt abscesses) and local release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1beta, IL-8, TNF-alpha) cause microvascular damage, superficial epithelial mucosal desquamation, focal ulcerations (3 to 7 mm), and purulent exudate formation.
  - Flask-Shaped Deep Ulceration (Amebic Dysentery): Entamoeba histolytica trophozoites erode through the epithelium and spread laterally in the submucosa, limited by the resistant muscularis propria layer. This creates classic "flask-shaped" ulcers with narrow necks and broad, undermined bases containing necrotic tissue, amebic trophozoites, and minimal surrounding neutrophilic response (due to amebic lysis of infiltrating neutrophils).
  - Pseudomembranous Colitis (C. difficile): Severe mucosal necrosis and intense neutrophilic exudation produce raised, 2 to 8 mm yellow-white, button-like inflammatory plaques (pseudomembranes composed of fibrin, mucin, necrotic enterocytes, and neutrophils) overlying erythematous colonic mucosa.
  - Protein-Losing Enteropathy & Mucosal Destruction: Severe shigellosis causes significant enteric protein loss into the gut lumen, contributing to acute kwashiorkor, worsening malnutrition, secondary bacteremia, and childhood growth stunting.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Classic Dysenteric Triad: Frequent, small-volume stools containing gross blood, mucus, and pus; high fever (38.5°C to 40°C); and severe colicky abdominal cramps with tenesmus (painful, distressing, ineffective straining to defecate).
  - Prodromal & Associated Symptoms: Disease often begins with a brief prodrome of watery diarrhea and high fever, which shifts within 12 to 48 hours to bloody, mucoid dysentery. Accompanied by severe anorexia, nausea, vomiting, malaise, headache, and toxic appearance.
  - Hemorrhagic Colitis Presentation (STEC): Severe, agonizing abdominal cramps followed by watery diarrhea that rapidly turns grossly bloody ("all blood and no stool") within 24 to 48 hours. Characteristically accompanied by MINIMAL OR NO FEVER in >70% of cases!
  - Amebic Dysentery Presentation: Insidious or gradual onset of bloody, mucoid diarrhea, lower abdominal pain, and tenesmus, often developing over 1 to 4 weeks; fever is absent or low-grade in most cases (unlike acute bacillary dysentery).
  - Anorectal / Proctitis Symptoms: Burning rectal pain, tenesmus, mucopurulent or bloody rectal discharge, and constipation or small-volume bloody stools (N. gonorrhoeae, HSV, C. trachomatis).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - General & Abdominal Signs: Febrile, toxic appearance, tachycardia, dry mucous membranes, and signs of dehydration; abdominal palpation reveals diffuse lower abdominal tenderness (most pronounced over the sigmoid colon), voluntary guarding, hyperactive bowel sounds, or focal rebound tenderness.
  - Extraintestinal Physical Findings:
    1. Systemic Shigellosis Signs: Meningismus, lethargy, confusion, or seizures in young children (associated with Shiga neurotoxin or high fever); purpura fulminans / petechiae and pallor (if HUS develops).
    2. C. difficile Colitis Signs: Marked abdominal distention, hypoactive bowel sounds, fever, and left/right lower quadrant tenderness.
    3. Amebic Colitis & Liver Abscess: Right upper quadrant tenderness, hepatomegaly, and jaundiced or septic appearance if complicated by amebic liver abscess.
    4. Proctitis / STI Examination Findings: Anorectal mucosal friability, purulent discharge in the rectal vault, perianal vesicular/ulcerative lesions (HSV), or inguinal lymphadenopathy (LGV / Syphilis).
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Stools of "Blood, Mucus, and Pus" with Tenesmus: Pathognomonic bedside stool appearance for acute Bacillary Dysentery (Shigellosis).
  - Flask-Shaped Submucosal Ulcers on Histology/Endoscopy: Pathognomonic endoscopic/histopathologic appearance for invasive Entamoeba histolytica amebic colitis.
  - Raised Yellow-White Pseudomembranous Plaques: Raised 2 to 8 mm yellow-white mucosal plaques on sigmoidoscopy pathognomonic for Clostridioides difficile colitis.
  - "All Blood and No Stool" with Minimal Fever: Grossly bloody diarrheal fluid with severe cramps and absence of fever pathognomonic for Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC O157:H7) hemorrhagic colitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Marked leukocytosis with a left shift (bandemia >10-20%) in acute bacillary dysentery and severe C. difficile colitis (unexplained leukemoid reaction WBC >30,000 to 50,000/mm3 indicates severe CDI). Leukopenia or normal WBC count may occur in amebic dysentery or typhoid fever.
  - Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia & Thrombocytopenia: Schistocytes / helmet cells on peripheral blood smear, low platelet count (<100,000/mm3), and acute renal failure (elevated serum creatinine/BUN) indicate Hemolytic-Uremic Syndrome (HUS) complicating STEC or S. dysenteriae type 1.
  - Fecal Inflammatory Biomarkers:
    1. Fecal Leukocyte Microscopy (Methylene Blue / Gram Stain): Direct microscopic examination demonstrates abundant sheets of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs) in clumps of mucus in bacillary dysentery (Shigella, C. jejuni, NTS, EIEC). In contrast, amebic dysentery exhibits markedly FEWER pyknotic leukocytes due to contact-dependent amebic destruction/lysis of host neutrophils!
    2. Fecal Lactoferrin & Fecal Calprotectin Immunoassays: Highly elevated in active inflammatory colitis, distinguishing invasive bacterial/clostridial dysentery from noninflammatory secretory diarrhea.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Abdominal Plain Radiography: Evaluates for toxic megacolon (colonic diameter >6 cm), paralytic ileus, or free air under the diaphragm (pneumoperitoneum indicating colonic perforation).
  - Computed Tomography (CT) of Abdomen/Pelvis: Modality of choice for complicated colitis, neutropenic enterocolitis (typhlitis), or suspected abscess. Demonstrates diffuse colonic wall thickening (>4 mm), submucosal edema ("accordion sign" in C. difficile colitis), pericolic fat stranding, pneumatosis intestinalis, or liver abscesses.
  - Endoscopy (Flexible Sigmoidoscopy / Colonoscopy) & Biopsy:
    1. Shigellosis / EIEC: Widespread, diffuse, intense mucosal inflammation with friability, edema, contact bleeding, and shallow 3 to 7 mm mucosal ulcerations covered by purulent exudate.
    2. Amebic Colitis: Discrete, small, "flask-shaped" mucosal ulcerations with raised, undermined edges and relatively normal intervening colonic mucosa; PAS stain of biopsy tissue confirms amebic trophozoites with ingested erythrocytes (erythrophagocytosis).
    3. C. difficile Colitis: Raised 2 to 8 mm yellow-white pseudomembranous plaques overlying erythematous mucosa.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Stool Culture & Isolation: Stool culture on selective/differential enteric media (MacConkey, Hektoen Enteric, XLD, SS agar) is essential for isolating Shigella, Nontyphoidal Salmonella, and EIEC. Isolation of Campylobacter jejuni requires selective Campy-BAP medium incubated at 42°C in a microaerophilic atmosphere (5% O2, 10% CO2, 85% N2). E. coli O157:H7 isolation requires Sorbitol-MacConkey (SMAC) agar (sorbitol-negative pale colonies). Vibrio parahaemolyticus requires TCBS agar. Yersinia enterocolitica requires cold enrichment or CIN agar.
  - Multiplex Molecular PCR Panels (BioFire GI Panel, FilmArray, TaqMan Array Card): Rapid, simultaneous detection of bacterial targets (Shigella/EIEC, C. jejuni, Salmonella, STEC stx1/stx2, ETEC, C. difficile), viral targets (Norovirus, Rotavirus), and parasites (E. histolytica, Cryptosporidium, Giardia).
  - Amebiasis Testing: Stool wet mount microscopy (low sensitivity <60%, cannot distinguish E. histolytica from nonpathogenic E. dispar); Serum / Stool Gal/GalNAc Lectin Antigen Capture ELISA (>95% sensitive) or E. histolytica PCR accurately differentiates virulent E. histolytica from E. dispar.
  - C. difficile Testing: Stool GDH antigen / PCR testing combined with Toxin A/B EIA immunoassay.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Stool Microbiological Culture demonstrating isolation and antimicrobial susceptibility of the specific bacterial pathogen (or validated NAAT / PCR / antigen detection identifying virulent E. histolytica or Shiga toxins) combined with clinical/endoscopic evidence of mucosal inflammation represents the gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Fluid & Electrolyte Resuscitation: Immediate oral rehydration therapy (WHO-ORS solution) or intravenous fluid resuscitation (Lactated Ringer's or normal saline) to correct hypovolemia, metabolic acidosis, and electrolyte losses.
  - Nutritional Support & Micronutrients: Continued age-appropriate feeding; Oral Zinc Supplementation (20 mg/day for 14 days in children; 10 mg/day for infants <6 months) significantly shortens dysentery duration, promotes weight gain, and reduces 6-month diarrheal morbidity. Single-dose Vitamin A (200,000 IU) reduces shigellosis severity and mortality in malnourished children.
  - STRICT CONTRAINDICATION of Antimotility Agents: Antimotility agents (Loperamide, Diphenoxylate-Atropine / Lomotil) are STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED in acute febrile dysentery, STEC infection, and C. difficile colitis! Inhibiting peristalsis enhances bacterial enterotoxin/Shiga toxin absorption, worsens toxic megacolon, increases HUS risk, and prolongs fever and infection!
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Indications: Empiric antimicrobial therapy is strongly recommended for acute febrile dysentery (bloody diarrhea with fever), hospitalized children, severe illness, or high-risk hosts.
  - Adult First-Line Empiric Regimen for Acute Febrile Dysentery:
    - Azithromycin 1000 mg PO single dose OR 500 mg PO daily for 3 days (PREFERRED globally, especially for international travel or areas with high fluoroquinolone resistance in Shigella and Campylobacter).
    - Alternative Adult Regimen (Low-Resistance Areas): Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO bid for 3 days OR Levofloxacin 500 mg PO daily for 3 days.
    - Severe / Hospitalized Parenteral Regimen: Ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV q24h x 3 to 5 days.
  - Pediatric First-Line Empiric Regimen:
    - Azithromycin 10 to 12 mg/kg PO daily (max 500 mg) for 3 days OR Ciprofloxacin 15 mg/kg PO bid (max 500 mg bid) for 3 days OR Ceftriaxone 50 to 100 mg/kg IV q24h for 3 days.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Pathogen-Directed Targeted Therapies:
    - Shigellosis (Shigella spp. / EIEC): Azithromycin 500 mg PO daily x 3 days OR Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO bid x 3 days (if susceptible) OR Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h x 3 days. (Reduces fever, dysentery duration, and fecal shedding).
    - Campylobacter jejuni: Azithromycin 500 mg PO daily x 3 days (indicated for severe, bloody, or prolonged dysentery).
    - Shiga Toxin-Producing E. coli (STEC / EHEC): ANTIBIOTICS ARE CONTRAINDICATED! Antibiotics (especially fluoroquinolones and beta-lactams) induce Stx-encoding prophages, trigger massive Shiga toxin release, and increase HUS risk by 17-fold! Therapy is strictly supportive fluid expansion.
    - Amebic Dysentery (Entamoeba histolytica): Metronidazole 750 mg PO tid x 7 to 10 days (or Tinidazole 2 g PO daily x 3 days) FOLLOWED MANDATORILY BY a luminal amebicide: Paromomycin 30 mg/kg/day PO in 3 divided doses x 7 days (or Iodoquinol 650 mg tid x 20 days) to eradicate residual intestinal cysts and prevent relapse!
    - Clostridioides difficile Colitis: Oral Fidaxomicin 200 mg PO bid x 10 days OR Oral Vancomycin 125 mg PO qid x 10 days.
    - Gastrointestinal Tuberculosis: 4-drug RIPE regimen (Rifampin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol) x 2 months, then RI x 4 to 7 months.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Emergency Colectomy / Laparotomy Indications:
    1. Intestinal Perforation: Emergency laparotomy with segmental resection or stoma creation for colonic or ileal perforation (occurs in severe typhoid fever, C. difficile colitis, amebic colitis, or toxic megacolon).
    2. Toxic Megacolon & Fulminant C. difficile / Amebic Colitis: Emergency subtotal colectomy with end ileostomy for progressive colonic dilation (>6 cm), severe toxicity, or rising lactate >5 mmol/L.
    3. Intestinal Obstruction or Strictures: Surgical relief of colonic obstruction occurring in ~3% of pediatric shigellosis cases or late cicatricial stenosis following Enteritis Necroticans (Pigbel) or amebomas.
    4. Percutaneous Drainage of Amebic / Pyogenic Liver Abscesses: Image-guided catheter drainage for large amebic liver abscesses (>5 cm, left lobe risk of pericardial rupture) failing 48 to 72 hours of metronidazole therapy.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Hemolytic-Uremic Syndrome (HUS): Triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute renal failure occurring ~1 week post-dysentery in 6% of STEC cases (15% in children <5 years) and S. dysenteriae type 1.
  - Intestinal Perforation, Severe Gastrointestinal Hemorrhage, and Toxic Megacolon.
  - Intestinal Obstruction (~3% in childhood shigellosis).
  - Extraintestinal Neurological Complications: Lethargy, confusion, meningismus, and acute seizures in young children with shigellosis (mediated by Shiga neurotoxin or high fever).
  - Severe Enteric Protein Loss & Acute Malnutrition (Kwashiorkor).
  - Secondary Bacteremia & Sepsis (Salmonella, C. fetus, Shigella in immunocompromised/malnourished hosts).
  - High Case Fatality: Epidemic S. dysenteriae type 1 carries mortality rates up to 10% to 20% in malnourished children; HUS carries 3% mortality in children and 33% in elderly.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Reactive Arthritis (formerly Reiter Syndrome): Sterile HLA-B27-associated asymmetric oligoarthritis, conjunctivitis, and urethritis occurring 2 to 5 weeks after dysentery caused by Shigella flexneri, Salmonella enterica, Campylobacter jejuni, Yersinia enterocolitica, or C. difficile.
  - Postinfectious Irritable Bowel Syndrome (PI-IBS): Chronic abdominal pain and altered bowel habits developing in 10% to 30% of survivors following acute dysentery.
  - Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS): Acute demyelinating polyneuropathy triggered by C. jejuni molecular mimicry.
  - Environmental Enteric Dysfunction (EED), Chronic Malabsorption, and Childhood Stunting.
  - Late Intestinal Strictures & Stenosis (following Pigbel or amebiasis).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Blood, Mucus, and Pus: Classic triad of fecal contents defining Bacillary Dysentery.
  - Tenesmus: Painful, distressing, ineffective straining to defecate.
  - Flask-Shaped Ulcers: Submucosa-undermining colonic ulcers characteristic of Entamoeba histolytica amebic dysentery.
  - Pseudomembranes: Yellow-white raised mucosal plaques pathognomonic for C. difficile colitis.
  - Shiga Toxin (Stx): Ribosome-inactivating AB5 cytotoxin causing vascular endothelial cell destruction and HUS.
  - Pigbel / Darmbrand: Enteritis Necroticans caused by Clostridium perfringens type C beta-toxin in sweet potato/pork feasts in Papua New Guinea.
  - "All Blood and No Stool": Grossly bloody diarrheal fluid in STEC O157:H7 hemorrhagic colitis.
  - Erythrophagocytosis: Endoscopic biopsy/wet mount finding of E. histolytica trophozoites containing ingested red blood cells.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Antibiotic Contraindication in STEC / EHEC Dysentery: Giving antibiotics (fluoroquinolones, beta-lactams) to patients with STEC O157:H7 increases Shiga toxin production via SOS response activation and INCREASES the risk of HUS by 17-fold; antibiotics MUST be withheld!
  - Absence of Fever in STEC Hemorrhagic Colitis: Unlike Shigella, Salmonella, and C. jejuni dysentery, STEC hemorrhagic colitis characteristically presents with MINIMAL OR NO FEVER in >70% of cases!
  - Fecal Leukocyte Microscopy Difference (Shigella vs. Amebiasis): Bacillary dysentery (Shigella) presents with abundant sheets of intact PMNs on stool methylene blue stain. In contrast, Amebic dysentery presents with FEWER pyknotic leukocytes due to contact-dependent cytolytic destruction of host neutrophils by E. histolytica trophozoites!
  - Mandatory Two-Step Amebiasis Treatment: Treating amebic dysentery with metronidazole alone is INSUFFICIENT; metronidazole treats tissue trophozoites but fails to eradicate luminal cysts. A luminal amebicide (Paromomycin or Iodoquinol) MUST follow metronidazole to prevent relapse!
  - Antimotility Agent Contraindication: Loperamide and Lomotil are STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED in dysentery and STEC colitis due to risks of toxic megacolon and increased Shiga toxin absorption!
  - Fluoroquinolone Resistance in Traveler's Dysentery: Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin) are no longer reliable empiric therapy for dysentery acquired in South/Southeast Asia due to widespread resistance in Campylobacter and Shigella; Azithromycin is the preferred first-line agent!
