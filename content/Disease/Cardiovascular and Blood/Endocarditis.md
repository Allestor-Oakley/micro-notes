# Endocarditis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Infective endocarditis (IE) is an infection of the endocardial surface of the heart, characterized by the physical presence of microorganisms within endocardial lesions (vegetations). It most commonly affects native or prosthetic heart valves, but may also occur on mural endocardium, chordae tendineae, eustachian valves, ventricular septal defects, or endovascular shunts (arteriovenous shunts, patent ductus arteriosus, or coarctation of the aorta). The term infective endocarditis is preferred over bacterial endocarditis because fungi, rickettsiae, chlamydiae, and other nonbacterial pathogens can cause the syndrome. The broader clinical spectrum also encompasses nonvalvular cardiovascular device infections, including cardiovascular implantable electronic devices (CIEDs such as permanent pacemakers and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators), left ventricular assist devices (LVADs), prosthetic vascular grafts (PVGI), percutaneous vascular closure devices (PVCD), and myocardial suture line devices.
- **Major Classifications (e.g., Acute vs. Chronic, Community vs. Nosocomial):**
  - Clinical Velocity / Severity:
    - Acute Endocarditis: Rapidly progressive, fulminant infection occurring over days to weeks, typically caused by virulent pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes, or Streptococcus pneumoniae. Characterized by high fever, severe systemic toxicity, rapid valvular destruction, early heart failure, and acute embolic phenomena.
    - Subacute Endocarditis: Indolent infection evolving over weeks to months, typically caused by less virulent organisms such as Viridans group streptococci, Enterococcus species, Coagulase-negative staphylococci, or HACEK organisms. Characterized by low-grade fever, fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, and prominent immunological stigmata (Osler nodes, Roth spots, glomerulonephritis).
    - Chronic Endocarditis: Insidious infection evolving over months to years, often blood culture-negative or caused by fastidious intracellular pathogens such as Coxiella burnetii (Q fever endocarditis), Bartonella species, or Tropheryma whipplei.
  - Substrate / Valve Type & Acquisition Setting:
    - Native Valve Endocarditis (NVE): Involves previously normal or diseased native heart valves (left-sided: mitral valve > aortic valve > combined mitral-aortic; right-sided: tricuspid valve > pulmonic valve). Subdivided into community-acquired native valve IE versus health care-associated native valve IE (HCAIE, arising from intravenous catheters, hemodialysis shunts, hyperalimentation, or chemotherapy lines).
    - Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE): Categorized by onset timing post-implantation: (1) Early-Onset PVE, manifesting within 1 year of valve replacement (peak incidence within initial 2 months), caused by intraoperative or perioperative contamination (CoNS, S. aureus, diphtheroids, Gram-negative bacilli, Candida); and (2) Late-Onset PVE, manifesting more than 1 year post-implantation, caused by community-acquired or healthcare-associated bacteremia (Viridans streptococci, S. aureus, Enterococci, CoNS, HACEK).
    - Right-Sided Endocarditis: Involves tricuspid or pulmonic valves; predominantly seen in people who inject drugs (PWID), patients with central venous catheters, or congenital heart disease. Characterized by septic pulmonary emboli, persistent fever, and a lower incidence of peripheral embolic/immunological stigmata.
    - Nonvalvular Cardiovascular Device Infections: (1) CIED Infections, categorized as pocket infections vs. lead-associated endocarditis (vegetations on leads, tricuspid valve, or right atrial wall), and early-onset (<6 months post-implantation, from surgical contamination) vs. late-onset (>6 months, from hematogenous seeding); (2) LVAD Infections, categorized as LVAD-specific (driveline exit site, pump pocket, cannula) vs. LVAD-related (endocarditis, bacteremia, mediastinitis).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Epidemiology: Annual incidence ranges from 3 to 12 cases per 100,000 population in industrialized nations (reported at ~12 per 100,000 in recent US data). Prosthetic valve endocarditis accounts for 16% to 33% of all definite IE cases. Health care-associated IE now accounts for approximately one-third of all cases. Overall in-hospital mortality ranges from 15% to 22%, and 1-year mortality reaches 30% to 40% (up to 48% in S. aureus PVE).
  - High-Risk Predisposing Cardiac Conditions (highest lifetime risk and worst prognosis): Previous episode of infective endocarditis (lifetime risk 110 to 440 times normal population), mechanical or bioprosthetic valve implants (lifetime risk 225 to 900 times normal), surgically constructed systemic or pulmonary shunts/conduits, unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease, repaired cyanotic CHD with residual defects/shunts, or within 6 months of complete repair using prosthetic material.
  - Moderate-Risk & Other Predisposing Risk Factors: Rheumatic heart disease (historically primary, now declining in developed nations), mitral valve prolapse with regurgitation or leaflet thickening, bicuspid aortic valve, degenerative calcific valvular disease, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, indwelling central venous catheters, hemodialysis shunts/fistulas, intravenous drug use, active inflammatory bowel disease, poor oral hygiene/periodontal disease, and immunosuppression (organ transplant, diabetes mellitus, chronic renal failure). In PWID, risk is elevated by contamination from skin/salivary flora, drug paraphernalia, and co-infection with HIV.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Staphylococcus aureus: The single leading cause of IE in the industrialized world (30% to 45% of native valve cases, up to 75% in PWID right-sided IE, and 20% to 40% of PVE). Includes MSSA and MRSA.
  - Viridans Group Streptococci (VGS): Accounts for 20% to 30% of native valve cases (Streptococcus sanguinis, S. mitis, S. oralis, S. mutans, S. salivarius, S. gordonii). Often penicillin-susceptible (MIC <=0.12 mcg/mL) or relatively penicillin-resistant. Granulicatella adiacens and Abiotrophia defectiva (formerly nutritionally variant streptococci) cause ~2% of cases.
  - Enterococci: Enterococcus faecalis (90% of enterococcal IE) and Enterococcus faecium (10%) account for 5% to 18% of cases overall (increasing frequency in elderly men after GU manipulation and younger women after OB procedures).
  - Coagulase-Negative Staphylococci (CoNS): Staphylococcus epidermidis, Staphylococcus lugdunensis (highly virulent native valve pathogen), Staphylococcus hominis, S. capitis. Main cause of early PVE (30% to 40%) and CIED lead infections (42%).
  - Streptococcus gallolyticus (formerly Streptococcus bovis): Accounts for 6% to 10% of cases, strongly associated with underlying colonic neoplasms, polyps, or GI vascular lesions.
  - HACEK Group Organisms: Fastidious Gram-negative bacilli accounting for 1% to 3% of cases: Haemophilus species (H. parainfluenzae, H. influenzae), Aggregatibacter aphrophilus (formerly Actinobacillus), Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Cardiobacterium hominis, Eikenella corrodens, and Kingella kingae.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups (e.g., Neonates, Elderly):**
  - Neonates & Infants: Group B Streptococcus (Streptococcus agalactiae), Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Candida species (especially with central line access or congenital heart disease).
  - Children & Adolescents: Viridans group streptococci, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes, and HACEK organisms.
  - Young Adults & Sexually Active / PWID: S. aureus (predominant in PWID, tricuspid valve), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PWID using Ts and blues), Streptococci, polymicrobial, Neisseria gonorrhoeae (rare).
  - Elderly (>65 years) & Healthcare-Exposed: Enterococcus faecalis, S. aureus (high MRSA rate), CoNS, Streptococcus gallolyticus, and aerobic Gram-negative bacilli (E. coli, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas aeruginosa).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Fastidious / Culture-Negative / Zoonotic Bacteria: Coxiella burnetii (Q fever endocarditis; phase I IgG >1:800), Bartonella henselae and Bartonella quintana (homeless, alcoholic, louse-borne, cat scratch exposure), Tropheryma whipplei (Whipple disease endocarditis), Chlamydia psittaci / C. pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila / L. micdadei, Brucella abortus / B. melitensis, Mycoplasma hominis, Corynebacterium species (diphtheroids like C. jeikeium, C. striatum), Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae, Listeria monocytogenes, Spirillum minus / Streptobacillus moniliformis, and Mycobacterium species (M. chimaera outbreaks from heater-cooler units, M. fortuitum, M. chelonae).
  - Fungi: Candida species (Candida albicans, Candida parapsilosis in PWID/CVCs, C. tropicalis, C. glabrata), Aspergillus species (A. fumigatus, A. flavus, A. niger; common in open heart surgery/PVE, immunosuppression), Histoplasma capsulatum, Coccidioides immitis, Blastomyces dermatitidis, Cryptococcus neoformans, Scedosporium, and Exserohilum. Fungal IE produces large, bulky vegetations with frequent major emboli and high mortality.
  - Non-HACEK Gram-Negative Bacilli: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter, Serratia marcescens, Acinetobacter species, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Endothelial Damage & NBTE Formation: Turbulent blood flow (from valvular lesions, high-velocity jets, prosthetic materials, or catheters) strips or damages the endothelial lining of heart valves or mural endocardium. Deposition of host platelets and fibrin at the site of endothelial injury forms a sterile microthrombus termed Nonbacterial Thrombotic Endocarditis (NBTE).
  - Transient Bacteremia & Microbial Adherence: Mucosal trauma (dental work, chewing, GI/GU procedures) or intravascular catheters introduce bacteria into the bloodstream. Pathogens express specific cell-surface adhesins (MSCRAMMs in S. aureus such as fibronectin-binding proteins A/B, clumping factor A/B; glucans and dextran in VGS; enterococcal surface protein Esp, Ebp pili) that bind specifically to fibronectin, fibrinogen, laminin, and collagen within the NBTE matrix.
  - Biofilm Growth & Vegetation Maturation: Adherent bacteria multiply rapidly within the fibrin-platelet meshwork, stimulating further local platelet aggregation and fibrin deposition. This shields the expanding bacterial colony from circulating neutrophils, macrophages, and humoral antibodies, forming a mature, protective vegetation. In biomaterials (prosthetic valves, CIED leads, LVADs), bacteria form dense sessile biofilms encased in extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) with metabolic downregulation and extreme antibiotic resistance.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Local Tissue Destruction: Bacterial exotoxins, hemolysins, proteases, and matrix metalloproteinases degrade extracellular matrix, leading to leaflet perforation, chordal rupture, valvular regurgitation, ring/perivalvular abscess formation, septal erosion, pericarditis, purulent pericardial effusion, and heart block (due to conduction system disruption in periannular AV node extension).
  - Embolic Phenomena: Friable, fragmenting vegetations embolize into the systemic or pulmonary circulation. Left-sided vegetations embolize to the brain (ischemic stroke, mycotic aneurysms, intracerebral hemorrhage), spleen, kidneys, mesentery, peripheral arteries, and coronary arteries. Right-sided vegetations embolize to the lungs, causing septic pulmonary emboli, pulmonary infarction, pneumatoceles, and lung abscesses.
  - Immunological Stigmata & Immune Complex Vasculitis: Chronic high-grade antigenemia triggers continuous antibody production, forming circulating immune complexes (IgG, IgM, complement). Immune complex deposition in small vessels causes Type III hypersensitivity reactions: leukocytoclastic vasculitis (Osler nodes, petechiae, Janeway lesions), immune-complex glomerulonephritis (diffuse proliferative or focal embolic glomerulonephritis with hypocomplementemia), and conjunctival/retinal hemorrhages (Roth spots).

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Constitutional Symptoms: Unexplained fever (present in 85% to 95% of cases; may be low-grade or remittent), chills, rigors, night sweats, profound fatigue, malaise, anorexia, weight loss, myalgias, arthralgias, and flank/back pain.
  - Cardiac & Respiratory Symptoms: New or worsening dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, peripheral edema, chest pain, or cough (indicative of acute heart failure or pulmonary emboli).
  - Neurological Symptoms: Sudden-onset focal weakness, paresthesias, dysphasia, severe headache, confusion, or altered mental status (secondary to ischemic stroke, mycotic aneurysm rupture, toxic encephalopathy, or brain abscess).
  - Subacute vs. Acute Onset: Subacute cases present with insidious weight loss, low-grade fevers, and vague malaise over weeks/months, whereas acute cases (S. aureus, pneumococcus) present with explosive fever, rigors, rapidly progressive dyspnea, and hemodynamically catastrophic valve destruction over hours/days.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Cardiac Signs: Cardiac murmur present in 80% to 90% of cases (new or changing regurgitant murmur—mitral regurgitation, aortic insufficiency, or tricuspid regurgitation). Signs of congestive heart failure (S3 gallop, pulmonary crackles, elevated JVP, peripheral edema).
  - Peripheral / Cutaneous Immunological & Embolic Signs:
    - Osler Nodes: Painful, tender, purplish erythematous nodular lesions located on the pads of fingers and toes (immunological origin).
    - Janeway Lesions: Painless, non-tender, flat erythematous macules on the palms of hands and soles of feet (septic embolic origin).
    - Splinter Hemorrhages: Linear, non-blanching dark red/brown subungual streak hemorrhages in the middle of nail beds.
    - Petechiae: Non-blanching small erythematous macules on conjunctiva, palate, buccal mucosa, and distal extremities.
    - Roth Spots: Exudative, pale oval retinal hemorrhages with central white spots seen on fundoscopic examination.
    - Splenomegaly & Flank Tenderness: Splenic enlargement (common in subacute IE >1 month) or renal costovertebral angle tenderness (renal infarction).
    - Neurological Deficits: Hemiparesis, cranial nerve palsies, meningismus, or coma.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Classic Oslerian Triad (Historical): Fever, heart murmur, and peripheral vascular/embolic stigmata.
  - Janeway Lesions: Non-tender erythematous macules on palms/soles pathognomonic for septic microemboli.
  - Roth Spots: Retinal hemorrhages with central pale centers pathognomonic for immune complex retinal vasculitis.
  - Osler Nodes: Painful erythematous nodules on digits pathognomonic for immune complex cutaneous vasculitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - CBC: Normocytic normochromic anemia (present in up to 90% of subacute cases); leukocytosis with neutrophilia and left shift (common in acute IE, but WBC may be normal in 50% of subacute cases).
  - Inflammatory Markers: Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) elevated in >90% of cases (often >50-100 mm/hr); C-reactive Protein (CRP) markedly elevated; useful as baseline to monitor therapeutic response.
  - Urinalysis & Renal Panel: Microscopic hematuria, proteinuria, and red blood cell casts (indicative of immune-complex glomerulonephritis or embolic renal infarction); elevated serum creatinine.
  - Serum Complement & Rheumatoid Factor: Rheumatoid factor (positive in 50% of subacute cases >6 weeks); decreased C3, C4, and total hemolytic complement (CH50) in active immune complex glomerulonephritis.
- **Imaging Modalities (e.g., X-ray, CT, Ultrasound):**
  - Transthoracic Echocardiography (TTE): Initial noninvasive imaging modality for all suspected IE cases. Rapid, widely available; sensitivity 60% to 65% for native valve vegetations, but limited in prosthetic valves (<20-35% sensitivity) or small vegetations (<5 mm).
  - Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE): Modality of choice and standard of care for complicated IE, PVE, CIED lead endocarditis, or when TTE is inconclusive. Sensitivity >95% (95-100% for native and prosthetic valve vegetations). Superior for detecting paravalvular extension (abscess, pseudoaneurysm, fistula), leaflet perforation, valve dehiscence, and chordal rupture. (Repeat TEE in 7 to 10 days if initial TEE is negative and clinical suspicion remains high).
  - Nuclear Medicine / Molecular Imaging: 18F-FDG PET/CT and 99mTc-HMPAO-labeled leukocyte SPECT/CT. Highly sensitive for detecting prosthetic valve endocarditis (18F-FDG uptake around prosthetic valve ring is a novel major Duke criterion in ESC guidelines), identifying CIED pocket/lead infections, and mapping silent systemic septic emboli (spleen, bone, vascular).
  - CT / CTA & MRI: Cardiac CTA delineates aortic root anatomy, paravalvular abscesses, pseudoaneurysms, and coronary artery origins before surgery. Brain MRI (with DWI/SWI) detects silent cerebral microemboli, ischemic strokes, and mycotic aneurysms. Abdominal CT identifies splenic/renal infarctions and abscesses.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Blood Cultures (The Diagnostic Cornerstone): Obtain at least 3 sets of blood cultures (drawn from separate venipunctures, with first and last set drawn >=1 hour apart) before starting antibiotics in stable patients. Bacteremia in IE is continuous and low-grade (<100 CFUs/mL in 80% of cases). Sensitivity is >90% in untreated patients (first two cultures positive in >90% of cases). Continuous monitoring systems (BACTEC, BacT/ALERT) improve yield.
  - Culture-Negative IE Workup: Occurs in 2% to 20% of cases (mostly due to prior antibiotic therapy within 2 weeks). Serologic testing for Coxiella burnetii (phase I IgG >1:800), Bartonella henselae/quintana, Brucella, and Chlamydia.
  - Tissue Cultures, PCR & Histopathology: Broad-range 16S rRNA gene PCR and sequencing on resected valve tissue, vegetations, or emboli yields pathogen DNA in up to 18-30% of culture-negative cases. Direct tissue Gram stain, Ziehl-Neelsen / Fite stain (mycobacteria), and Grocott methenamine silver (GMS) / PAS stains (fungi). Histopathology showing active endocarditis with neutrophilic infiltration and tissue necrosis confirms diagnosis.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Histopathologic demonstration of active endocarditis (neutrophilic exudate and necrosis) or microbiological confirmation (positive culture or PCR) from resected valvular tissue, vegetation, or intracardiac abscess obtained during surgery or autopsy remains the definitive gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Hemodynamic Stabilization: ICU admission for acute heart failure, cardiogenic shock, or severe sepsis/septic shock; invasive arterial monitoring, vasopressors, cautious diuresis, and afterload reduction. Avoid intra-aortic balloon pump in acute aortic regurgitation!
  - Anticoagulation Management: Discontinue oral anticoagulants (warfarin/DOACs) in patients with S. aureus PVE or native valve IE with cerebral embolic events for at least the first 2 weeks of antimicrobial therapy to prevent hemorrhagic transformation of embolic strokes.
  - Antimicrobial Timing: In stable patients with subacute presentation, hold antibiotics until 3 sets of blood cultures are drawn over several hours. In acute, critically ill, or hemodynamically unstable patients, obtain 3 sets of blood cultures over 30 to 60 minutes and immediately start empiric broad-spectrum therapy.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Empiric Native Valve IE (Community-Acquired or Subacute): Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h (targeting trough 15-20 mcg/mL or AUC/MIC 400-600) PLUS Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h OR Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h.
  - Empiric Healthcare-Associated NVE or Acute Fulminant IE: Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h PLUS Cefepime 2 g IV q8h OR Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h.
  - Empiric Early-Onset Prosthetic Valve IE (<=1 year post-op): Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h PLUS Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV q8h (or 3 mg/kg IV q24h) PLUS Rifampin 300 mg PO/IV q8h (add rifampin 2-3 days after vancomycin/gentamicin to prevent emergent resistance) PLUS Cefepime 2 g IV q8h.
  - Empiric Late-Onset Prosthetic Valve IE (>1 year post-op): Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h PLUS Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h (or Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h) PLUS Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV q8h.
- **Alternative Regimens (e.g., for Penicillin Allergy):**
  - Penicillin-Susceptible Viridans Group Streptococci & S. gallolyticus (MIC <=0.12 mcg/mL):
    - Preferred: Penicillin G 12-18 million units/day IV divided q4h x 4 weeks OR Ampicillin 2 g IV q4h x 4 weeks OR Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h x 4 weeks.
    - Short-Course 2-Week Regimen (Uncomplicated Native Valve): Penicillin G 12-18 million units/day IV OR Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h PLUS Gentamicin 3 mg/kg IV q24h x 2 weeks.
    - Penicillin Allergy / Anaphylaxis: Vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg IV q12h x 4 weeks (target trough 10-15 mcg/mL).
  - Relatively Penicillin-Resistant VGS (MIC 0.12 to 0.5 mcg/mL): Penicillin G 24 million units/day IV x 4 weeks PLUS Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV q8h x 2 weeks.
  - Enterococcal IE (Penicillin & Aminoglycoside Susceptible):
    - Dual Beta-Lactam (Preferred, Renal-Safe): Ampicillin 2 g IV q4h PLUS Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h x 6 weeks.
    - Standard Synergistic Regimen: Ampicillin 2 g IV q4h (or Penicillin G 18-30 million units/day IV) PLUS Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV q8h x 4 to 6 weeks.
    - Penicillin Allergy / VRE / Resistance: Vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg IV q12h x 6 weeks PLUS Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV q8h x 6 weeks. For VRE (vancomycin-resistant enterococci): Daptomycin 8-12 mg/kg IV q24h OR Linezolid 600 mg IV/PO q12h x >=6 weeks.
  - Methicillin-Susceptible S. aureus (MSSA):
    - Native Valve: Nafcillin or Oxacillin 2 g IV q4h x 4 to 6 weeks OR Cefazolin 2 g IV q8h x 4 to 6 weeks. (Adjunctive gentamicin is NOT recommended for native valve S. aureus IE due to nephrotoxicity risk without clinical benefit!).
    - Prosthetic Valve: Nafcillin or Oxacillin 2 g IV q4h x >=6 weeks PLUS Rifampin 300 mg PO/IV q8h x >=6 weeks PLUS Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV q8h x 2 weeks.
  - Methicillin-Resistant S. aureus (MRSA):
    - Native Valve: Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h x 4 to 6 weeks (trough 15-20 mcg/mL) OR Daptomycin 8 to 10 mg/kg IV q24h x 4 to 6 weeks.
    - Prosthetic Valve: Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h x >=6 weeks PLUS Rifampin 300 mg PO/IV q8h x >=6 weeks PLUS Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV q8h x 2 weeks.
  - HACEK Organisms: Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h x 4 weeks (6 weeks for PVE) OR Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h x 4-6 weeks. Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV / 500 mg PO q12h in severe beta-lactam allergy.
  - Culture-Negative Native Valve IE: Ampicillin-sulbactam 12 g/day IV in 4 divided doses PLUS Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h PLUS Gentamicin 3 mg/kg/day IV x 4-6 weeks.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions (e.g., Drainage, Debridement):**
  - Indications for Urgent / Early Cardiac Surgery (performed during active antibiotic therapy in up to 50% of cases):
    1. Acute Heart Failure: Refractory pulmonary edema or cardiogenic shock due to severe acute aortic or mitral regurgitation, valve perforation, or chordal rupture (the #1 indication for surgery).
    2. Uncontrolled Infection: Persistent bacteremia or fever despite >5 to 7 days of appropriate antimicrobial therapy; infection caused by highly resistant pathogens (fungi like Candida/Aspergillus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, VRE, VISA/VRSA).
    3. Paravalvular / Invasive Complications: Paravalvular abscess, pseudoaneurysm, intracardiac fistula, new conduction block (AV block), or mechanical prosthetic valve dehiscence / acute obstruction.
    4. Embolism Prevention: Large vegetations (>10 mm) associated with previous embolic events, or persistent large vegetations (>15 mm) under therapy.
  - CIED System Removal: Complete removal of all hardware (generator and all transvenous leads) is mandatory for all confirmed CIED infections. Percutaneous transvenous lead extraction is preferred over open surgery.
  - LVAD Management: Driveline exit site infections managed with debridement and prolonged oral suppressive therapy; pump/cannula infections or endocarditis require prolonged IV antibiotics, surgical debridement, and often emergency heart transplantation as definitive cure.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Cardiac Complications: Acute congestive heart failure (#1 cause of death in IE), severe valvular regurgitation, valve leaflet perforation/rupture, paravalvular abscess, ring abscess, pseudoaneurysm, aorto-cavitary fistula, purulent pericarditis, acute myocardial infarction (embolic occlusion of coronary arteries), and conduction heart block (third-degree AV block).
  - Neurologic Complications: Occur in 20% to 40% of left-sided IE: embolic ischemic stroke, mycotic aneurysm formation and rupture (causing subarachnoid or intracerebral hemorrhage), brain abscess, toxic encephalopathy, septic meningitis, and purulent meningoencephalitis.
  - Systemic Embolic & Organ Complications: Splenic infarction and splenic abscess (requiring splenectomy), renal infarction, acute tubular necrosis, immune-complex glomerulonephritis, acute kidney injury, mesenteric ischemia/infarction, peripheral arterial occlusion, and septic pulmonary emboli / lung abscesses / empyema (in right-sided IE).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Permanent valvular dysfunction requiring late elective valve replacement or repair.
  - Chronic heart failure and left ventricular remodeling.
  - Permanent neurological deficits (hemiplegia, aphasia) following embolic stroke or intracranial hemorrhage.
  - Chronic renal disease / persistent insufficiency following glomerulonephritis or acute tubular necrosis.
  - Recurrent infective endocarditis (rates range from 2% to 10% per patient-year).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Osler Nodes: Painful, tender red/purple nodules on finger and toe pads (immune complex deposition).
  - Janeway Lesions: Painless, non-tender macules on palms and soles (septic microemboli).
  - Roth Spots: Retinal hemorrhages with pale, white central centers.
  - Splinter Hemorrhages: Dark red linear subungual lines beneath nails.
  - HACEK: Acronym for fastidious Gram-negative bacilli (Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, Kingella).
  - Abiotrophia & Granulicatella: Formerly known as Nutritionally Variant Streptococci (NVS), requiring pyridoxal (vitamin B6) for growth.
  - Mycobacterium chimaera Outbreak: Contaminated heater-cooler units (Chitwood / Sorin Stockert 3T) during cardiopulmonary bypass causing delayed PVE months to years post-op.
  - Ts and Blues: Pentazocine (Talwin) plus tripelennamine abuse among PWID predisposed to Pseudomonas aeruginosa endocarditis.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - No Gentamicin in Native Valve S. aureus IE: Adjunctive gentamicin for native valve S. aureus IE confers high nephrotoxicity without clinical benefit and is explicitly discouraged in AHA guidelines!
  - Rifampin Delayed Addition in PVE: Rifampin should be delayed by 2 to 3 days after initiating vancomycin and gentamicin in staphylococcal PVE to reduce bacterial burden and prevent emergent rifampin resistance.
  - Dual Beta-Lactam Ampicillin + Ceftriaxone for Enterococcus faecalis: Ampicillin plus ceftriaxone achieves synergistic bactericidal activity against E. faecalis (by saturating PBP-2 and PBP-3) with zero nephrotoxicity compared to ampicillin plus gentamicin.
  - Normal WBC Count in Subacute IE: WBC count is normal in >50% of patients with subacute VGS or enterococcal endocarditis; normal WBC cannot exclude IE.
  - Negative TEE Does Not Exclude IE: Initial TEE can be false-negative in 5% of cases (small vegetations <2 mm or recent embolization); repeat TEE in 7 to 10 days if clinical suspicion remains high.
  - Aspirin / Antiplatelets Do Not Prevent Emboli in Established IE: Aspirin is NOT recommended for treatment of established IE because it increases bleeding risk without reducing embolic events, though experimental models show potential prophylactic benefit.
