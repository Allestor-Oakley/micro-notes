# Myocarditis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Inflammation of the myocardium (literally "inflammation of the cardiac muscle"), characterized by inflammatory leukocyte infiltration of cardiac tissue accompanied by myocyte degeneration and necrosis not characteristic of an ischemic event. In clinical practice, the term encompasses a range of infectious and noninfectious inflammatory heart diseases that directly or indirectly affect myocardial structure and function, frequently causing acute or chronic left/right ventricular dysfunction, conduction disturbances, ventricular arrhythmias, cardiogenic shock, and progression to dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM).
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Clinical Severity / Presentation Velocity: (1) Acute Myocarditis—presents with new-onset heart failure, chest pain, or arrhythmias over days to weeks with mild-to-moderate hemodynamic impairment; (2) Fulminant Myocarditis—rapidly progressive clinical course presenting with severe cardiogenic shock, acute hemodynamic collapse, and requirement for high-dose vasopressors or mechanical circulatory support (e.g., VAD, ECMO), but with high rates of complete long-term recovery (>90%) if supported through the acute phase; (3) Chronic / Subacute Myocarditis—indolent, slowly progressive course over months to years leading to irreversible left ventricular dilation and inflammatory dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM).
  - Histopathologic Classification (Dallas Criteria): (1) Active Myocarditis—demonstrates inflammatory cellular infiltrate with clear evidence of myocyte necrosis or degeneration not due to ischemia; (2) Borderline Myocarditis—less intense inflammatory infiltrate without light microscopic evidence of myocyte necrosis; (3) Nonmyocarditis / Resolved Myocarditis.
  - Cellular / Immunological Subtypes: (1) Lymphocytic Myocarditis (most common viral/autoimmune pattern); (2) Eosinophilic Myocarditis (associated with hypersensitivity drug reactions, parasite infections like Trichinella, Toxocara, or Churg-Strauss syndrome); (3) Giant Cell Myocarditis (rare, fulminant autoimmune entity featuring multinucleated giant cells and widespread myocyte destruction); (4) Granulomatous Myocarditis (seen in cardiac sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, or fungal infections).
  - Etiologic Classification: Infectious (viral, bacterial, spirochetal, rickettsial, fungal, parasitic) vs. Noninfectious (autoimmune/collagen vascular disease, drug hypersensitivity, direct cardiotoxins, radiation, immune checkpoint inhibitors).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:** Unsuspected myocarditis is found in 1% to 4% of unselected postmortem autopsies in the general population, rising to 10% to 20% in young individuals dying of sudden unexplained cardiac death. Idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) exhibits histological/molecular evidence of previous myocarditis in 10% to 20% of cases. Annual incidence of DCM is estimated at 2 to 8 cases per 100,000 population. Preexisting genetic susceptibility includes heterozygous or homozygous mutations in cytoskeletal genes (dystrophin) or desmosomal genes. Environmental and host risk factors include recent viral upper respiratory or gastrointestinal infections, male gender (higher susceptibility in animal models due to testosterone-mediated Th1 responses), pregnancy/peripartum state, young age (infants and young adults), selenium deficiency (Keshan disease, where selenium deficiency increases enteroviral virulence), strenuous physical exercise during acute infection (significantly increases viral replication, myocardial inflammation, and mortality), immunocompromised states (HIV/AIDS, post-transplantation), endemic geographic exposure (Trypanosoma cruzi / Chagas disease in Latin America), and recent receipt of immune checkpoint inhibitor immunotherapies (combination nivolumab/ipilimumab) or certain vaccines (e.g., smallpox/vaccinia).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Viruses: Cardiotropic viruses are the leading infectious causes in North America and Western Europe. Group B Coxsackieviruses (Coxsackievirus B1-B6, especially CVB3 and CVB5), Adenoviruses (types 2, 5, 7, 21), Parvovirus B19 (PVB19; frequently detected in cardiac endothelial cells with high viral load and replicative intermediates), Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6), Human Cytomegalovirus (HCMV), Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV), Echoviruses, Nonpolio Enteroviruses, Influenza A and B viruses, Dengue virus (types 1-4), Chikungunya virus, Hepatitis C virus, Hepatitis B virus, and Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV-1; direct viral effect or secondary opportunistic pathogens).
  - Parasites: Trypanosoma cruzi (the causative protozoan of Chagas disease; leading cause of infectious myocarditis and dilated cardiomyopathy in Central and South America, now increasing in nonendemic regions due to migration).
  - Spirochetes: Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme carditis; causes transient myocarditis with high-degree AV conduction block in 10% to 16% of untreated Lyme disease).
  - Bacteria & Toxin-Producers: Corynebacterium diphtheriae (diphtheritic myocarditis mediated by potent exotoxin inhibiting protein synthesis, causing myocarditis in up to 20% of diphtheria patients and accounting for 50% of diphtheria deaths).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Neonates & Infants (<3 months): Group B Coxsackieviruses (CVB), Echoviruses, Adenoviruses, Herpes simplex virus (HSV), and Human Cytomegalovirus (HCMV). Presentation is often part of a fulminant systemic infection with multi-organ failure, encephalomyocarditis, hepatomegaly, and circulatory collapse.
  - Children & Adolescents: Adenoviruses, Parvovirus B19, Group B Coxsackieviruses, Influenza viruses, Mumps virus, Rubella virus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme carditis).
  - Adults & Elderly: Parvovirus B19, HHV-6, Enteroviruses (Coxsackievirus B), Influenza viruses, Hepatitis C virus, HIV-1, Borrelia burgdorferi, Trypanosoma cruzi, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Rickettsia species (Rickettsia rickettsii, Orientia tsutsugamushi), Coxiella burnetii (Q fever), and bacterial septic emboli/bacteremia (Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes, Neisseria meningitidis, Listeria monocytogenes).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Bacteria & Rickettsiae: Brucella species, Campylobacter species, Clostridium perfringens (gas gangrene of myocardium/microabscesses), Francisella tularensis, Legionella pneumophila, Salmonella species, Shigella, Tropheryma whipplei (Whipple disease), Ureaplasma, Rickettsia prowazekii, Orientia tsutsugamushi (scrub typhus), and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (tuberculous myocarditis causing sudden cardiac death).
  - Fungi: Aspergillus species (A. fumigatus, A. flavus; presenting with myocardial infarction from coronary artery occlusion or complete heart block), Candida species (C. albicans), Cryptococcus neoformans (in HIV/AIDS patients), Histoplasma capsulatum, Coccidioides immitis, and Blastomyces dermatitidis.
  - Parasites & Helminths: Trichinella spiralis (trichinosis with persistent eosinophilic myocarditis and ventilatory failure), Toxoplasma gondii (toxoplasmic myocarditis in immunocompromised hosts/transplant recipients and acute acquired toxoplasmosis), Toxocara canis (visceral larva migrans), Trypanosoma gambiense / T. rhodesiense (African trypanosomiasis), and Schistosoma.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Entry & Cardiotropism: Cardiotropic viruses reach the myocardium predominantly via hematogenous dissemination following initial respiratory or gastrointestinal replication. Enteroviruses (CVB) and Adenoviruses attach to the specific Coxsackievirus-Adenovirus Receptor (CAR) and decay-accelerating factor (DAF/CD55) expressed on the sarcolemma of cardiac myocytes. Parvovirus B19 targets endothelial cells of myocardial microvessels via globoside receptors.
  - Biphasic / Triphasic Disease Model:
    - Phase 1 (Acute Direct Viral Injury & Innate Response, Days 1-7): Direct viral entry, endosomal escape, and intracellular replication cause direct myocyte lysis, apoptosis, and necrosis. Enteroviral protease 2A cleaves the host subsarcolemmal protein dystrophin, disrupting the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex and impairing sarcolemmal integrity and excitation-contraction coupling. Innate immune activation occurs via Toll-like receptors (TLR3, TLR4), inducing Type I Interferons (IFN-alpha/beta) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, IL-6).
    - Phase 2 (Acquired Autoimmune & Cell-Mediated Response, Weeks to Months): Antigen-presenting cells display viral peptides alongside released sequestered cardiac self-antigens (e.g., cardiac myosin heavy chain, beta-1 adrenergic receptors, ADP-ATP carrier proteins). Molecular mimicry and epitope spreading activate cytotoxic T-lymphocytes (CD8+) and B-cells producing autoantibodies. Cytotoxic T-cells and natural killer (NK) cells target both virus-infected cells and uninfected myocytes.
    - Phase 3 (Viral Clearance vs. Persistence & Remodeling): Successful immune response eliminates the virus, with downregulation of inflammation and clinical recovery. Alternatively, viral persistence (e.g., persistent enteroviral RNA or high PVB19 viral load) or chronic autoimmune inflammation causes ongoing myocyte loss, extensive interstitial fibrosis, myofibrillar fragmentation, and progression to dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM).
  - Toxin-Mediated & Parasitic Mechanisms: Diphtheria toxin inhibits protein synthesis via ADP-ribosylation of elongation factor 2 (EF-2), causing fatty degeneration and myofibrillar necrosis without direct bacterial invasion. In Chagas disease, Trypanosoma cruzi amastigotes replicate inside myocytes, forming intracellular pseudocysts; pseudocyst rupture releases amastigotes, triggering intense lymphocytic and eosinophilic inflammation, microvascular spasm, focal ischemia, and apical aneurysm formation.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Histopathologic Evolution: Early acute phase is characterized by interstitial edema, focal or diffuse mononuclear leukocyte infiltration (T-lymphocytes, macrophages, NK cells), loss of myocyte striations, nuclear pyknosis, and myocyte necrosis/degeneration. Eosinophils predominate in hypersensitivity/parasitic myocarditis.
  - Repair & Fibrosis: Healing is marked by macrophage clearing of necrotic debris, proliferation of fibroblasts, and progressive interstitial and perivascular collagen deposition (fibrosis) replacing lost myofibers.
  - Microvascular Dysfunction & Remodeling: Endothelial injury and microvascular spasm cause localized ischemia, focal contraction band necrosis, and ventricular wall thinning (classic apical thinning/aneurysm in Chagas disease).

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Antecedent Viral Prodrome: History of a flu-like or upper respiratory illness (fever, sore throat, myalgias, arthralgias, malaise) or gastrointestinal illness (diarrhea, nausea, vomiting) occurring 1 to 2 weeks prior to cardiac symptoms (present in 30% to 60% of cases, though often mild or long forgotten).
  - Cardiac Symptoms: Sudden or insidious onset of unexplained precordial or retrosternal chest pain (may be sharp, pleuritic, or positional, mimicking acute pericarditis, or squeezing/substernal, mimicking acute myocardial infarction); new-onset dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, and easy fatigability; palpitation, lightheadedness, presyncope, or syncope (secondary to ventricular arrhythmias or high-degree AV block).
  - Fulminant Presentation: Rapid development of severe cardiogenic shock, profound oliguria, altered mental status, cold clammy extremities, and pulmonary edema within hours to days of initial symptoms.
  - Asymptomatic / Subclinical: Mild, self-limited cases may be entirely asymptomatic or present only with transient nonspecific fatigue or minor ECG abnormalities.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Hemodynamic & Vital Signs: Tachypnea, sinus tachycardia disproportionate to the degree of fever (a classic clinical clue!), hypotension, narrow pulse pressure, and low-grade fever.
  - Cardiac Auscultation: Muffled or soft first heart sound (S1), prominent third (S3) and fourth (S4) gallop rhythms, apical systolic murmur of functional mitral regurgitation (due to ventricular dilation and papillary muscle dysfunction), and pericardial friction rub if concomitant pericarditis is present (myopericarditis).
  - Signs of Heart Failure & Hypoperfusion: Elevated jugular venous pressure (JVP) with hepatojugular reflux, pulmonary rales/crackles, peripheral edema, hepatomegaly, cool cyanotic extremities, and delayed capillary refill.
  - Specific Etiologic Signs: "Slapped cheek" rash or lacy reticular exanthem in Parvovirus B19; Romana sign (unilateral painless periorbital edema and palpebral swelling) or chagoma in acute Chagas disease; erythema migrans, cranial neuropathy, or lymphadenopathy in Lyme carditis; pharyngeal pseudomembrane and bull neck in diphtheria; muscle tenderness and orbital edema in trichinosis.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Sinus Tachycardia Disproportionate to Fever: Unexplained tachycardia out of proportion to body temperature elevation is a classic physical sign of acute myocarditis.
  - Romana Sign: Unilateral painless periorbital edema and conjunctivitis pathognomonic for acute Chagas disease (Trypanosoma cruzi inoculation via conjunctiva).
  - Biventricular Apical Thinning / Aneurysm with Right Bundle Branch Block: Classical combination of apical ventricular aneurysm, RBBB, and LAFB in an endemic patient pathognomonic for chronic Chagasic cardiomyopathy.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Biomarkers of Myocardial Necrosis: Serum Cardiac Troponin I (cTnI) and Troponin T (cTnT) are elevated in acute myocarditis and exhibit higher sensitivity than Creatine Kinase-MB (CK-MB). However, normal troponin levels do NOT exclude myocarditis (especially in subacute or chronic cases).
  - Inflammatory Markers: Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) and C-reactive Protein (CRP) are elevated in acute cases, reflecting systemic and myocardial inflammation, but lack specificity.
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis with neutrophilia in bacterial/fulminant viral cases; prominent peripheral eosinophilia in hypersensitivity myocarditis, parasitic infections (Trichinella, Toxocara), and Churg-Strauss syndrome.
  - Natriuretic Peptides: B-type Natriuretic Peptide (BNP) and N-terminal pro-BNP (NT-proBNP) are significantly elevated, reflecting ventricular wall stress, elevated filling pressures, and severity of heart failure.
  - Electrocardiogram (ECG): Abnormal in >90% of patients, though non-specific. Features: sinus tachycardia, ST-segment elevation (concave or convex, diffuses across non-contiguous leads without reciprocal depression, distinguishing it from ischemia), T-wave flattening or inversion, QRS prolongation, low voltage QRS (in severe edema or pericardial effusion), pathological Q waves (mimicking myocardial infarction), ventricular arrhythmias (PVCs, non-sustained/sustained VT), atrial fibrillation, and conduction system blocks (first-, second-, or third-degree AV block, intraventricular conduction delay, bundle branch blocks; high-degree AV block is classic in Lyme carditis, diphtheria, sarcoidosis, and giant cell myocarditis).
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Echocardiography: Key initial noninvasive test to evaluate ventricular chamber dimensions, geometry, and systolic/diastolic function, and to exclude valvular disease, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, or pericardial effusion. Findings: global left ventricular hypokinesis, regional wall motion abnormalities (mimicking CAD), normal or non-dilated LV cavity size with increased wall thickness (due to acute myocardial edema in fulminant cases), right ventricular dysfunction (an independent predictor of death or transplantation), ventricular thrombi, and pericardial effusion.
  - Cardiac Magnetic Resonance (CMR) Imaging: The noninvasive gold standard diagnostic imaging modality. Diagnostic accuracy is governed by the Lake Louise Criteria (requires at least 2 of the 3 parameters): (1) Regional or global myocardial signal intensity increase on T2-weighted images (indicating myocardial edema); (2) Increased global myocardial Early Gadolinium Enhancement (EGE) on T1-weighted images (indicating hyperemia/capillary leak); (3) Presence of at least one focal lesion with Late Gadolinium Enhancement (LGE) on T1-weighted inversion recovery images (indicating necrosis or fibrosis). LGE in myocarditis characteristically shows subepicardial or midwall patchy distribution sparing the subendocardium (distinguishing it from ischemic transmural/subendocardial infarction). Native T1 and T2 quantitative mapping techniques further improve diagnostic sensitivity and specificity.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Molecular Diagnostics (PCR on Endomyocardial Biopsy): Polymerase chain reaction (PCR), reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR), and in situ hybridization performed on frozen endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) tissue represent the definitive method to identify cardiotropic viral genomes (Coxsackievirus B, Adenovirus, Parvovirus B19, HHV-6, HCMV, EBV). High viral load copy number and detection of viral RNA replicative intermediates distinguish active pathogenic viral replication from incidental background persistence.
  - Viral Cultures & Serology: Throat swab and stool viral cultures provide circumstantial evidence during local outbreaks. Blood viral PCR for enteroviruses or adenoviruses. Serum IgM antibody titers or fourfold rise in IgG paired titers provide supportive evidence, but routine viral serology is generally unhelpful and not recommended due to high background prevalence of antibodies in the general population.
  - Specific Microorganism Diagnostic Testing: Blood cultures for bacterial bacteremia/sepsis; Borrelia burgdorferi serology (ELISA followed by Western Blot) for Lyme carditis; Trypanosoma cruzi serology (ELISA, IFA) or direct blood smear/PCR in acute Chagas disease; Toxin assays for Corynebacterium diphtheriae; Muscle biopsy or serology for Trichinella; Toxoplasma gondii serology/PCR.
  - Histopathology & Immunoperoxidase Stains: Microscopic examination of EMB tissue using H&E stain (Dallas criteria) and immunohistochemical staining using monoclonal antibodies against cell surface markers (CD3 for T-cells, CD68 for macrophages, HLA-DR) to quantify intramyocardial inflammation (>14 leukocytes/mm2).
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Endomyocardial Biopsy (EMB) demonstrating histopathologic evidence of myocardial inflammation and myocyte necrosis (Dallas criteria) combined with immunohistochemistry and viral PCR on tissue remains the absolute gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Hemodynamic & Circulatory Support: Admission to intensive care unit / cardiac care unit with continuous ECG and hemodynamic monitoring. In fulminant myocarditis or cardiogenic shock, immediate administration of IV inotropic agents (dobutamine, milrinone) and vasopressors (norepinephrine). Avoid high doses of digitalis/digoxin, as digitalis increases myocardial cytokine production and mortality in viral myocarditis models!
  - Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT) for Heart Failure: Standard heart failure therapy once hemodynamically stable: Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme (ACE) inhibitors (e.g., captopril, enalapril) or Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs), chronic beta-blockers (carvedilol, metoprolol succinate; introduced cautiously once acute shock resolves), aldosterone antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone), and loop diuretics for volume overload.
  - Strict Physical Rest: Strict bed rest and avoidance of competitive sports, strenuous exercise, or heavy physical exertion during the acute phase and for at least 3 to 6 months after diagnosis (exercise markedly enhances viral replication, cytokine production, and mortality).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Viral Myocarditis: Specific empiric antiviral therapy is generally NOT recommended for routine acute viral myocarditis. Treatment focuses on supportive care and GDMT. (Interferon-beta1b therapy has demonstrated efficacy in virus-clearing and functional improvement in chronic enteroviral or adenoviral cardiomyopathy).
  - Lyme Carditis: Intravenous Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h (or Cefotaxime 2 g IV q8h or High-dose Penicillin G 18-24 million units/day IV) for 14 to 21 days in patients with high-degree AV block, PR interval >300 ms, or severe symptoms; step-down to PO Doxycycline 100 mg PO q12h once AV block resolves.
  - Acute Chagasic Myocarditis (Trypanosoma cruzi): Benznidazole 5 to 7 mg/kg/day PO divided in 2 doses for 60 days OR Nifurtimox 8 to 10 mg/kg/day PO divided in 3-4 doses for 90 to 120 days.
  - Diphtheritic Myocarditis: Diphtheria Antitoxin (DAT) administered immediately (equine hyperimmune serum, dose 20,000 to 100,000 units IV depending on disease duration/severity) PLUS Erythromycin 500 mg IV/PO q6h x 14 days OR Penicillin G 600,000 units IM q12h x 14 days.
  - Bacterial / Septic Myocarditis: Empiric broad-spectrum parenteral antimicrobials targeting isolated bacteremic pathogens (e.g., Vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg IV q8-12h PLUS Cefepime 2 g IV q8h).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Immunomodulatory & Targeted Anti-Inflammatory Therapies:
    - Immunosuppressive Therapy (Prednisone + Cyclosporine / Azathioprine): Routine corticosteroids are CONTRAINDICATED in acute viral myocarditis (steroids enhance viral replication and myocardial necrosis in acute viral models). However, immunosuppression is FIRST-LINE therapy for non-viral / autoimmune myocarditis: Giant Cell Myocarditis (cyclosporine + high-dose corticosteroids + antithymocyte globulin), Cardiac Sarcoidosis, Hypersensitivity Myocarditis, and Virus-Negative Chronic Inflammatory Cardiomyopathy (TIMIC study protocol: prednisone + azathioprine for 6 months).
    - Anakinra (IL-1 Receptor Antagonist): Recombinant IL-1beta blocker (ACTION study protocol) under evaluation for acute refractory myocarditis.
    - Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG): Routine high-dose IVIG is NOT recommended for adult acute myocarditis based on randomized controlled trials, but may be considered in pediatric acute myocarditis or specific viral persistence (PVB19/CMV).
  - Alternative Antimicrobials: For Lyme carditis in oral phase or mild cases: Doxycycline 100 mg PO q12h or Amoxicillin 500 mg PO q8h or Cefuroxime axetil 500 mg PO q12h for 14 to 21 days.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Mechanical Circulatory Support (MCS): Temporary percutaneous or surgical Ventricular Assist Devices (VADs), Impella, or Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) in fulminant myocarditis with refractory cardiogenic shock. Bridge to recovery (spontaneous recovery occurs in >90% of fulminant cases) or bridge to transplantation.
  - Temporary or Permanent Cardiac Pacing: Temporary transvenous cardiac pacemaker insertion for symptomatic bradycardia, complete heart block, or high-degree AV block (frequently required in Lyme carditis, diphtheria, sarcoidosis, and giant cell myocarditis; block in Lyme carditis is usually temporary and resolves with antibiotics within 1 to 2 weeks, avoiding permanent pacemaker placement).
  - Endomyocardial Biopsy (EMB) Procedure: Right ventricular or left ventricular fluoroscopic or echocardiographic guided transvenous biopsy (recommended for unexplained acute heart failure <2 weeks duration with hemodynamic collapse, or 2 weeks to 3 months duration with new ventricular arrhythmias or heart block).
  - Cardiac Transplantation: Reserved as a last resort for acute myocarditis refractory to mechanical circulatory support or chronic end-stage inflammatory dilated cardiomyopathy where LV recovery fails to occur.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Cardiogenic shock and fulminant hemodynamic collapse requiring VAD/ECMO.
  - Malignant, life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias (sustained ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation) and sudden cardiac death.
  - High-degree atrioventricular block (complete 3rd-degree AV block), sinus arrest, and severe bradyarrhythmias.
  - Intracardiac mural thrombus formation and systemic or pulmonary thromboembolism (ischemic stroke, renal/splenic infarction).
  - Acute pericardial effusion and myopericarditis with cardiac tamponade.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Progression to chronic inflammatory Dilated Cardiomyopathy (DCM) and end-stage congestive heart failure in 10% to 20% of patients.
  - Persistent ventricular arrhythmias and chronic conduction system disease requiring permanent pacemaker or Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator (ICD) implantation.
  - Chronic apical ventricular aneurysm formation (classic in chronic Chagasic cardiomyopathy).
  - Requirement for heart transplantation or long-term VAD support.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Dallas Criteria: Classic histopathologic diagnostic criteria requiring inflammatory infiltrate and associated non-ischemic myocyte necrosis on biopsy.
  - Lake Louise Criteria: Diagnostic CMR criteria combining T2 edema, T1 early gadolinium enhancement, and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE).
  - Sinus Tachycardia Disproportionate to Fever: Signature physical sign of acute myocarditis.
  - Protease 2A: Enteroviral viral protease that directly cleaves host dystrophin, disrupting the sarcolemma and inducing cardiomyopathy.
  - Keshan Disease: Endemic cardiomyopathy in selenium-deficient regions of China caused by mutated, hypervirulent Coxsackievirus B.
  - Romana Sign: Unilateral painless palpebral edema and conjunctivitis pathognomonic for acute Chagas disease.
  - Subepicardial / Midwall LGE with Subendocardial Sparing: Non-ischemic pattern of Late Gadolinium Enhancement on CMR pathognomonic for myocarditis.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Fulminant vs. Nonfulminant Myocarditis Survival Paradox: Fulminant myocarditis presents with severe cardiogenic shock but has a dramatically BETTER long-term prognosis (>90% long-term survival with spontaneous recovery if supported with MCS) compared to subacute nonfulminant myocarditis, which frequently progresses to chronic DCM!
  - Corticosteroids Contraindicated in Acute Viral Myocarditis: Glucocorticoids enhance viral replication and myocyte destruction in acute viral myocarditis, yet are the treatment of choice in giant cell, sarcoid, and virus-negative autoimmune myocarditis!
  - Reversibility of Lyme High-Degree Heart Block: High-degree AV block in Lyme carditis is usually transient and completely resolves with IV ceftriaxone within 1 to 2 weeks, making permanent pacemaker insertion unnecessary in most cases!
  - Digitalis Toxicity Warning: Digitalis/digoxin increases myocardial inflammatory cytokine production and mortality in viral myocarditis models and must be used with extreme caution or avoided!
  - Troponin Elevation Absence: Troponin levels may be completely normal in up to 50% to 65% of biopsy-proven subacute or chronic myocarditis cases due to focal or indolent inflammation!
  - Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy Susceptibility: Patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy lack dystrophin and are exquisitely susceptible to enteroviral protease 2A cleavage of remaining cytoskeletal elements, accelerating severe viral cardiomyopathy.
