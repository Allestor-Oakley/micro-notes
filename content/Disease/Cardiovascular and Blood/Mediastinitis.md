# Mediastinitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Mediastinitis is an infection or invasive inflammatory process involving the structures of the mediastinum (the central compartment of the thoracic cavity bounded by the sternum anteriorly, the spine posteriorly, the lungs and pleura laterally, the thoracic inlet superiorly, and the diaphragm inferiorly). It encompasses acute necrotizing bacterial infections (secondary to esophageal perforation, descending neck infections, or cardiac surgery) as well as chronic fibroinflammatory conditions (fibrosing/sclerosing mediastinitis).
- **Major Classifications (e.g., Acute vs. Chronic, Community vs. Nosocomial):**
  - Acute Mediastinitis: Rapidly progressive, life-threatening infection categorized by underlying mechanism: (1) Post-Cardiothoracic Surgery Mediastinitis (poststernotomy mediastinitis / deep sternal wound infection, accounting for the vast majority of cases in modern surgical eras); (2) Esophageal Perforation Mediastinitis (iatrogenic, Boerhaave syndrome, foreign body, trauma, or neoplasm); (3) Descending Necrotizing Mediastinitis (DNM, extension of deep neck, pharyngeal, or odontogenic infections along fascial planes); and (4) Contiguous or Direct Spread Mediastinitis (extension from lung/pleural infection, osteomyelitis of sternum/ribs/spine, subphrenic abscess, or inhalational anthrax).
  - Chronic / Fibrosing / Sclerosing Mediastinitis: Non-suppurative, chronic fibroinflammatory process characterized by dense hyalinized fibrous tissue infiltrating and compressing mediastinal organs, vessels, and airways. Primarily an aberrant immunological reaction to Histoplasma capsulatum fungal antigens in mediastinal lymph nodes, but also seen with tuberculosis, actinomycosis, sarcoidosis, or IgG4-related disease.
  - Community-Acquired vs. Healthcare-Associated / Nosocomial: Community-acquired cases stem from spontaneous/iatrogenic esophageal perforation, foreign body ingestion, or descending neck infections (Ludwig angina, peritonsillar abscess). Healthcare-associated / nosocomial cases follow median sternotomy (CABG, valve replacement, heart transplantation), esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), or central line/ECMO placements.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Post-Cardiac Surgery Mediastinitis Epidemiology & Risk Factors: Occurs in 0.5% to 4% (generally <2% in modern series, ~0.3% to 0.9% in STS national database) of cardiac operations via median sternotomy. In outbreak settings, rates reach up to 23%. Associated with significant 30-day mortality (5% to 10%, historically 30% to 50%) and prolonged hospital stay (median 38 to 51 days). Preoperative risk factors: advanced age, diabetes mellitus, obesity (BMI >30 kg/m2 increases risk 2.2- to 6.5-fold), nasal colonization with Staphylococcus aureus, COPD, peripheral vascular disease, class 3-4 angina, hemodialysis-dependent renal failure, history of endocarditis, cigarette smoking, low cardiac output, and prolonged preoperative hospitalization. Operative risk factors: emergent surgery, heart transplantation, complex surgery, use of bilateral internal thoracic (mammary) arteries for CABG (doubles sternal wound risk due to sternal ischemia), prolonged operative or cardiopulmonary bypass time, core hyperthermia (>38°C) during bypass, and inappropriate antibiotic prophylaxis timing. Postoperative risk factors: reexploration for bleeding, prolonged mechanical ventilation (>48 hours), tracheostomy, lack of glycemic control, blood product transfusions (packed RBCs dose-dependently increase risk), intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP), mechanical support (VAD, ECMO), and postoperative MI.
  - Esophageal Perforation Epidemiology & Risk Factors: Historically the leading cause of mediastinitis before cardiac surgery. Spontaneous rupture (Boerhaave syndrome) accounts for 15% to 30% of cases. Currently, iatrogenic injury is the most common cause (flexible EGD perforation rate 0.074% to 0.4%; higher with prior mediastinal radiation, sclerotherapy, dilation, stenting, TEE, or transbronchial biopsy). Non-iatrogenic causes: swallowed foreign bodies (bones, coins, button batteries), caustic liquid ingestion, blunt/penetrating trauma, or esophageal carcinoma.
  - Descending Necrotizing Mediastinitis (DNM) Epidemiology & Risk Factors: Accounts for a minority of acute cases in the antibiotic era. Predominantly affects middle-aged men. Originates from odontogenic infections (Ludwig angina from mandibular second/third molars, accounting for 3.5% complication rate into mediastinitis), peritonsillar abscess, pharyngitis, epiglottitis, parotitis, or Lemierre syndrome.
  - Pediatric Population Risk Factors: Rates after pediatric cardiac surgery are lower (0.09% to 1.4%, average ~0.3%). Risk factors include young age (<90 days), genetic syndromes, ASA physical status class, complex surgery, prolonged ventilation, delayed sternal closure, and multiple blood transfusions.
  - Fibrosing Mediastinitis Epidemiology: Represents ~10% of primary mediastinal masses. Up to 73% to 84% of cases are linked to prior Histoplasma capsulatum infection (associated with HLA-DQB1*04:02 allele).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Post-Sternotomy Mediastinitis: Gram-positive cocci predominate (>70% of cases). Staphylococcus epidermidis and other coagulase-negative staphylococci (30%, range 6% to 45.5%, frequently biofilm producers); Staphylococcus aureus (25%, range 7.1% to 66.7%, including MSSA and MRSA); Enterococcus species (10%, range 8% to 18.8%); and Streptococcus species (2%). Gram-negative aerobic bacilli cause 15% to 20% of poststernotomy cases: Enterobacter species (10%), Escherichia coli (5%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (3%), Proteus species (2%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (2%), and Serratia marcescens. Candida albicans and non-albicans Candida species account for <2% to 20.5% (overall ~2-10%). Polymicrobial infection occurs in 10% of poststernotomy cases.
  - Esophageal Perforation & Descending Necrotizing Mediastinitis: Characterized by complex, synergistic polymicrobial oral microbiota combining oral anaerobes and aerobic/facultative bacteria. Anaerobes: Peptostreptococcus species, Prevotella species, Porphyromonas species, Bacteroides species, Fusobacterium species, Actinomyces species, Eubacterium, Lactobacillus, and Veillonella. Aerobic/facultative organisms: Viridans group streptococci, Streptococcus pyogenes, Staphylococcus aureus, Corynebacterium species, Moraxella, Enterobacteriaceae (E. coli, Klebsiella), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Eikenella corrodens, and Candida albicans.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups (e.g., Neonates, Elderly):**
  - Neonates & Infants: Staphylococcus aureus, CoNS, Group B Streptococcus, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella, and Candida species (associated with central lines, delayed sternal closure, or esophageal atresia repair).
  - Children: Staphylococcus aureus, CoNS, Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Gram-negative bacilli (higher proportion of Gram-negative pathogens in delayed sternal closure post-cardiac surgery).
  - Adults & Elderly: Poststernotomy: S. aureus (MSSA and MRSA), S. epidermidis, Enterococcus faecalis, Enterobacter, Klebsiella, E. coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In descending necrotizing mediastinitis: oral anaerobes (Prevotella, Fusobacterium, Peptostreptococcus) plus viridans streptococci. In esophageal perforation: mixed oral flora and GI Enterobacteriaceae.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Poststernotomy Outbreaks & Rare Bacteria: Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (Mycobacterium chimaera, Mycobacterium fortuitum, Mycobacterium chelonae, Mycobacterium wolinskyi outbreaks linked to colonized heater-cooler water units during cardiopulmonary bypass); Aspergillus fumigatus (airborne ICU/OR outbreak); Acinetobacter baumannii, Salmonella species, Legionella pneumophila, Burkholderia cepacia, Mycoplasma hominis, Nocardia species, Kluyvera, Gordonia sputi, Rhodococcus bronchialis, and Cytomegalovirus (CMV shedding associated with increased mortality).
  - Inhalational Anthrax: Bacillus anthracis spores cause severe, fatal hemorrhagic mediastinitis with mediastinal lymph node necrosis, edema, and widening.
  - Chronic / Fibrosing Mediastinitis Causes: Histoplasma capsulatum (predominant cause), Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Actinomyces israelii, Nocardia species, Blastomyces dermatitidis, Coccidioides immitis, Aspergillus species, Mucorales (Rhizopus, Conidiobolus), and Wuchereria bancrofti.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Direct Inoculation (Poststernotomy): Microorganisms from the patient's endogenous skin flora (S. aureus, S. epidermidis) or surgical team/environment contaminate the sternal incision, sternal wires, or mediastinal adipose tissue during surgery. Bacteria propagate in the protected, avascular retrosternal space, leading to sternal osteomyelitis, retrosternal abscesses, and sternal dehiscence.
  - Fascial Plane Extension (Descending Necrotizing Mediastinitis): Head and neck infections (Ludwig angina, peritonsillar abscess, pharyngeal space infections) spread into the mediastinum along three major cervical fascial spaces: (1) Retropharyngeal / Retrovisceral Space (accounts for 71% of cases, extending directly into the posterior mediastinum down to the diaphragm); (2) Viscerovascular / Lateral Pharyngeal Space (accounts for 21% of cases, tracking along the carotid sheath into the middle mediastinum); and (3) Pretracheal Space (accounts for 8% of cases, tracking into the anterior mediastinum). Spread is actively drawn downward by negative intrathoracic pressure generated during respiration.
  - Esophageal Contravasation / Spillage: Transmural esophageal breach (iatrogenic, Boerhaave, foreign body) spills gastric juice, saliva, bile, and digestive enzymes into the posterior mediastinum. This causes an initial severe, necrotizing chemical mediastinitis, followed rapidly by secondary polymicrobial bacterial and fungal infection.
  - Anthrax Inhalational Pathogenesis: Inhaled B. anthracis spores (1 to 5 µm) are phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages and transported via lymphatics to tracheobronchial and mediastinal lymph nodes, where spores germinate, multiply, and release lethal and edema exotoxins, causing widespread mediastinal lymphatic necrosis, severe hemorrhagic edema, and mediastinal widening.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Acute Tissue Response: Polymorphonuclear leukocyte infiltration, diffuse tissue edema, microvascular thrombosis, tissue necrosis, and purulent exudate accumulation within the fibrous compartments of the mediastinum. In poststernotomy cases, infection destroys retrosternal fat, causes sternal wire cut-through, osteomyelitis, and sternal instability. In DNM and esophageal rupture, bacterial synergy (anaerobes + aerobes) and digestive enzymes cause extensive fascial necrosis, phlegmon, and gas gangrene-like tissue destruction.
  - Chronic Fibroinflammatory Response (Fibrosing Mediastinitis): Persistent immunological hypersensitivity reaction to fungal antigens (Histoplasma capsulatum) within mediastinal lymph nodes. Triggers progressive, exuberant paucicellular hyalinized collagen deposition and dense fibrotic tissue infiltration that encases, compresses, and obliterates thin-walled mediastinal structures (superior vena cava, pulmonary veins, pulmonary arteries, main bronchi, esophagus).

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Poststernotomy Mediastinitis: Typically presents 2 to 4 weeks after surgery (median onset 10-14 days; early-onset <14 days often caused by Gram-negative bacilli or enterococci, late-onset >14 days dominated by S. aureus/CoNS). Symptoms: fever, chills, sternal/incisional chest pain (often pleuritic, greater than normal postoperative pain), purulent wound drainage, redness, sternal instability ("clicking" or movement of sternum), and dyspnea. May present subtly without local signs, with fever or unexplained septic shock as the sole manifestation.
  - Esophageal Perforation (Boerhaave / Iatrogenic): Abrupt onset of excruciating, severe substernal or precordial chest pain, shortness of breath, odynophagia, dysphagia, vomiting/retching history (in Boerhaave syndrome), or recent upper GI endoscopy/dilation. Pain may localize to epigastrium or radiate to the back and interscapular region.
  - Descending Necrotizing Mediastinitis: History of recent dental infection, tooth extraction, sore throat, or neck swelling. Symptoms include severe neck pain, odynophagia, dysphagia, trismus, hoarseness, high fevers, chills, and progressive dyspnea.
  - Chronic Fibrosing Mediastinitis: Insidious onset of exertional dyspnea, chronic cough, chest pain, exercise intolerance, hemoptysis, recurrent bronchitis/pneumonia, orthopnea, or facial swelling/headache.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Poststernotomy Signs: Sternal wound erythema, warmth, cellulitis, purulent discharge, dehiscence, sternal instability/mobility on palpation, bubbles emanating from sternal wound, and sternal click. Fever and tachycardia. (In transplant patients, fever and leukocytosis may be absent due to immunosuppression).
  - Esophageal Perforation / Pneumomediastinum Signs: Tachycardia, fever, subcutaneous emphysema / crepitus palpable over the neck or upper chest wall. Hamman Sign: A crunching, rasping, or clicking sound heard over the precordium synchronous with the heartbeat (present in ~50% of pneumomediastinum cases). Distant or muffled heart sounds.
  - Descending Necrotizing Mediastinitis Signs: Brawny induration, erythema, tenderness, and fluctuating swelling of the submandibular/neck region (Ludwig angina features), cervical crepitus, stridor, and systemic sepsis/shock.
  - Fibrosing Mediastinitis Signs: Superior Vena Cava (SVC) Syndrome: Plethora, facial/neck/upper torso edema, distended neck and collateral chest wall veins, headache, and visual disturbances. Signs of pulmonary vein stenosis ("pseudo-mitral stenosis") or pulmonary artery hypertension (right heart failure, peripheral edema, jugular venous distention).
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Hamman Sign: Precordial crunching or rasping sound synchronous with cardiac contraction, pathognomonic for pneumomediastinum / mediastinal emphysema.
  - Bubbles Emanating from Sternal Wound: Pathognomonic physical sign of deep poststernotomy mediastinal infection communicating with the atmosphere.
  - Mackler Triad (Classic for Boerhaave Syndrome): Vomiting + severe chest pain + subcutaneous emphysema.
  - SVC Syndrome with Mediastinal Mass: Facio-cervical edema, venous engorgement, and upper torso plethora pathognomonic for fibrosing mediastinitis compressing the superior vena cava.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - CBC: Leukocytosis with a leftward shift (elevated band forms) in acute infectious mediastinitis; may be absent or blunted in transplant recipients or immunocompromised hosts.
  - Inflammatory Markers: C-reactive Protein (CRP) and Serum Procalcitonin (PCT) are markedly elevated. Procalcitonin is superior to CRP in diagnosing post-cardiopulmonary bypass mediastinitis and in differentiating bacterial infection from acute rejection in heart transplant recipients.
  - Blood Cultures: Routine blood culture sets should be drawn before antibiotic administration. Positive in a high percentage of S. aureus and Gram-negative poststernotomy cases (S. aureus bacteremia in the first 60 days post-sternotomy has a 90% positive predictive value for mediastinitis). Positive in <10% of CoNS cases.
- **Imaging Modalities (e.g., X-ray, CT, Ultrasound):**
  - Plain Chest Radiography: Routine post-op X-rays are generally insensitive for early poststernotomy mediastinitis due to normal postsurgical changes. Chest X-ray may show pneumomediastinum (air tracking along mediastinal fascial lines, pneumopericardium), mediastinal widening, pleural effusions (common in Boerhaave syndrome), or apical pulmonary infiltrates. In anthrax, shows marked mediastinal widening and pleural effusions.
  - Computed Tomography (CT) with Contrast: Diagnostic imaging modality of choice for all forms of mediastinitis. Sensitivity 87.6%, specificity 90.5%. Key CT findings in acute mediastinitis: retrosternal soft tissue swelling, fluid and gas collections, retrosternal attenuation >20 Hounsfield units, sternal dehiscence or erosion, pericardial/pleural effusions, and fascial plane edema extending into the mediastinum. In fibrosing mediastinitis: calcified or non-calcified infiltrating mediastinal mass obliterating fat planes and compressing SVC, pulmonary vessels, or airways.
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): High soft tissue resolution, but less commonly used in acute poststernotomy settings due to metallic sternal wires, pacemakers, or vascular clips. Useful in fibrosing mediastinitis to delineate vessel patency when CT contrast is contraindicated.
  - Nuclear Medicine / PET Imaging: Indium-111 labeled leukocyte scan or 18F-FDG PET/CT helpful when CT is equivocal or to differentiate Dressler syndrome from mediastinitis.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Direct Operative & Tissue Cultures: Gold standard diagnostic yield comes from deep intraoperative mediastinal fluid, purulent aspirates, sternal bone/cartilage biopsy, or debrided tissue submitted for Gram stain, aerobic/anaerobic bacterial culture, fungal culture, and mycobacterial culture. Multiple tissue samples improve diagnostic yield.
  - CT-Guided Percutaneous Aspiration: CT-guided needle aspiration of mediastinal fluid or retrosternal collections provides rapid microbiologic identification in non-operable or ambiguous cases.
  - Serology & Histopathology (Fibrosing Mediastinitis): Fungal stains (GMS, PAS) on surgical biopsy showing yeast structures consistent with Histoplasma capsulatum (cultures usually negative); serology for Histoplasma antibodies (complement fixation, immunodiffusion).
  - Epicardial Pacer Wire Cultures: Controversial; high false-positive rate (PPV only 8% to 11%), not recommended for routine screening, but may support diagnosis if clinical suspicion is high.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Direct surgical exploration demonstrating retrosternal/mediastinal pus, tissue necrosis, or sternal osteomyelitis combined with positive microbiological cultures or characteristic histopathology from deep mediastinal tissue specimens remains the definitive gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Airway & Hemodynamic Resuscitation: Immediate endotracheal intubation or emergency tracheostomy for airway protection in descending necrotizing mediastinitis (due to severe upper airway edema / Ludwig angina). Fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support for septic shock.
  - Glycemic Control & Transfusion Protocol: Strict perioperative blood glucose control (target <180 mg/dL) and minimizing unnecessary packed red blood cell transfusions.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Empiric Post-Cardiothoracic Surgery Mediastinitis: Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h (targeting serum trough 15-20 mcg/mL or AUC/MIC 400-600) PLUS Cefepime 2 g IV q8h OR Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h OR Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h (to cover MRSA, CoNS, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and resistant Enterobacteriaceae).
  - Empiric Esophageal Perforation & Descending Necrotizing Mediastinitis: Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h OR Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h OR Carbapenem (Meropenem 1 g IV q8h / Imipenem 500 mg IV q6h) OR Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h + Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h (must cover oral streptococci, Enterobacteriaceae, and oral anaerobes like Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Bacteroides, and Fusobacterium).
  - Empiric Inhalational Anthrax Mediastinitis: Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h (or Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h) PLUS a protein synthesis inhibitor (Clindamycin 900 mg IV q8h or Linezolid 600 mg IV q12h) PLUS a third agent (e.g., Meropenem).
- **Alternative Regimens (e.g., for Penicillin Allergy):**
  - Gram-Positive / MRSA Coverage (Vancomycin Allergy / Intolerance): Daptomycin 6 to 8 mg/kg IV q24h OR Linezolid 600 mg IV/PO q12h.
  - Gram-Negative Coverage (Severe Penicillin / Beta-Lactam Allergy): Aztreonam 2 g IV q8h OR Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q8h / Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h.
  - Anaerobic Alternatives: Clindamycin 600-900 mg IV q8h OR Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h.
  - Targeted Directed Therapies:
    - Methicillin-Susceptible S. aureus (MSSA): Cefazolin 2 g IV q8h OR Nafcillin/Oxacillin 2 g IV q4h.
    - Candida Mediastinitis: Echinocandin (Caspofungin 70 mg load then 50 mg IV daily, Micafungin 100 mg IV daily, or Anidulafungin 200 mg load then 100 mg IV daily) OR Fluconazole 400-800 mg IV daily (for susceptible Candida species).
    - Treatment Duration: Antibiotic therapy for acute infectious mediastinitis is typically administered for a minimum of 4 to 6 weeks (longer if concomitant sternal osteomyelitis is present).
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions (e.g., Drainage, Debridement):**
  - Poststernotomy Mediastinitis Surgical Debridement: Emergency surgical re-exploration, complete sternal opening, radical debridement of all infected retrosternal tissue, necrotic bone, and devitalized cartilage, followed by:
    1. Vacuum-Assisted Closure / Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (NPWT): Modern gold standard first-line management; applied after debridement as a bridge to definitive closure, reducing mortality and hospital stay.
    2. Reconstruction Modalities: Sternal rewiring / rigid fixation, closed-drainage continuous irrigation systems, or soft tissue flap reconstruction (pectoralis major muscle flaps or rectus abdominis / omental flaps).
  - Esophageal Perforation Management: Early recognition (<24 hours) managed with primary surgical repair and drainage, or endoscopic placement of fully covered self-expanding metal stents (SEMS) combined with broad-spectrum antibiotics and percutaneous/mediastinal tube drainage.
  - Descending Necrotizing Mediastinitis Drainage: Extensive surgical drainage based on anatomical level: (1) Superior Mediastinum (above carina): Cervicotomy with transcervical mediastinal drainage; (2) Deep / Inferior Mediastinum (below carina): Transthoracic approach via thoracotomy, median sternotomy, or Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery (VATS) with decortication and aggressive mediastinal irrigation.
  - Fibrosing Mediastinitis Interventions: Antifungal therapy has no established benefit. Treatment focuses on palliative vascular/airway relief: percutaneous intravascular stent placement (for SVC syndrome or pulmonary vein/artery stenosis) or surgical bypass grafting.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Local Extension: Sternal osteomyelitis, sternal dehiscence/fracture, costochondritis (indolent infection of rib cartilages requiring resection), pericardial effusion, cardiac tamponade, purulent pericarditis, empyema, pleural effusion, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and peritonitis (via transdiaphragmatic extension).
  - Systemic: Sepsis, septic shock, bacteremia, multiorgan failure, and early postoperative mortality (5% to 10% in poststernotomy, 11% to 25% in DNM, 10% to 25% in esophageal rupture).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Sternal deformity, chronic chest wall pain, and requirement for extensive reconstructive plastic surgery (muscle/omental flaps).
  - Long-term mortality increase (59% increase in 10-year mortality following poststernotomy mediastinitis).
  - Fibrosing Mediastinitis Sequelae: Superior Vena Cava Syndrome, severe pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale, right heart failure, bronchial/tracheal stenosis, esophagobronchial fistulas, and lethal pulmonary vein stenosis (high mortality following pulmonary vein stent procedures).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Hamman Sign: Precordial crunching or rasping sound synchronous with heartbeat caused by mediastinal emphysema.
  - Boerhaave Syndrome: Spontaneous transmural rupture of the esophagus secondary to violent vomiting or retching.
  - Ludwig Angina: Bilateral cellulitis of the submandibular and sublingual spaces arising from mandibular molar infection, tracking into the mediastinum.
  - Danger Space: Fascial space posterior to the retropharyngeal space extending seamlessly from the skull base down to the diaphragm, serving as a primary highway for descending necrotizing mediastinitis.
  - Skeletonized Internal Mammary Artery Harvesting: Surgical technique harvesting the internal thoracic artery without adjacent tissue, preserving sternal blood supply and reducing mediastinitis risk compared to pedicled harvesting.
  - Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (NPWT) / VAC: Transformed poststernotomy mediastinitis outcomes, reducing mortality from >30% to <5%.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Spontaneous Pneumomediastinum Is NOT Mediastinitis: Spontaneous pneumomediastinum resulting from alveolar rupture (Macklin effect) is a benign, self-limited condition that requires NO antibiotics, NO surgical drainage, and often no hospital admission!
  - Absence of Fever & Leukocytosis in Heart Transplant Recipients: Transplant patients with poststernotomy mediastinitis frequently lack typical fever or elevated WBC count due to heavy immunosuppression; serum procalcitonin helps differentiate infection from organ rejection.
  - Absence of Local Signs in Poststernotomy Mediastinitis: Up to 30% of coagulase-negative staphylococcal poststernotomy cases lack local wound redness or pus, presenting solely as unexplained low-grade fever or subtle sternal clicking.
  - Mupirocin Prevention Targets ALL S. aureus: Preoperative nasal mupirocin reduces poststernotomy mediastinitis and MUST be targeted to patients colonized with ANY S. aureus strain (MSSA or MRSA), not restricted to MRSA alone!
  - Antifungal Therapy Useless in Fibrosing Mediastinitis: Antifungal agents (e.g., itraconazole) provide NO proven clinical benefit in chronic fibrosing mediastinitis because the condition is an sterile/hyperimmune fibrotic response to fungal antigens rather than active fungal replication.
  - Epicardial Pacer Wire Cultures Misleading: Routine culturing of epicardial pacing wires removed after cardiac surgery yields an unacceptably high false-positive rate (PPV only 8% to 11%) and should NOT be performed routinely.
