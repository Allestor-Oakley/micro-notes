# Pericarditis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Inflammation of the parietal and visceral layers of the pericardium, clinically manifested individually or in combination by chest pain, a characteristic pericardial friction rub, pericardial effusion, and electrographic subepicardial inflammatory changes. In severe or rapidly progressive cases, accumulation of pericardial fluid increases intrapericardial pressure, resulting in cardiac tamponade, or chronic organizing inflammation leads to constrictive pericarditis or effusive-constrictive pericarditis.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Clinical Progression & Velocity: (1) Acute Pericarditis (rapid onset of symptoms lasting less than 4 to 6 weeks, characterized by acute inflammatory exudate, sharp positional chest pain, friction rub, and widespread ST-segment elevation); (2) Incessant Pericarditis (pericarditis lasting more than 4 to 6 weeks but less than 3 months without remission); (3) Chronic Pericarditis (inflammation or effusion lasting longer than 3 months, often progressing to constrictive pericarditis); (4) Recurrent Pericarditis (rebound flare of pericardial inflammation occurring after a documented initial episode and symptom-free interval of 4 to 6 weeks or more).
  - Etiologic / Pathologic Subtypes: (1) Infectious Pericarditis (viral/idiopathic, purulent/suppurative bacterial, tuberculous, fungal, parasitic); (2) Noninfectious Pericarditis (acute myocardial infarction / Dressler post-cardiac injury syndrome, uremic, neoplastic, radiation-induced, collagen vascular / systemic autoimmune disease, drug-induced, or dissecting aortic aneurysm); (3) Auto-inflammatory vs. Autoimmune Pericarditis (autoinflammatory mediated by inflammasome activation and IL-1 release; autoimmune associated with type I interferon signature and systemic autoimmune diseases).
  - Hemodynamic / Anatomic Subtypes: (1) Dry / Fibrinous Pericarditis; (2) Effusive Pericarditis (serous, serofibrinous, serosanguineous, or purulent fluid accumulation); (3) Cardiac Tamponade (rapid intrapericardial pressure rise causing diastolic filling limitation, decreased stroke volume, and circulatory collapse); (4) Constrictive Pericarditis (dense fibrous thickening, scarring, and calcification obliterating the pericardial cavity and encasing the heart); (5) Effusive-Constrictive Pericarditis (coexistence of persistent visceral pericardial constriction despite removal of parietal pericardial fluid via pericardiocentesis).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:** Idiopathic acute pericarditis accounts for 40% to 86% of hospitalized acute pericarditis cases in Western series, with viral pathogens presumed responsible for most idiopathic cases. Purulent bacterial pericarditis is rare in the modern antibiotic era (less than 1% to 2% of medical pericarditis), occurring as a complication of pneumococcal/staphylococcal pneumonia, thoracic surgery, trauma, or infective endocarditis. Mycobacterium tuberculosis causes less than 5% of acute pericarditis in North America and Europe, but is the leading cause of pericarditis, pericardial effusions, and constrictive pericarditis in developing nations, Sub-Saharan Africa, and HIV/AIDS populations (where tuberculous pericarditis causes up to 50% to 80% of effusions). Risk factors for infectious pericarditis include recent viral respiratory or GI infection, open cardiothoracic or esophageal surgery, chest trauma, indwelling mediastinal drains, severe immunosuppression, advanced HIV/AIDS, end-stage renal disease / uremia, bacteremia or endocarditis, and active pulmonary tuberculosis.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Viruses / Idiopathic: Enteroviruses (Coxsackievirus B serotypes 1-6, Coxsackievirus A, Echoviruses, Nonpolio enteroviruses) are the most frequently implicated viral causes. Other cardiotropic viruses include Parvovirus B19, Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6), Adenoviruses, Influenza A and B viruses, Human Cytomegalovirus (HCMV), Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV), Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV), Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV), Hepatitis B virus, Mumps virus, Rubella virus, Arboviruses (Dengue, Chikungunya), and Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV-1).
  - Bacteria: Staphylococcus aureus (most common cause of purulent bacterial pericarditis, especially post-surgical, post-traumatic, or secondary to endocarditis perivalvular ring abscesses), Streptococcus pneumoniae (historically primary, now reduced due to vaccines and early antibiotic treatment of pneumonia), other Streptococcal species (Streptococcus pyogenes, Group B streptococci, Group G streptococci, Streptococcus milleri/anginosus group), Neisseria meningitidis (Group C primary meningococcal pericarditis in adults), Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Haemophilus influenzae (type b, primary cause in unimmunized children), and Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children & Adolescents: Staphylococcus aureus, Haemophilus influenzae type b (markedly reduced in vaccinated populations), Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus pyogenes, Enteroviruses (Coxsackievirus B, Echoviruses), Adenoviruses, Mumps virus, Parvovirus B19, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae.
  - Adults & Elderly: Viral/idiopathic agents (Coxsackievirus B, Echoviruses, Influenza, Parvovirus B19, HHV-6), Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Gram-negative bacilli (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella species, Yersinia enterocolitica, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella), Anaerobes (Bacteroides fragilis, Peptostreptococcus), Mycobacterium tuberculosis (especially in elderly or immunocompromised hosts), Coxiella burnetii (Q fever), Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme pericarditis), and Legionella pneumophila.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Mycobacteria: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (Mycobacterium avium-intracellulare complex, Mycobacterium kansasii, Mycobacterium bovis subsp. caprae).
  - Fastidious Bacteria, Anaerobes & Zoonoses: Actinomyces species (Actinomyces israelii causing lumpy jaw/thoracic extension), Nocardia species, Anaerobic bacteria (Bacteroides fragilis, Fusobacterium, Peptostreptococcus, often from malignant esophagopericardial or gastropericardial fistulas), Francisella tularensis, Brucella melitensis, Listeria monocytogenes, Campylobacter fetus, Chlamydia species, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Ureaplasma species, and Corynebacterium diphtheriae.
  - Fungi: Histoplasma capsulatum (frequent cause of acute sterile/reactive pericarditis during urban outbreaks or direct infection in disseminated disease), Coccidioides immitis/posadasii, Candida species (Candida albicans, Candida glabrata; in severely debilitated, neutropenic, post-cardiac surgery, or ICU patients), Aspergillus species (Aspergillus fumigatus, Aspergillus flavus; in severely immunocompromised hosts or post-transplant), Blastomyces dermatitidis, and Cryptococcus neoformans.
  - Parasites: Entamoeba histolytica (amebic pericarditis from rupture of left lobe amebic liver abscess into pericardial sac), Toxoplasma gondii, Toxocara canis (toxocaral tamponade), and Schistosoma.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Direct & Hematogenous Routes: Cardiotropic viruses reach the visceral and parietal pericardium via hematogenous dissemination. Bacterial pericarditis develops through four main mechanisms: (1) Direct spread from a contiguous focus of chest infection (pneumonia, empyema, mediastinitis); (2) Direct extension from an intracardiac focus (infective endocarditis perivalvular ring abscess breaching into pericardium); (3) Hematogenous seeding during distant bacteremia; or (4) Direct inoculation via penetrating chest trauma, thoracic surgery, or sternal wound infection. Tuberculous pericarditis spreads via retrograde lymphatic flow from infected tracheobronchial or mediastinal lymph nodes, hematogenous seeding during primary infection, or direct contiguous spread from lung/pleura. Fungal and amebic pericarditis occur via hematogenous dissemination or direct extension (e.g., rupture of hepatic amebic abscess through diaphragm).
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Inflammatory Exudate & Pathologic Stages: Injury to mesothelial pericardial surfaces induces vascular permeability, exudation of fluid, fibrin, and inflammatory cells. Fluid may be serous, serofibrinous, serosanguineous, or frankly purulent. Autoinflammatory pericarditis is driven by inflammasome activation releasing IL-1, recruiting neutrophils and macrophages; autoimmune pericarditis features a type I interferon signature.
  - Tuberculous Pericarditis Pathologic Stages: Four progressive stages: Stage 1 (Fibrinous exudate with abundant acid-fast bacilli, granulomas, and PMNs); Stage 2 (Serosanguineous pericardial effusion with lymphocytic and plasma cell predominance, where bacilli become hard to find); Stage 3 (Absorption of effusion, pericardial thickening, proliferation of granulomas, and dense fibrin deposition); Stage 4 (Constriction, obliteration of pericardial space by dense fibrous adhesions, thick parietal collagen, cholesterol crystal deposition, and calcification).
  - Tamponade & Constriction Mechanics: Rapid accumulation of pericardial fluid exceeds pericardial compliance, increasing intrapericardial pressure. Intrapericardial pressure equals or exceeds ventricular end-diastolic pressure, restricting diastolic ventricular filling, reducing stroke volume, lowering cardiac output, and causing obstructive shock. In constrictive pericarditis, dense fibrous/calcified pericardial scar encases the heart, preventing mid-to-late diastolic filling and creating dissociation between intrathoracic and intracardiac pressures during respiration.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Acute Pericarditis: Abrupt onset of sharp, severe retrosternal or precordial chest pain radiating to the neck, jaw, left shoulder, or trapezius ridge (the trapezius border radiation is highly specific!). Pain is characteristically positional and pleuritic: aggravated by inspiration, coughing, swallowing, and lying flat (supine), and relieved by sitting up and leaning forward. Accompanied by low-grade fever, chills, malaise, myalgias, and dyspnea.
  - Purulent / Bacterial Pericarditis: High spiking fevers, rigors, profuse diaphoresis, severe dyspnea, and acute toxic illness. Chest pain is reported in only one-third of purulent cases! Symptoms of systemic infection or underlying pneumonia often obscure pericardial involvement.
  - Tuberculous Pericarditis: Insidious onset of vague chest pain (39% to 76%), weight loss, night sweats, chronic cough, dyspnea, fever, and fatigue over weeks to months.
  - Cardiac Tamponade Symptoms: Severe, rapidly progressive dyspnea, tachypnea, orthopnea, dizziness, presyncope, syncope, cold diaphoresis, and anxiety.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Pericardial Friction Rub: The superficial, high-pitched, scratching, or grating sound heard best with the diaphragm of the stethoscope pressed firmly over the left lower sternal border during end-expiration while leaning forward. Classic rub has three components corresponding to cardiac motion: (1) Atrial systole (presystolic component); (2) Ventricular systole (systolic component, loudest and most constant); and (3) Early diastolic ventricular filling (early diastolic component). Present in ~50% of acute cases; evanescent and variable.
  - Cardiac Tamponade Signs: Beck Triad: (1) Hypotension / low systemic arterial pressure; (2) Distended neck veins / elevated jugular venous pressure; and (3) Muffled / distant heart sounds. Pulsus Paradoxus: An exaggerated drop in systemic systolic blood pressure of >10 mm Hg during quiet inspiration. Jugular Venous Pulse: Prominent x-descent with loss or attenuation of the y-descent. Tachycardia, tachypnea, cold clammy extremities, and clear lung fields (differentiating tamponade from cardiogenic shock).
  - Constrictive Pericarditis Signs: Signs of chronic right heart failure: marked JVP elevation, Kussmaul sign (paradoxical rise or lack of fall in JVP during inspiration), prominent y-descent (rapid early filling), Pericardial Knock (a high-pitched early diastolic sound occurring shortly after S2 due to abrupt arrest of ventricular filling), hepatomegaly, ascites, peripheral edema, and pulsus paradoxus (present in 20% to 30%).
  - Ewart Sign: Area of dullness and bronchial breath sounds on percussion and auscultation at the angle of the left scapula due to compression of the left lung lower lobe by a large pericardial effusion.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Trapezius Ridge Pain Radiation: Sharp precordial chest pain radiating specifically to the bilateral trapezius ridge (innervated by phrenic nerve) pathognomonic for pericardial inflammation.
  - Three-Component Pericardial Friction Rub: High-pitched scratching sound with presystolic, systolic, and early diastolic phases.
  - Beck Triad: Combination of hypotension, distended neck veins, and muffled heart sounds pathognomonic for acute cardiac tamponade.
  - Kussmaul Sign & Pericardial Knock: Inspiration-induced rise in JVP paired with an early diastolic cardiac knock sound pathognomonic for constrictive pericarditis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis with a left shift in acute bacterial/purulent pericarditis; normal or mild elevation in viral cases; lymphopenia or leukopenia in HIV/viral cases.
  - Inflammatory Markers: C-reactive Protein (CRP) and Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) are elevated in >90% of acute cases and serve as objective parameters to monitor disease activity and therapeutic response.
  - Cardiac Troponins (cTnI / cTnT): Elevated in 30% to 50% of acute pericarditis cases due to concomitant superficial epicardial inflammation (myopericarditis); elevation does not alter overall favorable outcome if LV function remains normal.
  - Blood Cultures & Tuberculin / IGRA Testing: Blood cultures drawn in all acutely ill or febrile patients (positive in bacteremic purulent pericarditis); Interferon-Gamma Release Assay (IGRA / QuantiFERON) or TST for suspected tuberculous pericarditis.
  - Electrocardiogram (ECG): Abnormal in >90% of acute pericarditis cases. Classic evolution through 4 distinct stages:
    - Stage I (First few days): Widespread, diffuse concave ST-segment elevation in almost all lead groups (I, II, III, aVF, V2-V6) with reciprocal ST depression only in aVR and V1; accompanying PR-segment depression in multiple leads (PR elevation in aVR) reflecting atrial subepicardial injury (a highly sensitive early sign!).
    - Stage II (Several days later): ST segments return to baseline; T-wave flattening.
    - Stage III: Diffuse T-wave inversions develop after ST segments have normalized.
    - Stage IV (Weeks to months): ECG returns to baseline normal.
    - Additional Features: Low QRS voltage and Electrical Alternans (beat-to-back alternation of QRS height and axis due to swinging heart in large effusions) pathognomonic for cardiac tamponade.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Echocardiography: Key initial noninvasive test of choice. Demonstrates pericardial fluid accumulation (hypoechoic space surrounding heart), measures effusion size, and detects hemodynamic compromise. Echocardiographic signs of Cardiac Tamponade: (1) Right ventricular early diastolic collapse / indention; (2) Right atrial late diastolic / early systolic collapse (lasting >1/3 of cardiac cycle); (3) Exaggerated respiratory variation in AV blood flow velocities (>50% increase in tricuspid, >25% decrease in mitral inflow velocity on inspiration); (4) Dilated inferior vena cava with loss of inspiratory collapse ("plethora"). In Constrictive Pericarditis: Pericardial thickening/calcification, abrupt flattening of LV posterior wall in diastole, ventricular septal bounce on inspiration, and hepatic vein expiration flow reversal.
  - Computed Tomography (CT): Delineates pericardial thickness (>3 mm indicates abnormal thickening), quantifies effusion density (attenuation >20 to 30 Hounsfield units suggests purulent, hemopericardium, or exudative effusion), detects pericardial calcifications (diagnostic of chronic constrictive pericarditis), and evaluates contiguous chest pathology (pneumonia, mass, mediastinitis).
  - Cardiac Magnetic Resonance (CMR) Imaging: Superior soft tissue resolution. Detects pericardial inflammation via Late Gadolinium Enhancement (LGE) and T2-weighted hyperintensity of parietal/visceral pericardium; Delayed Hyperenhancement (DHE) guides anti-inflammatory treatment duration. Evaluates ventricular septal motion and ventricular interdependence in constrictive pericarditis.
  - Plain Chest Radiography: May be normal in acute dry pericarditis. In large effusions (>250 mL), shows enlarged globular "water-bottle" cardiac silhouette with clear lung fields. Chest X-ray may show eggshell pericardial calcification in chronic constrictive pericarditis.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Pericardial Fluid Analysis: Pericardiocentesis or surgical drainage aspirates fluid for gross appearance, cell count/differential, protein, LDH, glucose, Gram stain, Ziehl-Neelsen / acid-fast stain, fungal stains (GMS, PAS), bacterial/fungal/mycobacterial cultures, and cytology. Purulent fluid has high PMN count (>50,000/mm3), low glucose, and high protein/LDH.
  - Molecular Diagnostics (PCR on Fluid & Tissue): Real-time PCR and RT-PCR on pericardial fluid or pericardial biopsy specimens for viral genomes (Enteroviruses, PVB19, HHV-6, Adenovirus) and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (NAAT / GeneXpert MTB/RIF). PCR significantly increases diagnostic yield in culture-negative or fastidious cases.
  - Pericardial Biopsy & Histopathology: Percutaneous or surgical pericardial biopsy yielding parietal tissue for histology and culture. Demonstrates granulomas with caseating necrosis and multinucleated giant cells (Langhans cells) in tuberculous pericarditis, acute neutrophilic/fibrinous inflammation in purulent cases, or dense collagenous fibrosis in constriction. Combines with fluid analysis to raise diagnostic yield from 20-25% to 54%.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Histopathologic demonstration of specific pericardial inflammation or granulomas combined with microbiological culture/PCR isolation of the pathogen from resected pericardial tissue or pericardial fluid aspirate remains the definitive gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Emergency Pericardiocentesis / Drainage: Cardiac tamponade is a life-threatening medical emergency requiring immediate pericardiocentesis (echocardiographic or fluoroscopic guided catheter aspiration) or emergency subxiphoid pericardiotomy. Resuscitate with IV boluses of normal saline to increase intravascular volume and preload pending drainage. Avoid positive-pressure mechanical ventilation if possible, as positive pressure decreases venous return and worsens tamponade!
  - Physical Rest: Restriction of physical activity / bed rest during acute pericarditis until complete resolution of fever, symptoms, and normalization of inflammatory markers (CRP).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Empiric Viral / Idiopathic Acute Pericarditis: High-dose NSAIDs (Ibuprofen 600 to 800 mg PO q8h OR Aspirin 750 to 1000 mg PO q8h) for 1 to 2 weeks with gradual taper PLUS Colchicine 0.5 mg PO once daily (if weight <70 kg) or 0.5 mg PO twice daily (if weight >=70 kg) for 3 months (colchicine reduces symptom persistence at 72 hours and halves recurrence rates!).
  - Empiric Purulent / Bacterial Pericarditis: Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h PLUS Cefepime 2 g IV q8h OR Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h (must cover MSSA, MRSA, S. pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacteriaceae).
  - Tuberculous Pericarditis Regimen: 4-drug antituberculous therapy for 2 months (Isoniazid 300 mg daily + Rifampin 600 mg daily + Pyrazinamide 1500 mg daily + Ethambutol 1200 mg daily), followed by 2-drug therapy (Isoniazid + Rifampin) for 4 months (total 6 months). Adjunctive Prednisolone regimen: 60 mg daily for 4 weeks, 30 mg daily for 4 weeks, 15 mg daily for 2 weeks, and 5 mg daily for 1 week (significantly reduces development of constrictive pericarditis, death, and need for repeat pericardiocentesis!).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Recurrent / Corticosteroid-Dependent / Refractory Pericarditis:
    - Anakinra (Recombinant IL-1 Beta Receptor Antagonist): Subcutaneous anakinra (AIRTRIP trial protocol: 100 mg SC daily) for colchicine-resistant or corticosteroid-dependent recurrent pericarditis.
    - Corticosteroids (Prednisone 0.2 to 0.5 mg/kg/day): Reserved ONLY as second-line therapy for acute pericarditis when NSAIDs/colchicine are contraindicated or failed, or in specific autoimmune/uremic pericarditis. (Avoid steroids in acute viral/idiopathic cases as early steroid use promotes recurrent pericarditis!).
  - Bacterial / Fungal Alternatives: Daptomycin 6-8 mg/kg IV q24h or Linezolid 600 mg PO/IV q12h for MRSA; Lipid formulation of Amphotericin B or Fluconazole / Echinocandin for fungal (Candida/Aspergillus) pericarditis.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Surgical Pericardial Drainage (Pericardiostomy / Pericardial Window): Subxiphoid pericardiotomy or Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery (VATS) creation of a pericardial window with chest tube placement. Mandatory for purulent pericarditis, loculated effusions, recurrent tamponade, or failed pericardiocentesis.
  - Pericardiectomy (Total Pericardial Resection): Surgical excision of parietal and visceral pericardium ("phrenic-to-phrenic" resection). Definitive surgical treatment of choice for chronic constrictive pericarditis, refractory effusive-constrictive pericarditis, or severe recurrent pericarditis unresponsive to medical therapy.
  - Intrapericardial Fibrinolysis: Intrapericardial instillation of streptokinase or tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) via pericardial catheter to break up loculations and fibrinous adhesions in purulent pericarditis, reducing need for early pericardiectomy.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Acute Cardiac Tamponade with obstructive shock and circulatory collapse.
  - Purulent pericardial loculation, pericardial abscess formation, and extension into myocardium or mediastinum.
  - Acute myopericarditis with transient ventricular dysfunction or arrhythmias.
  - High acute inpatient mortality in untreated or postoperative purulent pericarditis (overall mortality ~30%).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Recurrent / Relapsing Pericarditis (occurs in 15% to 30% of patients after initial acute episode; reduced to <15% with adjunctive colchicine).
  - Chronic Constrictive Pericarditis (occurs in <1% of viral/idiopathic cases, 2% to 5% of vasculitic/neoplastic, 20% to 50% of tuberculous pericarditis despite treatment, and up to 30% of purulent bacterial cases).
  - Effusive-Constrictive Pericarditis requiring complete surgical pericardiectomy.
  - Chronic right heart failure, hepatic congestion / cardiac cirrhosis, and physical disability.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Beck Triad: Hypotension + distended neck veins + muffled heart sounds (cardiac tamponade).
  - Pulsus Paradoxus: Exaggerated drop in systolic BP >10 mm Hg during quiet inspiration.
  - Hamman Sign: Precordial crunching sound synchronous with heartbeat in pneumomediastinum.
  - Water-Bottle Heart: Symmetrical globular cardiac silhouette enlargement on chest X-ray in large effusions.
  - Trapezius Ridge Pain: Pathognomonic radiation of pericardial pain along phrenic nerve distributions.
  - Electrical Alternans: Beat-to-beat variation in QRS height and amplitude caused by heart swinging in large fluid-filled pericardial sac.
  - Ewart Sign: Bronchial breath sounds and dullness at left scapular angle from lung compression by large effusion.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Steroids Exacerbate Viral Pericarditis Recurrence: Administration of corticosteroids during acute viral pericarditis enhances viral replication, delays clearance, and dramatically increases the risk of developing recurrent/relapsing pericarditis!
  - Absence of Chest Pain & Friction Rub in Purulent Pericarditis: Chest pain is absent in two-thirds and friction rub is absent in two-thirds of patients with purulent bacterial pericarditis; diagnosis is frequently missed until autopsy or tamponade occurs!
  - Absence of Enlarged Heart in Acute Tamponade: If pericardial fluid accumulates rapidly (e.g., trauma, aortic dissection), fatal cardiac tamponade occurs with as little as 100 to 150 mL of fluid WITHOUT any enlargement of the cardiac silhouette on chest X-ray!
  - Normal Pericardial Thickness in Constrictive Pericarditis: Up to 18% of patients with histologically proven constrictive pericarditis have completely NORMAL pericardial thickness on CT/MRI or pathology; normal thickness CANNOT rule out constrictive pericarditis!
  - Positive Pressure Ventilation Warning in Tamponade: Initiating positive-pressure mechanical ventilation in cardiac tamponade abolishes negative intrathoracic venous draw, drastically decreases cardiac preload, and can induce immediate cardiac arrest!
  - Colchicine Halves Recurrence Risk: Addition of colchicine to standard NSAID therapy for acute pericarditis reduces persistent symptoms at 72 hours and decreases recurrent pericarditis rates from ~37.5% to 16.7%!
