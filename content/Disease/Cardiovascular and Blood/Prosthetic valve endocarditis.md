# Prosthetic valve endocarditis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) is a potentially life-threatening infection involving a valve prosthesis (mechanical, bioprosthetic, or transcatheter valve) or an annuloplasty ring. It represents an endovascular infection where microorganisms colonize non-endothelialized foreign material, sewing cuffs, or damaged prosthetic cusps, forming protective biofilms, vegetation, and perivalvular destructive lesions.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Onset Timing: (1) Early-Onset PVE: Manifesting within 1 year of valve replacement or repair surgery (historically defined as within 60 days, but 1 year is now preferred because less virulent perioperatively acquired pathogens such as coagulase-negative staphylococci peak between 60 and 365 days). Caused predominantly by intraoperatively or perioperatively acquired microorganisms (S. aureus, CoNS, diphtheroids, fungi, nosocomial Gram-negative bacilli). (2) Late-Onset PVE: Manifesting more than 1 year after prosthetic valve placement. Usually acquired in the community or via health care-associated exposures, with microbiologic spectrum resembling native valve endocarditis (viridans group streptococci, enterococci, S. aureus, CoNS).
  - Valve Type & Structure: (1) Mechanical Valve PVE: Involves mechanical prostheses (caged-ball, single-tilting disk, or bileaflet-tilting disk valves made of metal or carbon alloys). Infection typically originates at the interface between the sewing cuff and native tissue, predisposing to perivalvular extension, ring abscesses, dehiscence, and paravalvular leaks. (2) Bioprosthetic Valve PVE: Involves tissue heterografts (porcine, bovine pericardial, or homograft/allograft). Infection involves both the sewing cuff and the biological leaflets/cusps, leading to cusp tears, perforation, or vegetation-induced valvular stenosis. (3) Transcatheter Valve PVE: Occurs following Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation (TAVI/TAVR) or transcatheter pulmonary/mitral valve-in-valve procedures. Features median onset at 5 months (3 to 9 months), high enterococcal and S. aureus frequency, and frequent perivalvular abscesses (47%).
  - Acquisition Setting: (1) Health Care-Associated PVE: Constitutes ~37% of PVE cases overall and >70% of early-onset PVE cases. Associated with intravascular catheters, hemodialysis, or recent healthcare exposures. Predominantly caused by S. aureus (frequently MRSA), CoNS, enterococci, multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacilli (Pseudomonas, Serratia, Acinetobacter, Stenotrophomonas), and Candida species. (2) Community-Acquired PVE: Predominantly late-onset (>1 year post-op). Caused by viridans group streptococci, enterococci, S. aureus, CoNS, and HACEK organisms.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Epidemiology: Incidence ranges from 1% to 6% of prosthetic valve implantations, or 0.3% to 0.6% per patient-year. PVE accounts for 16% to 33% of all definite cases of infective endocarditis (IE). Cumulative risk is highest within the initial 12 months after surgery, peaking in the first 2 months. In-hospital mortality remains high at 20% to 40% (up to 48.2% in S. aureus PVE).
  - Risk Factors: Male sex, previous native valve endocarditis (NVE), long cardiopulmonary bypass time during valve replacement surgery, presence of indwelling central venous catheters, hemodialysis, active nosocomial bacteremia or fungemia, residual aortic regurgitation after TAVI (doubles risk, hazard ratio 2.05), diabetes mellitus, renal failure, immunocompromise, poor oral hygiene or periodontal procedures, chronic skin disorders, IV drug use, and burn injury.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Staphylococcus aureus: The overall leading cause of PVE across both early and late periods (~25% to 45% of cases), with high rates of methicillin resistance (MRSA).
  - Coagulase-Negative Staphylococci (CoNS): Staphylococcus epidermidis and related species cause ~30% of cases, dominating early PVE (peak between 60 to 365 days) and health care-associated cases.
  - Enterococci: Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium account for 10% to 18% of PVE cases, serving as the third most common pathogen in late PVE and after TAVI procedures.
  - Viridans Group Streptococci: Streptococcus sanguinis, S. mutans, S. gordonii, S. parasanguis, S. mitis, S. oralis, and Streptococcus gallolyticus (formerly S. bovis) account for late community-acquired cases.
  - Aerobic Gram-Negative Bacilli: Account for 10% to 15% of early or health care-associated cases: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Serratia marcescens, Acinetobacter species, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus, and Enterobacter species.
  - HACEK Group: Haemophilus parainfluenzae, Aggregatibacter aphrophilus, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Cardiobacterium hominis, Eikenella corrodens, and Kingella species (cause late community-acquired PVE with large vegetations).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Pediatric & Young Adults: N/A
  - Elderly (>65 years) & TAVI Patients: Enterococci (E. faecalis) and Staphylococcus aureus predominate in TAVI-PVE, followed by CoNS, viridans group streptococci, Gram-negative bacilli, and Candida species.
  - Specific Epidemiological Subgroups (From AHA Table 81.5):
    - IV Drug Use: S. aureus, CoNS, Beta-hemolytic streptococci, P. aeruginosa, fungi.
    - Genitourinary Disorders or Manipulation: Enterococcus species, Group B streptococci, Gram-negative bacilli, Neisseria gonorrhoeae.
    - Dental Health or Procedures: Viridans group streptococci, Abiotrophia defectiva, Granulicatella species, Gemella species, HACEK organisms.
    - Solid Organ Transplantation: S. aureus, Aspergillus fumigatus, Enterococcus species, Candida species.
    - Farm or Animal Exposure: Brucella species, Coxiella burnetii, Erysipelothrix species, Bartonella species, Pasteurella, Capnocytophaga.
    - Homelessness or Body Lice: Bartonella species.
    - Gastrointestinal Lesions: Streptococcus gallolyticus (S. bovis), Enterococcus species, Clostridium septicum.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Fungi: Candida species (Candida albicans, Candida parapsilosis [most frequent fungal causes], Candida glabrata, Candida krusei) cause early, healthcare-associated, or post-candidemic PVE. Molds: Aspergillus fumigatus, Aspergillus flavus, Scopulariopsis species, Cryptococcus neoformans, Histoplasma capsulatum. Characterized by large, dense, bulky vegetations, high embolic rates, and mandatory surgical indication.
  - Mycobacteria: Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (NTM) including Mycobacterium chimaera (global prolonged outbreak linked to contaminated Sorin Stockert 3T heater-cooler units during open-chest cardiac surgery, with median presentation 21 months post-op), Mycobacterium fortuitum, Mycobacterium chelonae, and Mycobacterium wolinskyi.
  - Fastidious / Culture-Negative / Zoonotic Pathogens: Coxiella burnetii (Q fever PVE; anti-phase I IgG >=1:800), Bartonella species (B. henselae, B. quintana), Brucella species (B. melitensis), Tropheryma whipplei, Legionella species, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia species, Diphtheroids / Corynebacterium species, Erysipelothrix, and Enteroviruses.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Lack of Endothelialization & Host Matrix Coating: During the early post-implantation period, the prosthetic valve structure, sewing cuff, annular tissue, and sutures lack an endothelial lining. Host extracellular matrix proteins (fibrinogen, fibrin, fibronectin, collagen, elastin, sialoglycoproteins) rapidly coat the foreign material, acting as target ligands for bacterial adhesins.
  - Microbial Adhesin Binding: Pathogens express specialized surface MSCRAMMs (microbial surface components recognizing adhesive matrix molecules) that bind to host matrix molecules. S. aureus uses clumping factor A/B (ClfA, ClfB) and fibronectin-binding proteins A/B (FnBPA, FnBPB); streptococci use glucans, Fim A, Ace (collagen adhesin), and Hsa (sialic acid-binding protein); enterococci use biofilm-associated proteins (Esp, Ebp pili), Acm, and Scm.
  - Biofilm Matrix & Persister Phenotype Formation: Adherent microorganisms form dense sessile colonies encased in a protective extracellular polymeric matrix (biofilm). The biofilm acts as a physical diffusion barrier against host antibodies, phagocytes, and antibiotics. Nutrient-deprived bacteria inside the biofilm enter a slow-growing, metabolically quiescent "persister" state with altered gene expression and high phenotypic tolerance to cell wall-active antimicrobials (beta-lactams and glycopeptides).
  - Platelet & Monocyte Activation: Bacteria trigger tissue factor release from monocytes and endothelial cells via FnBPA, activating the extrinsic coagulation cascade. Microorganisms directly activate platelets via ClfA/ClfB (S. aureus), SrpA (S. sanguinis), or GspB/Hsa (S. gordonii), driving continuous platelet-fibrin deposition and vegetation enlargement. S. aureus can also internalize into intact endothelial cells via alpha-5-beta-1 integrins, causing persistent or recurrent bacteremia.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Mechanical vs. Bioprosthetic Valve Invasion Patterns: In mechanical valves (composed of non-adherent metal or carbon alloys), infection originates at the sewing cuff-tissue interface, causing suture breakdown, ring/paravalvular abscesses, purulent erosion into the intraventricular septum, AV conduction disruption (complete heart block), aortocavitary fistulas, sinus of Valsalva aneurysms, valve dehiscence, "dancing" dislodged prostheses, and paravalvular regurgitant leaks. In bioprosthetic valves, infection involves both the sewing ring and the biological leaflets or cusps, causing cusp destruction, perforation, tearing, rapid tissue degeneration, or large obstructive vegetations.
  - Secretion of Exotoxins & Degradative Enzymes: Virulent pathogens like S. aureus secrete tissue-degrading enzymes and exotoxins coordinated by global regulators (Agr, SigB, SarA), driving rapid tissue necrosis, invasion, and liquefactive paravalvular destruction.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Fever: The most common presenting symptom, present in 73% to 92% of PVE cases. May be low-grade, remittent, or accompanied by rigors. Note: Fever is frequently absent in elderly patients, those with end-stage renal disease, or patients already receiving antibiotics or antipyretics.
  - Heart Failure Symptoms: New onset or worsening dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, fatigue, and peripheral edema due to acute prosthetic valve dysfunction (regurgitation or stenosis).
  - Systemic & Embolic Symptoms: Acute focal neurological deficits (hemiparesis, aphasia from embolic stroke), severe headache (mycotic aneurysm rupture or brain abscess), sudden flank pain or hematuria (renal infarction), left upper quadrant pain (splenic infarction), or pleuritic chest pain or hemoptysis (septic pulmonary emboli in right-sided PVE).
  - Nonspecific Constitutional Symptoms: Anorexia, weight loss, night sweats, malaise, myalgias, and arthralgias (especially in subacute or late-onset PVE).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Cardiac Signs: New or changing heart murmur (present in a majority of patients), new regurgitant paravalvular murmur, muffled or absent prosthetic valve closure sounds, apical gallop (S3/S4), and signs of congestive heart failure (pulmonary rales, elevated jugular venous pressure, hepatojugular reflux, peripheral edema).
  - Conduction & Rhythm Signs: New bradycardia, PR interval prolongation, or new complete heart block on ECG/telemetry, indicating perivalvular abscess invasion into the AV node or intraventricular septum.
  - Peripheral Embolic & Immunological Signs: Embolic stroke, Janeway lesions (painless erythematous macules on palms/soles), Osler nodes (painful nodules on digits), splinter hemorrhages, conjunctival petechiae, Roth spots (retinal hemorrhages), splenomegaly, and secondary metastatic musculoskeletal abscesses (psoas abscess, vertebral osteomyelitis, diskitis). Classic Oslerian immunologic signs are often ABSENT in acute PVE.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Echocardiographic "Dancing" Prosthesis: Flail motion or hypermobility of a dislodged mechanical or bioprosthetic valve ring due to severe paravalvular dehiscence and ring abscess.
  - New Paravalvular Regurgitation Regurgitant Jet: Demonstration of a new paravalvular leak or leak-induced murmur around a prosthetic valve sewing ring.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - CBC: Normocytic normochromic anemia; leukocytosis with a left shift in acute cases (may be blunted in elderly or immunocompromised patients).
  - Inflammatory Markers: Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) and C-reactive Protein (CRP) are markedly elevated and serve as baselines to monitor therapeutic response.
  - Urinalysis & Basic Panel: Microscopic hematuria, proteinuria, or red blood cell casts (immune-complex glomerulonephritis or embolic renal infarction); elevated BUN/creatinine.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE): The diagnostic imaging modality of choice and standard of care for PVE. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is severely limited (sensitivity only 17% to 36%) due to acoustic shadowing, reverberations, and artifacts from mechanical or bioprosthetic valve hardware. TEE has a high sensitivity of 86% to 92% for detecting vegetations and is vastly superior for diagnosing perivalvular complications: ring or paravalvular abscesses, pseudoaneurysms (pulsatile echofree sacs communicating with a cardiac chamber), fistulas, valve dehiscence, leaflet perforations, and intracardiac shunts. Repeat TEE in 7 days if initial TEE is negative but clinical suspicion remains high.
  - Positron Emission Tomography / Computed Tomography (18F-FDG-PET/CT or PET/CTA): Positron emission tomography with 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose combined with CT angiography. Detects focal or heterogeneous glucose uptake by activated inflammatory cells (neutrophils, macrophages) around the prosthetic valve ring. Inclusion of increased perivalvular 18F-FDG uptake as a major Duke criterion increases sensitivity from 70% to 97%. Also maps subclinical extracardiac septic emboli (vertebral osteomyelitis, psoas/splenic abscesses). Note: False positives can occur in the early postoperative period (<3 months post-op) due to surgical inflammation or BioGlue; ESC guidelines recommend deferring PET/CT for up to 3 months post-cardiac surgery.
  - Radiolabeled Leukocyte Scintigraphy (SPECT/CT): Indium-111, Gallium-67, or Technetium-99m HMPAO-labeled white blood cell SPECT/CT. Highly specific for localizing perivalvular infection and abscesses when echocardiography is inconclusive.
  - Multislice Cardiac Computed Tomography (MSCT / CTA): Delineates paravalvular anatomy, aortic root abscesses, pseudoaneurysms, and coronary artery anatomy prior to reoperation.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Blood Cultures (The Diagnostic Foundation): Obtain at least 3 sets of blood cultures (drawn from separate venipuncture sites at any time, as bacteremia in endocarditis is continuous and low-grade) before starting antimicrobial therapy in stable patients. Positive in 78% to 91% of PVE cases. Culture-negative PVE (9% to 13%) is most commonly caused by prior antibiotic administration or fastidious/noncultivable organisms.
  - Serology: Anti-phase I IgG antibody titer >=1:800 by microimmunofluorescence for Coxiella burnetii (major Duke criterion); serologies for Brucella melitensis, Bartonella species, Legionella, Mycoplasma, Chlamydia, and Aspergillus galactomannan antigen.
  - Resected Valve Tissue Cultures, Histopathology & Stains: Gram stain, fungal stains (GMS, PAS), Ziehl-Neelsen / Fite stain, and cultures of resected bioprostheses, mechanical sewing cuffs, perivalvular abscess tissue, or embolic fragments. Histopathology showing active endocarditis with polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMLs) confirms diagnosis.
  - Molecular Diagnostics (16S rRNA & Panfungal PCR): Broad-range 16S rRNA gene PCR and sequencing on resected valve tissue, vegetations, or embolic material identifies bacterial DNA in culture-negative cases. Panfungal ITS rRNA PCR identifies fungal DNA. (Note: Sensitivity of 16S PCR on valve tissue is lower in PVE than NVE; PCR can detect nonviable DNA weeks after cure).
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Histopathologic demonstration of active endocarditis (polymorphonuclear leukocyte infiltration and tissue destruction) or positive culture or PCR from resected prosthetic valve hardware, perivalvular tissue, or embolic fragments obtained during surgery or autopsy remains the definitive gold standard diagnostic test.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Hospitalization & Hemodynamic Monitoring: Immediate ICU or monitored bed admission for all suspected PVE cases. Medical stabilization of acute heart failure, cardiogenic shock, or pulmonary edema.
  - Anticoagulation Management in PVE:
    - Mechanical Valves: Chronic anticoagulation (warfarin) SHOULD BE CONTINUED with close clinical monitoring because the aggregate risk of thromboembolism without anticoagulation is drastically higher (42% vs 11%) without increasing cerebral hemorrhage risk. EXCEPTION: If the patient develops a CNS embolic stroke or intracranial hemorrhage, ALL anticoagulation must be immediately stopped or reversed. Once stable and without extension of hemorrhage, anticoagulation may be cautiously resumed with IV unfractionated heparin.
    - Bioprosthetic Valves: Anticoagulation is NOT indicated for bioprosthetic PVE unless there are independent medical indications (e.g., atrial fibrillation).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Early-Onset PVE (<=1 year post-op) or Health Care-Associated PVE: Triple-drug combination regimen: Vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV q12h (adjusting trough to 10-20 mcg/mL) PLUS Rifampin 300 mg PO/IV q8h PLUS Gentamicin 3 mg/kg IV/IM daily (in 2 or 3 divided doses, e.g., 1 mg/kg IV q8h) PLUS Cefepime 2 g IV q8h (or Meropenem 1 g IV q8h) for broad Gram-negative coverage. Total duration: minimum 6 weeks (Gentamicin given for initial 2 weeks).
  - Late-Onset PVE (>1 year post-op, Community-Acquired): Vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV q12h PLUS Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h (or Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h) PLUS Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV q8h (for initial 2 weeks). Total duration: minimum 6 weeks.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Pathogen-Directed Antimicrobial Regimens (Minimum 6 Weeks Total Duration):
    - Methicillin-Susceptible Staphylococci (MSSA): Nafcillin or Oxacillin 2 g IV q4h for >=6 weeks PLUS Rifampin 300 mg PO/IV q8h for >=6 weeks PLUS Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV q8h (or 3 mg/kg daily divided) for initial 2 weeks. (Cefazolin 2 g IV q8h can be substituted in non-immediate penicillin allergy).
    - Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococci (MRSA / MR-CoNS): Vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV q12h (trough 10-20 mcg/mL) for >=6 weeks PLUS Rifampin 300 mg PO/IV q8h for >=6 weeks PLUS Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV q8h for initial 2 weeks. (Note on Rifampin timing: Initiate rifampin ONLY AFTER 2 days of effective two-drug antistaphylococcal therapy to lower bacterial burden and prevent selection of rifampin-resistant mutant strains!).
    - Penicillin-Susceptible Streptococci (MIC <=0.12 mcg/mL): Penicillin G 18-24 million units/day IV or Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h for >=6 weeks (optional Gentamicin 3 mg/kg daily for initial 2 weeks).
    - Relatively/Highly Penicillin-Resistant Streptococci (MIC >0.12 mcg/mL), Abiotrophia, or Granulicatella: Penicillin G or Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h for 6 weeks PLUS Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV q8h for 6 weeks.
    - Enterococcus Species (Susceptible to Penicillin, Gentamicin, Vancomycin): Ampicillin 2 g IV q4h (or Penicillin G 18-30 million units/day) for 6 weeks PLUS Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV q8h (in 3 divided doses) for 6 weeks. OR Dual Beta-Lactam Regimen: Ampicillin 2 g IV q4h PLUS Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h for 6 weeks (preferred in patients with renal impairment, renal clearance <50 mL/min). If beta-lactamase producing: Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h x 6 weeks. If VRE or multidrug-resistant: Linezolid, Daptomycin, or Daptomycin + Ampicillin salvage.
    - HACEK Organisms: Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h or Ampicillin 2 g IV q4h for 6 weeks (Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV / 500 mg PO q12h if penicillin-allergic).
    - Fungal PVE (Candida / Aspergillus): Lipid formulation of Amphotericin B or Echinocandin (Caspofungin/Micafungin/Anidulafungin) PLUS early surgical valve replacement, followed by lifelong oral suppressive therapy (e.g., Fluconazole).
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Indications for Early Surgical Intervention in PVE (Table 81.6): Surgical intervention is required in ~42% to 49% of PVE patients. Indications include:
    1. Severe heart failure or mild-moderate heart failure unresponsive to medical therapy (due to valve dehiscence, regurgitation, cusp perforation, or obstruction).
    2. Perivalvular extension of infection leading to myocardial/ring abscess, pseudoaneurysm, intracardiac fistula, or shunt.
    3. New severe conduction defects (complete heart block, new bundle branch block) suggesting perivalvular invasion into the conduction system.
    4. Uncontrolled infection: Persistent fever and positive blood cultures for >5 to 7 days despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy.
    5. PVE caused by fungi (Candida, Aspergillus), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, or other multidrug-resistant organisms.
    6. Multiple (>1) systemic embolic events despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy.
    7. Persistent large vegetation (>1 cm) after an embolic event.
  - Surgical Technique: Radical debridement of all infected valvular, perivalvular, and necrotic tissue back to bleeding viable margins. Reconstruction of aortic root/LVOT using autologous or bovine pericardial patches or cryopreserved aortic allografts (homografts, which lack prosthetic sewing rings and are more resistant to reinfection) or stentless bioprostheses (Toronto, Freestyle).
  - Timing of Surgery: Urgent surgery should be performed promptly without delay; delaying surgery for additional days of antibiotics in the setting of severe heart failure or dehiscence increases mortality and does not reduce postoperative reinfection rates!
  - Postoperative Antimicrobial Duration: If surgical valve or perivalvular culture is POSITIVE, give a full 6-week course of antimicrobials postoperatively (counting day 1 from surgery). If surgical cultures are NEGATIVE, the preoperative antibiotic course may be counted toward the total 6-week duration.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Severe acute congestive heart failure (#1 indication for surgery) due to prosthetic valve dehiscence, paravalvular leak, cusp perforation, or obstructive vegetation.
  - Perivalvular extension: Paravalvular ring abscesses, aortocavitary fistulas, sinus of Valsalva aneurysms, pseudoaneurysms, and intracardiac shunts.
  - Conduction system disruption: Complete third-degree heart block or new bundle branch blocks.
  - Cerebrovascular & Systemic Emboli: Symptomatic (35%) and silent (30%) embolic strokes, intracranial mycotic aneurysm rupture / cerebral hemorrhage, brain abscess, and metastatic abscesses (psoas abscess, vertebral osteomyelitis, diskitis, renal/splenic infarction).
  - High in-hospital mortality (20% to 40% overall; 48.2% in S. aureus PVE).
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Recurrent PVE (relapse or reinfection) in 6% to 15% of surgical survivors.
  - Sterile prosthetic valve dysfunction or late structural bioprosthetic degeneration requiring reoperation (18% to 26%).
  - Chronic heart failure and persistent neurological deficits following embolic stroke.
  - Long-term 10-year survival ranges from 41% to 73%.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Dancing" Prosthesis: Echocardiographic flail motion of a dislodged prosthetic valve disanchored by a paravalvular ring abscess.
  - Silzone Valve: Prosthetic valve with a silver-impregnated sewing cuff introduced in 1998 to prevent PVE, withdrawn after causing high rates of paravalvular leakage, tissue necrosis, and embolic events due to silver inhibition of fibroblast growth/healing.
  - Sorin Stockert 3T Heater-Cooler Unit Outbreak: Global prolonged outbreak of Mycobacterium chimaera PVE traced to contaminated water aerosolization during open-chest surgery.
  - Dual Beta-Lactam Synergy (Ampicillin + Ceftriaxone): Regimen for E. faecalis PVE that saturates PBP-2 and PBP-3, providing bactericidal activity equivalent to ampicillin + gentamicin with zero nephrotoxicity.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Continuation of Anticoagulation in Mechanical PVE: Unlike native valve endocarditis (where anticoagulation is generally held), chronic oral anticoagulation SHOULD BE CONTINUED in mechanical PVE because stopping anticoagulation drastically increases thromboembolic risk (42% vs 11%) without reducing bleeding, UNLESS a CNS embolic stroke or intracranial hemorrhage occurs!
  - Rifampin Delay Rule: In staphylococcal PVE, rifampin MUST NOT be started immediately; it should be delayed for at least 2 days until effective two-drug antistaphylococcal therapy reduces bacterial load to prevent rapid emergence of rifampin resistance!
  - Multiple Daily Dosing for Enterococcal Gentamicin: Unlike streptococcal endocarditis (where once-daily gentamicin is acceptable), enterococcal PVE REQUIRES multiple-daily divided doses of gentamicin (q8h) to achieve bactericidal synergy!
  - PET/CT False-Positive Postoperative Window: 18F-FDG-PET/CT should be deferred for up to 3 months following cardiac surgery due to non-infectious inflammatory FDG uptake from surgical healing and BioGlue causing false-positive results!
  - Surgery Should NOT Be Delayed for Antibiotics: In PVE complicated by heart failure or dehiscence, delaying surgery to give "a few more days of antibiotics" increases operative mortality and does NOT reduce postoperative reinfection rates!
