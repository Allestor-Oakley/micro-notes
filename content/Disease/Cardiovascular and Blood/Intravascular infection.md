# Intravascular infection

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Intravascular infection encompasses a heterogeneous group of life-threatening endovascular infections involving non-valvular cardiac and vascular structures, indwelling vascular catheters, native or synthetic blood vessels, and implanted intravascular hardware. Core entities include catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSI), suppurative (septic) thrombophlebitis of superficial or deep central/pelvic veins, infected (mycotic) arterial aneurysms and pseudoaneurysms, infective endarteritis, prosthetic vascular graft infections (PVGI), percutaneous vascular closure device (PVCD) infections, and endovascular stent infections. These infections are characterized by microbial colonization of damaged vascular endothelium or foreign material, intraluminal or perivascular thrombus/biofilm formation, continuous or persistent bacteremia/fungemia, and local vascular wall destruction.
- **Major Classifications:**
  - Catheter-Associated & Intravascular Device Infections: Categorized into local catheter infections (exit-site infection, tunnel infection, pocket infection) versus systemic catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSI).
  - Suppurative Thrombophlebitis: Anatomically classified as (1) Superficial Suppurative Thrombophlebitis (most common in cannulated peripheral veins of upper or lower extremities and burn patients); (2) Great Thoracic Central Vein Suppurative Thrombophlebitis (superior vena cava, subclavian, jugular, or innominate veins); (3) Pelvic Suppurative Thrombophlebitis / Septic Pelvic Thrombophlebitis (involving uterine, ovarian, or iliac veins and inferior vena cava, classically post-partum, post-abortion, or post-gynecologic surgery); (4) Lemierre Syndrome (septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein following pharyngeal infection, typically caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum); and (5) Septic Portal Vein Thrombosis (pylephlebitis, complicating intra-abdominal/diverticular infections or appendicitis).
  - Arterial Infections & Mycotic Aneurysms: Classified by pathogenesis: (1) Embolomycotic aneurysms (septic microemboli lodging in vasa vasorum, often during infective endocarditis); (2) Infection of pre-existing atherosclerotic aneurysms or altered arterial intima; (3) Contiguous extension from adjacent purulent foci (e.g., vertebral osteomyelitis, tuberculous lymph nodes); and (4) Traumatic or iatrogenic pseudoaneurysms (from direct needle contamination in intravenous drug users, vascular surgery, cardiac catheterization, percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty, or percutaneous vascular closure devices).
  - Vascular Graft & Stent Infections: Categorized by anatomic site (central/intracavitary abdominal or thoracic grafts vs. peripheral/extracavitary groin or extremity grafts) and timing of onset (early-onset within 2 to 3 months of surgery from perioperative contamination vs. late-onset after 2 to 3 months from hematogenous seeding or indolent low-virulence organisms).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  - Intravascular Catheter Infections & Suppurative Thrombophlebitis: More than half of hospitalized patients undergo vascular catheterization. Risk is 40 times higher with plastic cannulas (8% risk) than steel needles (0.2%). Key risk factors include prolonged catheter duration (>48 to 72 hours for peripheral lines, >5 days for cutdowns), lower-extremity cannulation, emergency placement, plastic catheter material, frequent catheter manipulations, burn injury (4% to 8% incidence in burn centers), total parenteral nutrition, intensive care unit stay, malignancy, and systemic corticosteroids.
  - Pelvic Suppurative Thrombophlebitis: Associated with parturition (1 in 2000 deliveries), septic abortion (1 in 200), and major gynecologic surgery (1 in 800), particularly in females aged 15 to 40 years.
  - Mycotic Aneurysms & Endarteritis: Atherosclerosis accounts for >74% of secondary arterial aneurysm infections. Major risk factors include non-Typhi Salmonella bacteremia in adults >50 years of age (25% have an endovascular focus), active infective endocarditis, illicit intravenous drug use (needle trauma causing pseudoaneurysms in femoral, brachial, or carotid arteries), percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty, intra-arterial chemotherapy ports, and femoral artery catheterization.
  - Prosthetic Vascular Graft & Stent Infections: Incidence ranges from <1% for abdominal aortic grafts to 1.5% to 2% for aortofemoral grafts and up to 6% for infrainguinal groin grafts. Risk factors include groin incisions, emergency surgery, postoperative surgical site wound infection or hematoma, perioperative bacteremia, redo bypass surgery, diabetes mellitus, female gender, and hemodialysis PTFE graft cannulation. Overall 5-year survival for aortic graft infections is only ~50%.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Staphylococcus aureus: The leading causative organism across all intravascular infections (accounting for 65% to 78% of superficial suppurative thrombophlebitis, 30% of vascular graft infections, 40% of infected native arterial aneurysms, 75% of vascular closure device infections, and 76% of illicit drug-related pseudoaneurysms). Includes MSSA and MRSA.
  - Coagulase-Negative Staphylococci (CoNS): Staphylococcus epidermidis and related species account for 33% of prosthetic vascular graft infections, major proportion of catheter-related bacteremias, and late indolent endovascular device infections.
  - Aerobic Gram-Negative Bacilli: Enterobacteriaceae (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella-Enterobacter species, Proteus species, Serratia marcescens, Providencia) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa cause a significant proportion of nosocomial catheter infections, suppurative thrombophlebitis in patients receiving broad-spectrum antibiotics, and 35% of infected atherosclerotic aneurysms.
  - Salmonella Species: Non-Typhi Salmonella species (S. enteritidis, S. enterica serotype Choleraesuis, S. typhimurium) have a unique tropism for damaged arterial endothelium, causing up to 20% of infected atherosclerotic aortic and iliac aneurysms in patients >50 years of age.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Neonates & Infants: Staphylococcus aureus, CoNS, Group B Streptococcus, Escherichia coli, Candida species, Malassezia furfur (in premature infants receiving parenteral lipid emulsions via deep central venous catheters), and umbilical artery catheter-associated aortic mycotic aneurysms (primarily S. aureus).
  - Young Adults / PWID / Obstetric Patients: In PWID, S. aureus (76%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (18%, especially with Ts and blues abuse), polymicrobial flora, and oral anaerobes. In septic pelvic thrombophlebitis, polymicrobial anaerobic and aerobic vaginal flora: Bacteroides species (B. fragilis), microaerophilic and anaerobic streptococci, E. coli, and Beta-hemolytic streptococci. In Lemierre syndrome, Fusobacterium necrophorum.
  - Elderly (>50 to 65 years): Salmonella species (S. Choleraesuis, S. enteritidis), Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococcus faecalis, Listeria monocytogenes, and Clostridium species (e.g., Clostridium septicum associated with colonic malignancy).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Anaerobic Bacteria: Bacteroides fragilis, Fusobacterium necrophorum (Lemierre syndrome internal jugular vein thrombophlebitis), Peptostreptococcus, Clostridium septicum (associated with aortitis and occult cecal/colonic cancer), Clostridium perfringens, and Cutibacterium acnes (prosthetic graft infections).
  - Fastidious & Zoonotic Bacteria: Coxiella burnetii (vascular graft and aortic aneurysm infections), Brucella abortus / B. melitensis, Bartonella species, Campylobacter fetus, Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae, Listeria monocytogenes, Arizona hinshawii (in elderly diabetic men with aortic aneurysms), and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (tuberculous aortic aneurysms from contiguous lymph nodes or direct seeding).
  - Fungi: Candida species (Candida albicans, Candida tropicalis, Candida parapsilosis, Candida glabrata) cause suppurative thrombophlebitis of central thoracic veins, IVC filter infections, and fungal graft endarteritis; Aspergillus species (A. fumigatus, A. flavus) and Mucorales (Rhizopus) cause fungal mycotic aneurysms and aortic bypass graft endarteritis; Histoplasma capsulatum, Coccidioides immitis, and Bipolaris.
  - Mycobacteria & Other Organisms: Nontuberculous mycobacteria (Mycobacterium chimaera, M. fortuitum, M. chelonae) and Prototheca.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Routes of Intravascular Entry:
    1. Direct Extraluminal Migration: Microorganisms from skin flora migrate along the external catheter surface between the catheter and perivascular tissue (the primary route for short-term intravascular catheters).
    2. Intraluminal Contamination: Contamination of the catheter hub, connecting tubing, or intravenous fluid infusions.
    3. Direct Mechanical Injury & Inoculation: Arterial trauma from percutaneous procedures (angioplasty, stenting, closure devices), illicit IV drug injections, or open vascular surgery directly inoculates bacteria into extravasated hematomas, forming infected pseudoaneurysms.
    4. Hematogenous Seeding: Circulating bacteremia from a distant focus seeds a sterile intraluminal thrombus, pre-existing atherosclerotic plaque, or prosthetic graft.
    5. Contiguous Extension: Direct erosion into vascular walls from adjacent purulent collections (e.g., vertebral osteomyelitis, tuberculous lymph nodes, or deep wound abscesses).
  - Pathogenesis of Suppurative Thrombophlebitis: Endothelial damage from mechanical catheter irritation or hyperosmolar fluids causes intravenous thrombus formation. Bacteria entrapment within the thrombus leads to intraluminal suppuration, vein wall necrosis, and persistent bacteremia. In pelvic septic thrombophlebitis, uterine/ovarian vein stasis during pregnancy or parturition combined with uterine infection leads to septic thrombosis extending to the inferior vena cava.
  - Pathogenesis of Mycotic Aneurysms & Graft Biofilms: Bacterial MSCRAMMs adhere to damaged arterial intima, exposed Dacron/PTFE fibers, or thrombi. Bacterial exotoxins and local neutrophilic enzymes degrade the elastica and media layers of the arterial wall, causing rapid wall thinning, saccular dilation, pseudoaneurysm formation, or fatal rupture. Adherent bacteria synthesize an extracellular polymeric slime layer (biofilm), entering a sessile phenotype resistant to host defenses and antimicrobials.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Vascular Destruction & Suppuration: Acute polymorphonuclear leukocyte infiltration causes intramural abscesses, vessel wall necrosis, destruction of internal elastic lamina, and gross intraluminal purulence (pus within the vein or artery).
  - Embolic & Hemorrhagic Sequelae: Fragmenting septic thrombi break off continuously, causing septic pulmonary emboli (in venous thrombophlebitis) or peripheral ischemic emboli/septic infarction (in arterial endarteritis/mycotic aneurysms). Progressive arterial destruction leads to catastrophic hemorrhage, aortoenteric fistulas (eroding into third/fourth duodenal segments), or aortobronchial fistulas.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Catheter-Related BSI & Suppurative Thrombophlebitis: High fever, rigors, chills, profuse diaphoresis, and systemic sepsis persisting despite catheter removal and appropriate antimicrobial therapy. In burn patients, a sudden unexplained dramatic increase in daily insulin requirement or late-onset sepsis/pneumonia may be the sole heralding symptom.
  - Pelvic Suppurative Thrombophlebitis: Spiking fevers, rigors, severe lower abdominal or flank pain, and chills occurring 3 to 21 days following delivery, abortion, or gynecologic surgery, characteristically unresponsive to broad-spectrum antibiotics alone until heparin is added.
  - Lemierre Syndrome: Severe sore throat / pharyngitis followed 1 to 2 weeks later by spiking fevers, rigors, neck pain, swelling along the sternocleidomastoid muscle, and pleuritic chest pain with hemoptysis (from septic pulmonary emboli).
  - Mycotic Aneurysms & Vascular Graft Infections: Localized pulsatile pain over the involved artery or graft site, unremitting back/abdominal pain (aortic aneurysms), fever, chills, night sweats, or sudden gastrointestinal bleeding / hematemesis (herald bleed of an aortoenteric fistula).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Superficial Suppurative Thrombophlebitis: Localized erythema, warmth, severe tenderness, induration, and a palpable cord along a previously cannulated vein. Note: Local inflammatory findings are absent in >65% of burn patients and lower-extremity catheter infections, where systemic sepsis is the only presentation! Expression of gross pus from the vein stump or cutdown site is diagnostic.
  - Pelvic & Deep Central Vein Thrombophlebitis: Lower abdominal or adnexal tenderness; vague deep pelvic mass; swelling and edema of the face, neck, and upper extremity (superior vena cava syndrome in thoracic central vein thrombosis); or limb swelling in femoral vein septic thrombosis.
  - Mycotic Aneurysms & Peripheral Pseudoaneurysms: Tender, diffusely indurated, pulsatile mass with an associated bruit (in 50% to 60% of cases); distal arterial insufficiency (coolness, pallor, diminished pulses); skin petechiae, purpura, or localized gangrene; and localized femoral/groin sinus tract or hematoma after vascular procedures.
  - External Iliac Artery Aneurysm Triad: (1) Lower extremity / anterior thigh pain with quadriceps wasting and depressed knee jerk; (2) Distal arterial insufficiency with leg coolness and pallor; and (3) Persistent bacteremia.
  - Hepatic Artery Aneurysm Triad: Colicky right upper quadrant abdominal pain, jaundice, and gastrointestinal hemorrhage or hemobilia.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Gross Intraluminal Pus: Expression of frank purulent exudate from a vein lumen or transected vascular stump during exploratory venotomy.
  - External Iliac Aneurysm Clinical Triad: Anterior thigh pain/quadriceps wasting + leg ischemia/pallor + persistent bacteremia.
  - Herald Bleed of Aortoenteric Fistula: Self-limited initial gastrointestinal hemorrhage preceding massive catastrophic arterial rupture in an aortic graft or stent infection.
  - Palpable Tender Cord with Pus: Purulent cord along a cannulated vein track.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - CBC: Marked leukocytosis with a prominent neutrophilic left shift (toxic granulation, bandemia) in acute septic thrombophlebitis and infected aneurysms; normocytic anemia in chronic graft infections.
  - Inflammatory Markers: Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) and C-reactive Protein (CRP) are markedly elevated and serve as objective baselines to monitor response to therapy.
  - Urinalysis & Basic Metabolic Panel: Microscopic hematuria or proteinuria; elevated blood urea nitrogen and creatinine reflecting acute kidney injury secondary to sepsis or contrast administration.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Ultrasonography & Duplex Doppler: Modality of choice for peripheral veins and extremity arteries. Detects superficial and deep vein thrombosis, loss of venous compressibility, absence of flow, perivascular fluid collections, and arterial pseudoaneurysms.
  - Computed Tomography (CT) with Contrast / CT Angiography (CTA): Imaging modality of choice for central thoracic/abdominal veins, portal vein, aortic/visceral mycotic aneurysms, and vascular graft infections. Key findings include intraluminal thrombus, perivascular fluid or soft tissue attenuation, perigraft gas (diagnostic of infection >6 to 8 weeks post-op), pseudoaneurysm formation, wall thickening, and obliteration of tissue planes.
  - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) & MR Angiography (MRA): High soft-tissue resolution; superior to CT in differentiating subacute hematoma from active perigraft inflammation, particularly when CT is inconclusive or iodinated contrast is contraindicated. High positive predictive value (95%).
  - Nuclear Medicine Imaging: 18F-FDG PET/CT, Indium-111 labeled WBC scintigraphy, and Technetium-99m labeled WBC SPECT-CT. 18F-FDG PET/CT is highly sensitive for localized endarteritis, infected vascular grafts, and hidden central suppurative thrombophlebitis. Software-based fusion imaging (CTA combined with WBC scintigraphy) yields 94% sensitivity for graft infections.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Blood Cultures (The Cornerstone): At least two to three sets of blood cultures obtained from separate venipunctures prior to antimicrobial therapy. Persistent bacteremia or fungemia (positive in 80% to 90% of suppurative thrombophlebitis cases and >85% of vascular closure device infections) is a hallmark of intravascular infection.
  - Semiquantitative & Quantitative Catheter Cultures:
    - Roll-Plate Semiquantitative Technique (Maki Roll-Plate Method): A 5.7-cm catheter segment is rolled across a 5% sheep blood agar plate. Isolation of >=15 colony-forming units (CFUs) correlates strongly with catheter-related infection. Confluent growth (>1000 colonies) indicates suppurative thrombophlebitis.
    - Differential Time to Positivity (DTP): Blood cultures drawn simultaneously from central venous catheter (CVC) and peripheral vein; growth in CVC culture >=2 hours earlier than peripheral culture confirms CRBSI.
  - Direct Pus, Tissue & Graft Cultures: Gram stain and aerobic/anaerobic/fungal/mycobacterial cultures of expressible pus, CT-guided perivascular fluid aspirates, explanted vascular grafts, or resected arterial wall specimens.
  - Molecular Diagnostics / PCR: Broad-range 16S rRNA gene PCR and sequencing on tissue or sonication fluid from explanted grafts/stents to detect fastidious or culture-negative pathogens.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:** Surgical exploration demonstrating gross intraluminal pus or histopathologic demonstration of microbial invasion and acute vascular wall inflammation combined with positive culture from resected vascular tissue or graft material remains the definitive gold standard.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Hemodynamic Resuscitation: Immediate IV fluid resuscitation, blood transfusion, and vasopressor support for patients presenting with severe sepsis, septic shock, or acute hemorrhagic shock from ruptured mycotic aneurysms.
  - Catheter Removal & Vascular Access Management: Prompt removal of all indwelling intravenous lines, central catheters, or arterial sheaths implicated in infection. Immediate discontinuation of hyperalimentation or lipid infusions.
  - Anticoagulation: Full-dose systemic anticoagulation with intravenous unfractionated heparin in pelvic suppurative thrombophlebitis and deep central vein septic thrombosis to halt thrombus propagation and septic embolization.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Empiric Catheter-Related Sepsis & Suppurative Thrombophlebitis: Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h (targeting serum trough 15-20 mcg/mL or AUC/MIC 400-600) PLUS an antipseudomonal beta-lactam (Cefepime 2 g IV q8h OR Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h OR Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h).
  - Empiric Pelvic Suppurative Thrombophlebitis: Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h (or Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h) PLUS IV Heparin drip.
  - Empiric Infected Arterial Aneurysm / Vascular Graft / Closure Device Infection: Vancomycin 15 to 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h PLUS Cefepime 2 g IV q8h (or Meropenem 1 g IV q8h) to cover MRSA, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and resistant Enterobacteriaceae.
  - Empiric Treatment of Non-Typhi Salmonella Arteritis / Aneurysm: Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h OR Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h / 750 mg PO q12h.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - MRSA / Gram-Positive Coverage (Vancomycin Allergy / Intolerance / VRE): Daptomycin 6 to 10 mg/kg IV q24h OR Linezolid 600 mg IV/PO q12h.
  - Gram-Negative Coverage (Severe Penicillin / Beta-Lactam Allergy): Aztreonam 2 g IV q8h OR Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q8h / 750 mg PO q12h OR Levofloxacin 750 mg IV/PO q24h.
  - Candida Suppurative Central Vein Thrombosis: Lipid formulation of Amphotericin B 3 to 5 mg/kg IV daily (or conventional Amphotericin B 0.7 mg/kg IV daily plus Flucytosine 25 mg/kg PO q6h) OR Fluconazole 400 to 800 mg IV/PO daily OR Echinocandin (Caspofungin 70 mg loading then 50 mg daily, Micafungin 100 mg daily, or Anidulafungin 200 mg loading then 100 mg daily) for 4 to 6 weeks.
  - Directed Antimicrobial Duration:
    - Superficial Suppurative Thrombophlebitis (Post-Vein Excision): 2 to 3 weeks of parenteral therapy (at least 4 weeks if S. aureus).
    - Great Central Vein Suppurative Thrombophlebitis (Medical Management): 4 to 6 weeks of intravenous antibiotics.
    - Infected Arterial Aneurysms & Prosthetic Vascular Grafts: Minimum 6 weeks of parenteral bactericidal therapy postoperatively, followed by 3 to 6 months of oral step-down therapy (or lifelong oral suppressive therapy for MRSA, Pseudomonas, fungal graft infections, or in situ reconstructions).
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions:**
  - Exploratory Venotomy & Vein Excision (Superficial Thrombophlebitis): Ligation and total excision of the infected vein segment and all involved tributaries from uninfected back-bleeding margin to proximal patent lumen. Delayed wound closure. Indicated when local pus is present, or bacteremia/fever persists >24 to 48 hours despite catheter removal and antibiotics.
  - Surgical Management of Infected Aneurysms & Vascular Grafts:
    1. Complete Graft / Aneurysm Excision: Radical debridement of all infected vascular tissue, foreign material, and perivascular necrotic soft tissue back to healthy bleeding arterial margins.
    2. Revascularization Strategies:
       - Extraanatomic Bypass: Revascularization through uninfected tissue planes (e.g., axillofemoral or femoral-femoral bypass) performed prior to or during excision of infected intra-abdominal/groin grafts.
       - In Situ Reconstruction: Arterial reconstruction using autologous saphenous vein grafts, cryopreserved arterial allografts, or rifampin-bonded synthetic grafts.
  - Drainage of Perivascular & Hepatic Abscesses: CT-guided catheter drainage or open surgical evacuation of perivascular abscesses or associated pyogenic liver abscesses (in septic portal vein thrombosis).

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Local & Vascular: Catastrophic arterial rupture, massive gastrointestinal or retroperitoneal hemorrhage, aortoenteric fistula, aortobronchial fistula, acute limb-threatening ischemia, graft thrombosis/occlusion, and local perivascular abscess formation.
  - Embolic & Systemic: Septic pulmonary emboli with secondary lung abscesses, pneumatoceles, empyema, and respiratory failure (in venous thrombophlebitis); metastatic systemic embolic infarction (brain stroke, splenic abscess, renal infarction, septic arthritis, subperiosteal bone abscesses); and severe sepsis, septic shock, and multiorgan failure.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Permanent limb loss / amputation (up to 41% morbidity in infected infrainguinal vascular grafts; 11% in drug users with excised femoral pseudoaneurysms).
  - Chronic venous insufficiency, post-thrombotic syndrome, and persistent lymphedema.
  - Recurrent bacteremia, persistent endovascular infection, graft nonunion, or secondary graft failure requiring repeat complex revascularization.
  - High long-term mortality (overall 5-year survival for aortic graft infections is ~50%).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - Maki Roll-Plate Technique: Semiquantitative culture method rolling 5.7-cm catheter tip on blood agar plate; >=15 colonies indicates catheter infection.
  - Lemierre Syndrome: Septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum following sore throat/pharyngitis.
  - Pylephlebitis: Septic thrombosis of the portal vein and its branches, complicating intra-abdominal infection.
  - Aortoenteric Fistula: Erosion of an infected aortic graft or stent into the third or fourth portion of the duodenum presenting with a herald bleed.
  - Herald Bleed: A self-limiting initial gastrointestinal bleed preceding massive fatal rupture in an aortoenteric fistula.
  - External Iliac Aneurysm Triad: Anterior thigh pain/quadriceps wasting + leg ischemia + persistent bacteremia.
  - Hepatic Aneurysm Triad: Right upper quadrant pain + jaundice + hemobilia/GI hemorrhage.
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Absence of Local Signs in Superficial Thrombophlebitis: In burn patients and lower-extremity catheter infections, local inflammatory signs (warmth, redness, cord) are absent in >65% of cases; systemic sepsis or septic pulmonary emboli is often the ONLY presenting manifestation!
  - Heparin-Vancomycin Incompatibility: Systemic heparin and vancomycin form a precipitate with partial loss of antibacterial activity when administered simultaneously through the same IV line; they must NEVER be infused through the same line!
  - Heparin Requirement in Pelvic Septic Thrombophlebitis: Postpartum or post-abortion fever in septic pelvic thrombophlebitis is characteristically refractory to broad-spectrum antibiotics alone, defervescing rapidly within 24 to 48 hours ONLY after IV heparin anticoagulation is added!
  - Candida Central Vein Thrombophlebitis vs. Candida Endocarditis: Unlike Candida endocarditis (which almost always requires valve replacement surgery), Candida suppurative thrombophlebitis of the great central veins is curable with medical antifungal therapy alone combined with catheter removal!
  - Superficial Swab Cultures Misleading: Swab cultures of draining sinus tracts or catheter exit skin sites do NOT correlate with deep vascular wall or graft pathogens and should not guide definitive therapy!
