# Uveitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Uveitis refers to inflammation of the uvea (the pigmented, highly vascular middle layer of the eye embryologically sandwiched between the outer cornea-sclera protective layer and the inner retina, derived from the Latin uva meaning grape) or the retina. The uvea comprises the iris (regulates light entering the eye), ciliary body (produces aqueous humor and supports the lens), and choroid (nourishes the retina). Retinitis is classified as a form of uveitis because the retina is intimately involved when choroidal inflammation is present. Infectious uveitis represents intraocular invasion or immune-mediated post-infectious inflammatory destruction of these structures, accounting for 20% to 50% of all uveitis cases worldwide (with higher frequencies in tropical and developing countries).
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Anatomical Classification (International Uveitis Study Group / SUN Criteria):
     - Anterior Uveitis: Inflammation primarily involving the iris (iritis), anterior ciliary body (cyclitis), or both (iridocyclitis). Characterized by white blood cells in the aqueous humor, keratic precipitates on the corneal endothelium, iris nodules, and synechiae. Accounts for up to 90% of uveitis cases in community practices (and 57% in referral centers). Approximately 10% of anterior uveitis cases have an infectious etiology.
     - Intermediate Uveitis: Inflammation primarily involving the anterior vitreous, ciliary body (pars plana), and adjacent peripheral retina (pars planitis). Characterized by vitreous cells, vitreous "snowballs", and "snow banks" over the pars plana. Infectious causes are rare (<1%).
     - Posterior Uveitis: Inflammation primarily involving the choroid (choroiditis), retina (retinitis), both (chorioretinitis), or retinal vessels (retinal vasculitis/periphlebitis). Characterized by chorioretinal lesions, vitreous cells/vitritis, or retinal vascular sheathing. Accounts for 11% to 25% of cases in referral centers. Approximately 50% of posterior uveitis cases have an infectious etiology.
     - Panuveitis: Inflammation involving all three parts of the uvea (anterior, intermediate, and posterior segments simultaneously) with white blood cells in both aqueous and vitreous humors.
     - Extended Anatomical Types: Keratouveitis (extending to cornea) and Sclerouveitis (extending to sclera).
  2. Etiologic & Clinical Necrotizing Retinitis Syndromes:
     - Acute Retinal Necrosis (ARN): Rapidly progressive, necrotizing retinitis occurring mainly in immunocompetent hosts, caused by HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, or rarely CMV/EBV. Defined by American Uveitis Society criteria: (1) focal peripheral retinal necrosis, (2) rapid circumferential progression, (3) occlusive vasculopathy, and (4) prominent aqueous/vitreous vitritis.
     - Progressive Outer Retinal Necrosis (PORN): Rapidly progressive viral retinitis targeting outer deep retinal layers, occurring almost exclusively in severely immunocompromised hosts (HIV patients with CD4+ T-cell count < 100 cells/mm3, average 20 cells/mm3). Characterized by outer retinal involvement, lack of vitritis/aqueous cell, and lack of retinal vasculitis.
     - CMV Retinitis: Indolent or progressive white hemorrhagic necrotizing retinitis in severely immunocompromised patients (AIDS CD4+ < 50 cells/mm3), characterized by a clear vitreous view due to absence of significant vitritis.
  3. Pathological Reaction Types:
     - Granulomatous Uveitis: Featuring large "mutton-fat" keratic precipitates on the corneal endothelium, iris nodules (Koeppe nodules at pupil margin, Busacca nodules on iris stroma), and choroidal granulomas (seen in Syphilis, Tuberculosis, Sarcoidosis, Toxocara, Cat-scratch disease, Brucellosis, Whipple disease).
     - Nongranulomatous Uveitis: Featuring fine, small keratic precipitates, diffuse aqueous flare/cells, without iris nodules (seen in acute viral anterior uveitis, Leptospirosis, Lyme disease).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Epidemiology: Uveitis has a prevalence of 70 to 115 cases per 100,000 population in the United States and accounts for up to 10% of global blindness. Mean age at presentation ranges from 35 to 45 years, though it can occur at any age (e.g., ocular toxocariasis occurs predominantly in children). More common in women overall in population studies.
  2. Geographic & Regional Variation in Infectious Etiologies:
     - Infections cause 20% to 50% of uveitis cases globally (higher in tropical/developing nations).
     - In the US (Boston referral center study): Infectious causes accounted for ~10% of anterior uveitis (HSV 10%, Syphilis/TB/Lyme <1%), <1% of intermediate (Lyme <1%), ~50% of posterior (Toxoplasma 25%, CMV 12%, ARN 6%, Toxocara 3%, Syphilis 2%, Candida <1%), and 10% of panuveitis (Syphilis 6%, TB 2%, Candida 2%).
     - In South Africa: Infections cause 47% of uveitis cases, with Syphilis (16%) being the most common.
     - In Vietnam: Infections cause 27% of cases, with Tuberculosis (9%) being the most common.
     - In Iran: Infections cause 20% of cases, with Herpetic infections and TB predominant.
     - In India: Eales disease (TB-associated occlusive retinal vasculitis) accounts for 1 in 135 referrals to specialty eye centers; high prevalence of Leptospiral uveitis following monsoon flooding.
  3. Host Risk Factors & Exposures: Severe immunodeficiency / HIV/AIDS (CD4+ < 50 cells/mm3 for CMV retinitis; CD4+ < 100 cells/mm3 for PORN; organ transplant recipients), pet/animal contact (cat ownership/exposure for Bartonella henselae and Toxocara cati; dog feces/contaminated soil/sandboxes for Toxocara canis), consumption of undercooked meat or oocyst-contaminated water (Toxoplasma gondii), travel or residence in endemic regions (Histoplasma in Ohio/Mississippi river valleys; Leptospira in tropical flood zones; Brucella in Middle East/Peru; Ebola in West Africa; Zika/Chikungunya/Dengue in tropical mosquito-endemic regions), high-risk sexual behavior (Treponema pallidum / Syphilis), and HLA genetic associations (HLA-DR15/HLA-DQ6 for presumed ocular histoplasmosis syndrome; HLA-B27 for acute anterior uveitis / reactive arthritis).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Viral Pathogens: Herpes Simplex Virus type 1 (HSV-1; causes 90% of infectious anterior uveitis, ARN, keratouveitis), Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV; anterior uveitis, ARN, PORN), Cytomegalovirus (CMV; CMV retinitis in AIDS/transplant, CMV chronic anterior uveitis in immunocompetent), Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV), West Nile Virus (WNV; multifocal chorioretinitis), Dengue virus, Chikungunya virus, Ebola virus (post-EVD panuveitis), Zika virus, HTLV-1, HIV.
  - Parasitic Pathogens: Toxoplasma gondii (most common cause of infectious posterior uveitis overall in the US and worldwide), Toxocara canis and Toxocara cati (ocular toxocariasis), Onchocerca volvulus, Acanthamoeba.
  - Bacterial Pathogens: Treponema pallidum (Syphilis; "the great imitator", causes anterior, intermediate, posterior, or panuveitis), Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Tuberculosis; choroidal tubercles, serpiginous-like choroiditis, Eales disease), Bartonella henselae (Cat-scratch disease; neuroretinitis, macular star), Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme disease), Leptospira interrogans (Leptospiral panuveitis/hypopyon uveitis; serovar Australis), Brucella melitensis / Brucella spp. (relapsing uveitis), Mycobacterium leprae (leprosy iridocyclitis), Tropheryma whipplei (Whipple disease panuveitis).
  - Fungal Pathogens: Candida albicans and non-albicans Candida species (endogenous Candida chorioretinitis/endophthalmitis), Histoplasma capsulatum (Presumed Ocular Histoplasmosis Syndrome / POHS), Aspergillus species, Cryptococcus neoformans, Coccidioides immitis.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children & Adolescents: Toxocara canis / T. cati (ocular toxocariasis occurs almost exclusively in children exposed to soil/sandboxes), Toxoplasma gondii (congenital or acquired toxoplasmosis), Congenital Syphilis (interstitial keratitis and uveitis in teens), HSV-1 (primary or recurrent in young patients), Cat-scratch disease (Bartonella henselae neuroretinitis in children/adolescents), Lyme disease (borrelial uveitis/arthritis in children).
  - Adults & Elderly: Toxoplasma gondii reactivation, HSV-1 / VZV anterior uveitis and ARN, Treponema pallidum (ocular syphilis in sexually active adults and older adults), Mycobacterium tuberculosis, CMV retinitis and PORN (in immunocompromised/AIDS adults), Presumed Ocular Histoplasmosis Syndrome (in young/middle-aged adults < 50 years).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Severely Immunocompromised / HIV / Transplant Hosts: Cytomegalovirus (CMV retinitis), Varicella-Zoster Virus (Progressive Outer Retinal Necrosis / PORN), Cryptococcus neoformans, Aspergillus species, Candida species, Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), Microsporidia (Encephalitozoon cuniculi, Vittaforma corneae), Toxoplasma gondii (multifocal/atypical necrotizing retinitis).
  - Zoonotic, Tropical & Re-emerging Agents: Leptospira interrogans (following monsoon flood exposure), Brucella melitensis, Mycobacterium leprae, Tropheryma whipplei (Whipple disease panuveitis with PAS-positive macrophages), Ebola virus (persisting in aqueous humor during convalescence), Chikungunya virus, Zika virus, West Nile virus (linear/geographic chorioretinal lesions), Bartonella grahamii and Bartonella elizabethae.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Route of Intraocular Entry: Microorganisms enter the uveal tract via hematogenous dissemination across the rich choroidal/ciliary microvasculature (Toxoplasma, Candida, Syphilis, TB, Leptospira, WNV, Histoplasma, Bartonella), retrograde axonal transport from sensory ganglia along the trigeminal nerve CN V1 (HSV-1, VZV), direct intraocular viral persistence (Ebola virus persisting in aqueous humor months after systemic recovery), or accidental ingestion of parasite eggs/oocysts leading to larval migration into ocular circulation (Toxocara, Toxoplasma).
  - Viral Reactivation & Neurotropism: HSV and VZV establish latency in the trigeminal ganglion; reactivation leads to anterograde virion transport to the iris and ciliary body (causing anterior uveitis with trabeculitis and ischemic iris atrophy) or peripheral retina (causing acute retinal necrosis / ARN or PORN).
  - Parasitic Encystment & Reactivation: Toxoplasma gondii tachyzoites lodge in retinal tissue during initial bacteremia/parasitemia, forming tissue cysts (bradyzoites) that remain dormant within retinal cells. Rupture of a bradyzoite cyst triggers intense local cell-mediated immune responses, causing necrotizing retinitis adjacent to an old hyperpigmented chorioretinal scar.
  - Immune-Mediated Vasculitis & Hypersensitivity: T. pallidum and M. tuberculosis induce direct tissue destruction alongside heavy immune complex deposition, cell-mediated delayed-type hypersensitivity, and occlusive retinal vasculitis (periphlebitis). In Eales disease, hypersensitivity to tuberculoprotein causes retinal capillary occlusion, ischemia, and secondary neovascularization. In Leptospirosis and Dengue, uveitis occurs predominantly during the delayed immune phase weeks to months after systemic fever clearance.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Uveal Vascular Permeability & Exudation: Inflammatory cytokines increase iris and ciliary capillary permeability, causing protein leakage (flare) and white blood cell extravasation into the anterior chamber (cells) and vitreous body (vitritis).
  - Keratic Precipitates & Iris Damage: Infiltrating macrophages and lymphocytes adhere to the corneal endothelium, forming "mutton-fat" (granulomatous) or fine (nongranulomatous) keratic precipitates. Iris stromal infiltration leads to Koeppe/Busacca nodules, ischemic sectoral iris atrophy (pathognomonic for HSV/VZV/CMV), and posterior synechiae (adhesions between iris and lens).
  - Retinal Necrosis & Choroidal Destruction: Direct viral infection (HSV, VZV, CMV) or Toxoplasma tachyzoite destruction causes full-thickness liquefactive retinal necrosis, occlusive retinal arteritis/phlebitis, and destruction of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE).
  - Vitreous Traction & Retinal Detachment: Retinal necrosis creates full-thickness retinal breaks; subsequent vitreous organization and fibrotic traction lead to rhegmatogenous or tractional retinal detachment in over 50% of ARN cases.
  - Macular Edema & Choroidal Neovascularization: Chronic uveal inflammation stimulates vascular endothelial growth factors, inducing cystoid macular edema (a major cause of vision loss) or choroidal neovascular membranes (CNVM in POHS or Toxoplasma).

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Anterior Uveitis: Acute onset of deep eye pain, ocular redness (ciliary flush), marked photophobia (light sensitivity), and decreased visual acuity.
  - Intermediate Uveitis: Painless blurring of vision and prominent "floaters" (spots or webs moving in field of vision); pain and photophobia are characteristically ABSENT.
  - Posterior Uveitis & Panuveitis: Painless (or mildly uncomfortable) loss of vision, central scotomas, blurred vision, and floaters.
  - Associated Historical Features: Prior episodes of herpetic keratitis or cold sores; recent cat exposure, scratch, or kitten ownership (CSD); exposure to dogs/soil/sandboxes (Toxocara); history of treated or untreated STI / painless genital chancre (Syphilis); cough/night sweats/TB contact; unpasteurized dairy or livestock exposure (Brucella); wading in floodwaters (Leptospira); recent viral illness or mosquito bite in endemic areas (Dengue, Chikungunya, Zika, WNV); history of Ebola virus disease recovery; or severe immunocompromise (AIDS, organ transplant).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Anterior Segment Findings:
    - Ciliary Flush: Dilation of deep episcleral vessels forming a red ring around the corneal limbus.
    - Anterior Chamber Cells & Flare: White blood cells floating in aqueous (graded 1+ to 4+) and protein flare; heavy cellular settling produces a hypopyon (layer of pus in inferior AC).
    - Keratic Precipitates (KPs): Clusters of inflammatory cells on the posterior corneal surface; classified as "mutton-fat" KPs (large, greasy, waxy granulomatous deposits typical of Syphilis, TB, Sarcoid) or fine/stellate KPs (nongranulomatous, seen in HSV, VZV, CMV).
    - Iris Lesions: Koeppe nodules (at pupillary margin), Busacca nodules (on iris surface), granulomatous iris nodules, posterior synechiae (adhesions between posterior iris and anterior lens capsule causing irregular pupil), and patchy, sectoral, or diffuse iris atrophy (pathognomonic sign of HSV, VZV, or CMV). Elevated intraocular pressure (glaucomatocyclitic crises / trabeculitis).
  - Intermediate Segment Findings:
    - Vitreous Cells & Snowballs: Aggregates of inflammatory cells floating in the anterior vitreous humor.
    - Snowbanks: White fibrotic inflammatory exudate layered over the inferior pars plana.
  - Posterior Segment Findings:
    - Ocular Toxoplasmosis: Classic creamy-yellow, focal necrotizing chorioretinal lesion immediately adjacent to an old, dark, hyperpigmented chorioretinal scar, accompanied by intense vitritis ("headlight in the fog").
    - Acute Retinal Necrosis (ARN): Peripheral, sharply demarcated, yellowish-white confluent retinal necrosis spreading circumferentially, occlusive retinal arteritis/vasculitis, and marked vitritis.
    - Progressive Outer Retinal Necrosis (PORN): Deep/outer multifocal retinal yellow-white lesions rapidly coalescing to full-thickness necrosis, with ABSENCE of vitritis, ABSENCE of anterior chamber cell, and ABSENCE of retinal vasculitis in a severely immunocompromised patient.
    - CMV Retinitis: Dense white retinal infiltrates with prominent intraretinal hemorrhages ("pizza-pie" or "cheese and ketchup" appearance) and perivascular sheathing ("frosted branch angiitis"), with a CLEAR vitreous (absence of significant vitritis).
    - Cat-Scratch Disease Neuroretinitis: Optic disc edema/papillitis associated with a "macular star" (striking sunburst radial pattern of hard lipid exudates around the macula).
    - Syphilitic Posterior Uveitis: Multifocal chorioretinitis, acute posterior placoid chorioretinitis (large, solitary, yellow macular placoid lesions), pseudoretinitis pigmentosa, or optic neuritis.
    - Tuberculous Posterior Uveitis: Choroidal tubercles (yellowish subretinal nodules), presumed tubercular serpiginous-like choroiditis, and Eales disease (occlusive retinal periphlebitis with vascular sheathing, peripheral capillary nonperfusion, and vitreous hemorrhages).
    - West Nile Virus Chorioretinitis: Bilateral multifocal chorioretinitis arranged in linear or geographic patterns reflecting retinal nerve fiber layer anatomy.
    - Presumed Ocular Histoplasmosis Syndrome (POHS): Triad of (1) multiple punched-out chorioretinal scars ("histo spots"), (2) peripapillary atrophy, and (3) choroidal neovascularization, with a COMPLETELY CLEAR vitreous and aqueous (no cells).
    - Ocular Toxocariasis: Unilateral peripheral chorioretinal granuloma (50%), posterior pole chorioretinal granuloma (25%), or diffuse endophthalmitis/panuveitis (25%).
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Creamy-yellow active chorioretinal lesion immediately adjacent to a dark, hyperpigmented old scar with dense vitritis ("headlight in the fog", pathognomonic of ocular toxoplasmosis reactivation).
  - Macular star (sunburst radial hard exudates around macula) combined with optic disc edema (pathognomonic of Bartonella henselae neuroretinitis).
  - Sectoral iris atrophy with elevated intraocular pressure and keratic precipitates (pathognomonic of herpetic anterior uveitis / HSV or VZV).
  - Punched-out "histo spots", peripapillary atrophy, and choroidal neovascularization with a COMPLETELY CLEAR vitreous (pathognomonic of Presumed Ocular Histoplasmosis Syndrome).
  - Rapidly progressive peripheral deep retinal necrosis lacking vitritis and vasculitis in an AIDS patient with CD4+ < 50 (pathognomonic of Progressive Outer Retinal Necrosis / PORN).
  - White "snowbanks" layered over the inferior pars plana and vitreous "snowballs" (pathognomonic of intermediate uveitis / pars planitis).

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Systemic Serologic & Infectious Workup:
    - Mandatory Syphilis Testing: Specific treponemal test (TP-PA / Treponema pallidum particle agglutination or FTA-ABS) MUST be ordered on ALL patients presenting with uveitis, regardless of anatomical category, because syphilis is "the great imitator". Non-treponemal tests (RPR / VDRL) must also be checked to gauge activity (high titers in secondary syphilitic uveitis), though RPR can be negative in indolent tertiary ocular syphilis or falsely positive in autoimmune disease. TP-PA is preferred over FTA-ABS due to lower false-positive rates in rheumatologic conditions.
    - Tuberculosis Screening: Tuberculin Skin Test (TST / Mantoux) or Interferon-Gamma Release Assay (IGRA / QuantiFERON-TB Gold).
    - Lyme Disease Serology: Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) confirmed by Western Blot (in endemic regions).
    - Cat-Scratch Disease Serology: Serum IgG/IgM titers for Bartonella henselae.
    - Toxoplasma Serology: Serum IgG and IgM antibodies (IgG confirms prior exposure; negative IgG strongly excludes toxoplasmosis).
    - Viral Serology: HIV antibody/viral load screening, HSV/VZV/CMV IgG/IgM.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Ophthalmic Diagnostics:
    - Slit-Lamp Biomicroscopy: Primary essential tool to quantify aqueous cells and flare, characterize keratic precipitates, identify iris nodules/atrophy/synechiae, and examine anterior vitreous.
    - Indirect Ophthalmoscopy: Mandatory dilated funduscopic exam to visualize peripheral retina (essential for detecting early ARN, peripheral granulomas, or snowbanks).
    - Fluorescein Angiography (FA) & Indocyanine Green Angiography (ICGA): Visualizes retinal vascular sheathing, capillary nonperfusion, macular edema, optic disc leakage, and choroidal hyper/hypofluorescent lesions (e.g., placoid lesions in syphilis or choroidal tubercles in TB).
    - Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT): High-resolution cross-sectional imaging quantifying cystoid macular edema, macular star exudates, subretinal fluid, and choroidal neovascular membranes.
    - Chest Radiography & High-Resolution CT (HRCT): Chest X-ray or HRCT to evaluate for pulmonary tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, or histoplasmosis.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Diagnostic Aqueous Paracentesis or Vitreous Sampling: Obtained via anterior chamber paracentesis (aqueous tap, 0.1 mL) or vitreous aspirate/vitrectomy.
  - Molecular Testing (Polymerase Chain Reaction / PCR):
    - Real-Time PCR on Aqueous or Vitreous Humor: The diagnostic test of choice for herpetic uveitis and necrotizing viral retinitis. Vitreous yields higher sensitivity (96% in ARN) than aqueous. Multiplex PCR assays detect HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, CMV, and EBV DNA.
    - Diagnostic Utility & Limitations: Highly effective in ARN, PORN, and herpetic anterior uveitis. However, PCR sensitivity is low in ocular toxoplasmosis (27% sensitivity, 100% specificity), ocular Whipple disease (60% sensitivity with PAS-positive macrophages on biopsy), and ocular TB (false negatives due to PCR inhibitors, false positives in 23% of PPD-negative controls).
  - Goldmann-Witmer Coefficient (GWC): Compares intraocular (aqueous/vitreous) specific antibody IgG titers to serum IgG titers normalized for total IgG; a GWC ratio > 3.0 confirms local intraocular antibody production (useful in ocular toxoplasmosis, Toxocara, and Rubella/CMV anterior uveitis).
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - No Universal Gold Standard Test Exists: Clinical diagnosis established by an expert ophthalmologist based on characteristic pathognomonic physical examination findings, supported by specific serology (e.g., positive specific treponemal TP-PA test for ocular syphilis), intraocular PCR verification (for viral retinitis / ARN / PORN), or therapeutic response to specific antimicrobial agents.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Emergency Ophthalmology Co-Management: Urgent referral and daily co-management between infectious disease clinicians and retina specialists.
  - Topical Anti-inflammatory & Cycloplegic Therapy:
    - Topical Cycloplegics: Atropine 1%, Cyclopentolate 1%, or Homatropine 5% drops applied 2 to 4 times daily to relieve severe ciliary body spasm, reduce pain/photophobia, and prevent or break posterior synechiae.
    - Topical Corticosteroids: Prednisolone acetate 1% drops applied hourly to q2h for non-necrotizing, non-viral anterior uveitis or as an adjunct in ocular toxoplasmosis / CSD / TB / Syphilis. STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED as monotherapy in active, untreated viral retinitis (ARN, PORN, CMV) or fungal/bacterial intraocular infections.
  - Control of Intraocular Pressure: Topical aqueous suppressants (Timolol, Brimonidine, Dorzolamide) or oral Acetazolamide for secondary glaucoma (avoid miotics like pilocarpine or prostaglandin analogs that worsen inflammation).
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Ocular Toxoplasmosis (First-Line Regimen - Triple / Quadruple Therapy for 4 to 6 weeks):
    - Oral Pyrimethamine (loading dose 100 mg PO x 1 day, then 25 to 50 mg PO daily) COMBINED WITH
    - Oral Sulfadiazine (loading dose 2 g PO, then 1 g PO 4 times daily) COMBINED WITH
    - Oral Leucovorin / Folinic Acid (10 to 25 mg PO daily, to prevent pyrimethamine-induced bone marrow suppression) COMBINED WITH
    - Systemic Corticosteroids (Oral Prednisone 0.5 to 1.0 mg/kg/day, initiated 24 to 48 hours AFTER starting antiparasitic coverage, to suppress destructive macular inflammation).
    - Alternative Oral Regimen: Oral Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX, 1 double-strength tablet PO twice daily) PLUS Leucovorin.
  - Acute Retinal Necrosis (ARN - Emergency Regimen):
    - Intravenous Acyclovir (10 to 15 mg/kg IV every 8 hours for 10 to 14 days) OR Oral Valacyclovir (2 g PO 3 times daily / 6 g total daily, high-dose regimen) to halt rapid retinitis progression.
    - Direct Intravitreal Antiviral Injection: Immediate intravitreal injection of Foscarnet (2.4 mg in 0.1 mL) or Ganciclovir (2 mg in 0.1 mL) administered at diagnostic paracentesis to achieve immediate high intraocular drug levels and reduce retinal detachment risk.
    - Maintenance Oral Antiviral Suppression: Oral Valacyclovir (1 g PO 3 times daily) or Acyclovir (800 mg PO 5 times daily) continued for 3 to 6 months post-IV therapy to prevent late involvement of the contralateral eye.
  - Progressive Outer Retinal Necrosis (PORN) & Severe Immunocompromised Viral Retinitis:
    - Combination Intravenous and Intravitreal Antiviral Therapy: Intravenous Ganciclovir (5 mg/kg IV q12h) OR Intravenous Foscarnet (90 mg/kg IV q12h) COMBINED WITH repeated twice-weekly Intravitreal Foscarnet (2.4 mg) or Ganciclovir (2 mg) injections.
  - Ocular Syphilis (Treated as Neurosyphilis):
    - Aqueous Crystalline Penicillin G (18 to 24 million units daily, administered as 3 to 4 million units IV every 4 hours or continuous infusion) for 10 to 14 days.
    - Alternative: Procaine Penicillin 2.4 million units IM daily PLUS Probenecid 500 mg PO 4 times daily for 10 to 14 days.
  - Ocular Tuberculosis (Treated as TB Meningitis):
    - Standard Four-Drug RIPE Regimen (Rifampin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol) for 2 months, followed by Rifampin and Isoniazid for 7 to 10 additional months (total 9 to 12 months therapy), combined with systemic corticosteroids.
  - Ocular Bartonellosis / Cat-Scratch Neuroretinitis:
    - Oral Doxycycline (100 mg PO twice daily) or Oral Azithromycin (500 mg PO daily) for 4 to 6 weeks, with or without Oral Rifampin (300 mg PO twice daily), combined with systemic corticosteroids.
  - Ocular Lyme Disease (Treated as Neuroborreliosis):
    - Intravenous Ceftriaxone (2 g IV daily) for 14 to 21 days.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Penicillin-Allergic Patients with Ocular Syphilis: Ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily for 10 to 14 days (with close supervision) or desensitization to Penicillin.
  - CMV Retinitis Alternatives: Oral Valganciclovir (900 mg PO twice daily for 21 days induction, then 900 mg daily maintenance), IV Ganciclovir, or IV Foscarnet.
  - Alternative Ocular Toxoplasmosis Regimens: Intravitreal Clindamycin (1.0 mg) plus Dexamethasone (0.4 mg) injections, or oral Clindamycin (300 mg PO 4 times daily) plus Azithromycin.
  - Leptospiral Uveitis: Oral Doxycycline (100 mg PO twice daily) or IV Penicillin G, though late immune-phase uveitis responds primarily to topical corticosteroids and cycloplegics.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Prophylactic Barrier Laser Photocoagulation: Applied around demarcated areas of retinal necrosis in ARN to create a 360-degree chorioretinal scar barrier, preventing late rhegmatogenous retinal detachment.
  - Therapeutic Pars Plana Vitrectomy (PPV) & Retinal Detachment Repair: Mechanical vitreous débridement, silicone oil or gas tamponade, and scleral buckling for severe retinal detachment, dense non-clearing vitreous hemorrhage, or persistent vitritis.
  - Intraocular Antiviral / Antiparasitic / Corticosteroid Injections: Diagnostic and therapeutic intravitreal injections of Foscarnet, Ganciclovir, Amphotericin B, Voriconazole, Clindamycin, or Triamcinolone.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Rapid Rhegmatogenous & Tractional Retinal Detachment: Complicates >50% of ARN cases within 3 weeks to 5 months of onset due to retinal necrotic tears and vitreous contraction.
  - Secondary Glaucoma & Trabeculitis: Severe intraocular pressure elevation secondary to trabecular meshwork inflammatory clogging, pupillary block from 360-degree posterior synechiae (seclusio pupillae), or herpetic trabeculitis.
  - Occlusive Retinal Vasculitis & Retinal Artery/Vein Occlusion: Ischemic infarction of the retina and macula.
  - Cystoid Macular Edema (CME): Fluid accumulation in the central retina, serving as the single most common cause of permanent visual reduction in non-necrotizing uveitis.
  - Vitreous Hemorrhage: Complicating retinal neovascularization in Eales disease or Dengue.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Irreversible Blindness & Severe Visual Impairment: Complete loss of vision (No Light Perception) or permanent scotomas from macular scarring, optic atrophy, or retinal necrosis.
  - Complicated Cataract (Uveitic Cataract): Posterior subcapsular cataract formation resulting from chronic intraocular inflammation and prolonged topical/systemic corticosteroid therapy.
  - Choroidal Neovascular Membranes (CNVM) & Macular Scarring: Subretinal neovascular sprouting causing central disciform macular scars (in POHS or Toxoplasma).
  - Phthisis Bulbi & Hypotony: Atrophy, shrinkage, and hypotony (IOP < 5 mmHg) of the non-functional globe following end-stage ciliary body destruction.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Mutton-fat keratic precipitates" (large, greasy white blood cell deposits on corneal endothelium in granulomatous uveitis).
  - "Headlight in the fog" (focal creamy-yellow Toxoplasma retinitis viewed through dense vitreous vitritis).
  - "Macular star" (sunburst radial pattern of hard lipid exudates surrounding the macula in Bartonella neuroretinitis).
  - "Pizza-pie" / "Cheese and ketchup" appearance (dense white retinal necrosis with widespread retinal hemorrhages in CMV retinitis).
  - "Frosted branch angiitis" (severe perivascular sheathing of retinal vessels resembling frozen tree branches).
  - "Snowbanks" and "Snowballs" (pars plana exudates and vitreal white blood cell clumps in intermediate uveitis).
  - "Histo spots" (punched-out chorioretinal scars in Presumed Ocular Histoplasmosis Syndrome).
  - "Eales disease" (idiopathic or TB-associated occlusive retinal periphlebitis in young men).
  - "Kyrieleis arterial plaques" (segmental, glistening white periarterial plaques seen in ocular toxoplasmosis or herpetic retinitis).
  - "Candle-wax drippings" (perivascular inflammatory exudates in retinal vasculitis / sarcoidosis).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - The "Clear Vitreous" Exception in CMV Retinitis & PORN: While Toxoplasma, ARN, and bacterial posterior segment infections cause dense, cloudy vitritis ("headlight in the fog"), CMV retinitis and PORN occur in severely immunocompromised hosts who lack the CD4+ T-cells required to mount a vitreal inflammatory response; thus, the vitreous remains COMPLETELY CLEAR, providing a sharp view of the retina.
  - The "Clear Vitreous" Exception in POHS: Presumed Ocular Histoplasmosis Syndrome (POHS) presents with extensive chorioretinal "histo spots", peripapillary atrophy, and choroidal neovascularization, but characteristically features ZERO cells in the vitreous or anterior chamber (completely clear vitreous).
  - False-Negative Treponema RPR in Tertiary Ocular Syphilis: Non-treponemal RPR tests can be negative in up to 30% of patients with late or indolent ocular syphilis; therefore, a negative RPR does NOT exclude ocular syphilis, and a specific treponemal test (TP-PA or FTA-ABS) must be ordered on every uveitis patient.
  - Inefficacy of Oral/Systemic Corticosteroid Monotherapy in Herpetic Retinitis: Prescribing systemic steroids alone for misdiagnosed "autoimmune" posterior uveitis when the true cause is unrecognized HSV or VZV leads to catastrophic, explosive full-thickness retinal destruction and total blindness within days.
  - Inefficacy of Negative Aqueous/Vitreous PCR in Excluding Toxoplasmosis or TB: Aqueous PCR sensitivity for Toxoplasma gondii is only 27%, and PCR for ocular TB suffers from false-negatives due to intraocular PCR inhibitors; thus, a negative PCR result MUST NOT be used to rule out toxoplasmosis or intraocular TB when clinical findings are characteristic.
  - Ebola Virus Persistence in Convalescent Intraocular Fluid: Ebola virus can persist in viable, infectious form inside the immune-privileged aqueous humor for months after the patient has completely recovered from systemic Ebola virus disease and cleared the virus from blood, presenting as delayed panuveitis.
  - Retinoblastoma Diagnostic Trap in Ocular Toxocariasis: Vitreous aspiration or biopsy in a young child with a solitary leukocoria/retinal lesion suspected of ocular toxocariasis is STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED if retinoblastoma is in the differential diagnosis, because needle biopsy causes extraocular tumor seeding and fatal dissemination.
