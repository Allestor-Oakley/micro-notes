# Conjunctivitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Microbial conjunctivitis is an acute or chronic inflammation of the thin, transparent mucous membrane lining the inner surface of the eyelids (palpebral conjunctiva) and the anterior outer surface of the eyeball (bulbar conjunctiva), caused by bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites. It is characterized by conjunctival vessel hyperemia, ocular irritation or foreign-body sensation, increased tearing, and proteinaceous or mucopurulent discharge, usually without severe pain or visual loss unless the underlying cornea becomes secondarily involved (keratoconjunctivitis).
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Temporal Classification:
     - Acute Conjunctivitis: Symptoms developing and resolving within 4 to 6 weeks.
     - Chronic Conjunctivitis: Symptoms persisting for longer than 4 to 6 weeks.
  2. Etiologic / Microbiologic Categories:
     - Viral Conjunctivitis: Most common cause of infectious conjunctivitis overall (prevalent in adults); includes Adenoviral Conjunctivitis (Pharyngoconjunctival Fever caused by serotypes 3, 4, 7; Epidemic Keratoconjunctivitis caused by serotypes 8, 19), Acute Hemorrhagic Conjunctivitis (Coxsackievirus A24 variant, Enterovirus 70), Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) Conjunctivitis, Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV) Conjunctivitis, Vaccinia / Variola Conjunctivitis, and other viral etiologies (Molluscum contagiosum, Newcastle disease virus, Avian influenza H7N7, Measles, Mumps, Rubella, EBV).
     - Bacterial Conjunctivitis: More prevalent in children; includes Acute (Mucopurulent) Bacterial Conjunctivitis (S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, Moraxella), Hyperacute (Purulent) Bacterial Conjunctivitis (Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Neisseria meningitidis), and Chronic Bacterial Conjunctivitis (Staphylococcus spp., Moraxella spp., enteric gram-negative rods like Proteus mirabilis, K. pneumoniae, E. coli, Serratia marcescens).
     - Chlamydial Conjunctivitis: Caused by Chlamydia trachomatis; includes Adult Inclusion Conjunctivitis (serotypes B, Ba, D-K), Trachoma (chronic follicular keratoconjunctivitis caused by repeated infection with serotypes A, B, Ba, C), Neonatal Chlamydial Conjunctivitis, and Lymphogranuloma Venereum (serotypes L1-L3).
     - Neonatal Conjunctivitis (Ophthalmia Neonatorum): Any conjunctivitis occurring within the first 4 weeks of life; includes Chemical Conjunctivitis (silver nitrate response within 1-24 hours), Gonococcal (24-48 hours), Gram-positive bacterial (2-5 days), Chlamydial (5-14 days), HSV (6-14 days), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (5-18 days).
     - Parinaud Oculoglandular Conjunctivitis: Granulomatous follicular conjunctivitis accompanied by ipsilateral painful preauricular or submandibular lymphadenopathy (most commonly caused by Bartonella henselae / Cat-Scratch Disease; also Francisella tularensis, M. tuberculosis, Sporothrix schenckii, Syphilis, EBV, Mumps).
     - Fungal & Parasitic Conjunctivitis: Fungal (Candida spp., Blastomyces, Sporothrix, Rhinosporidium seeberi, Microsporidia including Vittaforma corneae / Encephalitozoon); Parasitic (Phthirus pubis / lice, Demodex mites, African trypanosomiasis, Loa loa, Cryptosporidium, fly larvae).
  3. Morphologic / Reaction-Based Categories:
     - Follicular Conjunctivitis: Avascular, translucent lymphoid elevations (follicles) with surrounding vessel networks, prominent in viral (adenovirus, HSV), chlamydial, and toxic infections.
     - Papillary Conjunctivitis: Hyperemic mounds of conjunctival epithelium with a central vascular core, seen in bacterial infections, allergic disease, vernal conjunctivitis, contact lens wear, and ocular prostheses.
     - Membranous and Pseudomembranous Conjunctivitis: Coagulum of fibrin, leukocytes, and cellular debris; true membranes bleed upon peeling due to granulation tissue growth, whereas pseudomembranes peel easily without bleeding (seen in EKC, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, S. pyogenes, N. gonorrhoeae, HSV, Stevens-Johnson syndrome).
     - Phlyctenular & Granulomatous Conjunctivitis: Delayed hypersensitivity nodules at the limbus (Staphylococcus, TB, coccidioidomycosis) or discrete granulomas (foreign body, sarcoid, CSD, tularemia).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Epidemiology: Conjunctivitis affects male and female patients of all ages globally. Viruses are the single most common cause of infectious conjunctivitis overall, with bacteria being the second most frequent agent. Bacterial conjunctivitis is more prevalent in children, whereas viral conjunctivitis is more prevalent in adults. S. aureus is the most common bacterial cause in adults and children over 6 years old; H. influenzae and S. pneumoniae predominate in children under 6 years old.
  2. Risk Factors: Contact lens wear (a major risk factor for polymicrobial, Pseudomonas, or Acanthamoeba keratitis/conjunctivitis), contaminated ocular medications, exposure to an infected individual (fomites, towels, swimming pools), visit to camps or poultry farms (avian influenza H7N7, Newcastle disease), vaginal delivery from a mother with untreated sexually transmitted infections (C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae, HSV-2), poor hygiene and face-washing access in overcrowded endemic communities (trachoma), liquid detergent capsule exposure in children, and systemic immunodeficiency / HIV infection (predisposing to microsporidial, fungal, or molluscum contagiosum conjunctivitis).

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Viral Pathogens: Adenovirus (most common overall, serotypes 1-11, 14, 16, 19, 29; serotypes 3, 4, 7 in pharyngoconjunctival fever; serotypes 8, 19 in epidemic keratoconjunctivitis), Coxsackievirus A24 variant, Enterovirus 70, Herpes Simplex Virus type 1 (HSV-1) and type 2 (HSV-2), Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV).
  - Bacterial Pathogens: Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae (nontypeable and type b), Moraxella catarrhalis and Moraxella lacunata, Corynebacterium species, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus pyogenes, Viridans streptococci, and enteric gram-negative rods (Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Serratia marcescens, Pseudomonas aeruginosa).
  - Chlamydial Pathogens: Chlamydia trachomatis (serotypes B, Ba, D-K in adult inclusion conjunctivitis and neonatal conjunctivitis; serotypes A, B, Ba, C in trachoma; serotypes L1-L3 in lymphogranuloma venereum).
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Neonates (First 4 Weeks of Life - Ophthalmia Neonatorum):
    - Chlamydia trachomatis (most common infectious cause in the US; 30-40% transmission rate from infected mother).
    - Neisseria gonorrhoeae (hyperacute purulent discharge at 24-48 hours).
    - Gram-positive bacteria: Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus species (at 2-5 days).
    - Gram-negative bacilli: Pseudomonas aeruginosa (greenish discharge at 5-18 days; can cause rapid corneal perforation and systemic sepsis), Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, Serratia marcescens.
    - Viruses: Herpes Simplex Virus type 2 or 1 (at 6-14 days).
    - Noninfectious Chemical: Silver nitrate toxicity (at 1-24 hours).
  - Children (Under 6 Years): Haemophilus influenzae and Streptococcus pneumoniae predominate (H. influenzae is most common under age 6). Adenovirus, HSV-1, Enterovirus, and primary varicella (chickenpox).
  - Children (Over 6 Years) and Adults: Staphylococcus aureus becomes the predominant bacterial cause, alongside H. influenzae. Adenovirus is the major viral cause. Sexually transmitted pathogens in sexually active adolescents and adults include Chlamydia trachomatis (adult inclusion conjunctivitis, 1 in 300 genital chlamydia cases) and Neisseria gonorrhoeae (hyperacute purulent conjunctivitis).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Zoonotic & Occupational Agents: Newcastle disease virus (poultry workers, veterinarians, lab techs), Avian Influenza virus serotype H7N7, Bacillus anthracis (cutaneous anthrax), Francisella tularensis, Bartonella henselae (Cat-Scratch Disease), Brucella species.
  - Mycobacteria: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Mycobacterium chelonae, Mycobacterium fortuitum.
  - Fungi: Candida albicans and non-albicans Candida species, Blastomyces dermatitidis, Sporothrix schenckii, Rhinosporidium seeberi (fleshy pedunculated conjunctival mass), Microsporidia (Vittaforma corneae, Encephalitozoon cuniculi, Anncaliia algerae; often seen in HIV/AIDS or rugby players exposed to mud).
  - Parasites & Ectoparasites: Phthirus pubis (crab lice on eyelashes), Demodex folliculorum / Demodex brevis (mites in lash follicles/meibomian glands causing cylindrical dandruff), Loa loa, Cryptosporidium, Trypanosoma brucei (tsetse fly transmission causing periorbital edema and preauricular adenopathy), Leishmania species, fly larvae (Oestrus ovis / ophthalmomyiasis).
  - Bioterrorism / Vaccine Reactions: Variola virus (smallpox) and Vaccinia virus (ocular vaccinia following smallpox vaccination or contact with military vaccinees).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Transmission Pathways: Exogenous direct contact with infected conjunctival secretions or respiratory droplets/fomites (adenovirus, bacterial pathogens); autoinoculation or hand-to-eye transfer from genital mucosal infections (C. trachomatis serotypes D-K, N. gonorrhoeae); vertical intrapartum transmission during passage through an infected birth canal (neonatal chlamydial, gonococcal, or herpetic conjunctivitis); direct vector/ectoparasite infestation or contaminated soil/water exposure (Acanthamoeba, microsporidia, Newcastle disease).
  - Tissue Localization & Epithelial Adherence: Microorganisms attach to conjunctival epithelial cells via surface adhesins, pili (N. gonorrhoeae), or viral receptor binding (adenovirus fiber protein binding to CAR/CD46; HSV entry glycoproteins). P. aeruginosa and Neisseria species produce extracellular enzymes and virulence factors capable of penetrating intact corneal and conjunctival epithelium within 24 hours.
  - Hypersensitivity & Immune Complex Mechanisms: Staphylococcal exotoxins incite an immune-mediated hypersensitivity reaction on the cornea and conjunctiva, leading to phlyctenule formation or marginal corneal ulceration. Repeated reinfections with C. trachomatis serotypes A-C trigger chronic cell-mediated delayed hypersensitivity reactions, producing conjunctival scarring, cicatricial entropion, and trichiasis in trachoma.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Vascular Congestion & Chemosis: Dilatation and engorgement of the rich subepithelial conjunctival vessel network cause marked conjunctival hyperemia (greatest at the periphery, fading toward the limbus) and fluid extravasation into the loose subepithelial stroma (chemosis).
  - Inflammatory Cell Infiltration & Secretion: Polymorphonuclear neutrophils predominate in bacterial infections, generating purulent or mucopurulent exudates containing fibrin, mucin, and sloughed epithelial cells. Lymphocytes and monocytes predominate in viral infections, forming subepithelial lymphoid follicles with germinal centers. Eosinophils and mast cell degranulation (histamine release) drive severe itching in allergic conjunctivitis and vernal keratoconjunctivitis.
  - Membranous Coagulum & Tissue Cicatrization: In severe exudative inflammation (EKC, C. diphtheriae, S. pyogenes, N. gonorrhoeae), proteinaceous exudate and fibrin form a yellowish-white pseudomembrane over the palpebral conjunctiva. If granulation tissue intertwines with the conjunctival stroma, a true membrane forms; peeling a true membrane tears delicate capillaries, causing active bleeding. Chronic inflammation damages goblet cells and lacrimal ductules, causing dry eye syndrome, conjunctival cicatrization, symblepharon (adhesion between palpebral and bulbar conjunctiva), and corneal scarring.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Ocular Discomfort & Irritation: Irritation, burning, or foreign-body sensation. True severe eye pain is UNCOMMON in uncomplicated conjunctivitis; presence of significant pain indicates corneal involvement (keratitis), eyelid ulceration (HSV, smallpox, vaccinia), or intraocular inflammation (endophthalmitis, uveitis, glaucoma).
  - Secretions & Discharge: Increased ocular tearing or discharge causing "mattering" and adherence / "sticking together" of the eyelids upon waking in the morning. Discharge character varies by etiology: watery/serous in viral and chemical; mucopurulent in typical bacterial; profuse, thick, yellowish-green purulent in hyperacute gonococcal; ropy/mucoid in allergic/vernal.
  - Pruritus / Itching: Variable burning or itching; severe, intense itching is the hallmark of allergic and toxic conjunctivitis and is less common in pure bacterial infection.
  - Visual Acuity Status: Visual acuity is typically normal or only mildly reduced due to tear film/discharge blurriness, clearing with blinks or topical lubrication. Significant vision loss indicates corneal ulceration, keratitis, or uveitis.
  - Photophobia: Mild light sensitivity may occur; prominent photophobia suggests corneal involvement (keratoconjunctivitis).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Conjunctival Hyperemia & Injection: Diffuse, bright red palpebral and bulbar conjunctival injection, most intense near the peripheral fornices and decreasing toward the limbus. Subconjunctival petechiae, saccular aneurysms, or intraconjunctival hemorrhages may be present (prominent in coxsackievirus A24, enterovirus 70, EKC).
  - Conjunctival Papillae: Elevated mounds of conjunctival epithelium with a central red vascular dot (dilated capillary core), seen on palpebral conjunctiva in bacterial infections, rosacea, and contact lens wear. Giant cobblestone papillae (1-8 mm diameter) on the superior tarsal conjunctiva occur in vernal keratoconjunctivitis.
  - Conjunctival Follicles: Translucent, avascular, round white or pale elevations (0.5 to 2.0 mm) with blood vessels climbing up their sides, located in the palpebral conjunctiva and fornices (hallmark of adenoviral, chlamydial, herpetic, and toxic conjunctivitis).
  - Lymphadenopathy: Tender or nontender enlargement of preauricular or submandibular lymph nodes. Preauricular adenopathy is a KEY feature distinguishing viral (adenovirus, HSV), chlamydial (adult inclusion conjunctivitis, LGV), and hyperacute gonococcal conjunctivitis from typical acute bacterial conjunctivitis (where preauricular adenopathy is ABSENT).
  - Periocular & Skin Findings: Lid edema, vesicular/pustular skin lesions on lid margins (HSV, VZV, vaccinia), molluscum contagiosum umbilicated nodules on lid margins, facial flushing/telangiectasias (ocular rosacea), cylindrical dandruff on eyelashes (Demodex mites), crab lice/nits on lashes (Phthirus pubis).
  - Specific Syndromic Signs:
    - Pharyngoconjunctival Fever (PCF): Triad of fever, pharyngitis, and follicular conjunctivitis with preauricular adenopathy in ~90% of cases.
    - Epidemic Keratoconjunctivitis (EKC): Mixed follicular/papillary reaction, watery discharge, severe chemosis, subconjunctival hemorrhages, conjunctival membranes/pseudomembranes in one-third, and subepithelial corneal infiltrates in 20-50%.
    - Trachoma (WHO Simplified Grading System): Trachomatous inflammation-follicular (TF: >= 5 follicles >= 0.5 mm on upper tarsal conjunctiva); Trachomatous inflammation-intense (TI: inflammatory thickening obscuring > 50% of deep tarsal vessels); Trachomatous conjunctival scarring (TS: visible tarsal scars, Arlt line); Trachomatous trichiasis (TT: >= 1 eyelash rubbing eyeball); Corneal opacity (CO: opacity blurring pupil margin). Superior corneal pannus and Herbert pits (depressions at limbus from cleared limbal follicles) are classic.
    - Parinaud Oculoglandular Syndrome: Unilateral granulomatous conjunctival nodule/ulcer with massive ipsilateral preauricular or submandibular lymphadenopathy and fever.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Preauricular lymphadenopathy with acute follicular conjunctivitis and watery discharge (pathognomonic clinical presentation of viral / adenoviral or chlamydial inclusion conjunctivitis).
  - Copious, profuse, thick yellowish-green purulent discharge reaccumulating immediately after wiping (pathognomonic feature of hyperacute N. gonorrhoeae conjunctivitis).
  - Cobblestone giant papillae on upper tarsal conjunctiva with ropy mucoid discharge and Horner-Trantas dots at the limbus (pathognomonic of vernal keratoconjunctivitis).
  - Herbert pits (cicatricial limbal depressions) and Arlt line (horizontal tarsal conjunctival scar) in trachoma.
  - Cylindrical dandruff / sleeves around the base of eyelashes (pathognomonic of Demodex blepharoconjunctivitis).

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Systemic Laboratory Testing: Routine blood work (CBC, ESR, CRP) is NOT required or indicated for uncomplicated cases of acute conjunctivitis.
  - Specific Systemic Workup Indications: Indicated in neonates (to evaluate for systemic gonococcal bacteremia, meningitis, or chlamydial pneumonitis), suspected systemic diseases (Sweet syndrome / acute febrile neutrophilic dermatosis showing leukocytosis with neutrophilia, Sarcoidosis, Syphilis with RPR/TPPA, Adult Reactive Arthritis / HLA-B27, Cat-Scratch Disease serology for B. henselae).
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Diagnostic Imaging: Imaging is generally NOT indicated for routine microbial conjunctivitis.
  - Clinical Indications: CT or MRI of the orbit/brain is indicated if preseptal or orbital cellulitis, dacryoadenitis, dacryocystitis, cavernous sinus thrombosis, or orbital abscess is suspected. Slit-lamp biomicroscopy and in vivo confocal microscopy allow detailed ocular surface visualization.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Indications for Microbiological Testing: Routine laboratory testing is unnecessary for typical uncomplicated mild conjunctivitis. Mandatory testing is required in:
    1. Neonates in the first month of life (ophthalmia neonatorum).
    2. Suspected hyperacute purulent conjunctivitis (N. gonorrhoeae).
    3. Chronic or recurrent conjunctivitis unresponsive to empiric therapy.
    4. Suspected Chlamydia, HSV, or Vaccinia infections.
    5. Contact lens wearers with corneal involvement.
    6. Cases with true membrane or pseudomembrane formation or corneal ulceration.
  - Specimen Collection: Conjunctival scraping or calcium alginate / Dacron swabs collected from the lower palpebral conjunctiva.
  - Diagnostic Stains & Cytology:
    - Gram Stain: Reveals numerous polymorphonuclear neutrophils along with gram-positive cocci in clusters (Staphylococcus), gram-positive diplococci (S. pneumoniae), gram-negative coccobacilli (H. influenzae), or intracellular gram-negative kidney-bean diplococci (N. gonorrhoeae).
    - Giemsa Stain: Identifies inflammatory cell predominant type (neutrophils in bacterial; lymphocytes/monocytes in viral; eosinophils in allergic); multinucleated giant cells and Cowdry A eosinophilic intranuclear inclusion bodies in HSV/VZV; basophilic intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies (Halberstaedter-Prowazek bodies) near the nucleus of epithelial cells in C. trachomatis.
    - Modified Trichrome / Calcofluor White: Identifies microsporidial spores or fungal hyphae.
  - Molecular Testing (PCR & NAATs):
    - Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests (NAATs / PCR): Highly sensitive and specific gold standard method for detecting C. trachomatis from ocular swabs (Aptima assay). Real-time PCR for HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, and Adenovirus.
    - Point-of-Care Antigen Tests: Rapid antigen immunoassay kits (RPS Adeno Detector / AdenoPlus) detecting adenoviral hexon protein from conjunctival swabs in 10 minutes in office settings.
  - Culture Media: Blood agar and chocolate agar (incubated in 5% CO2 for Neisseria and Haemophilus), Thayer-Martin media for N. gonorrhoeae, Sabouraud dextrose agar for fungi, viral transport media / shell vial cell culture for viruses.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - No Universal Single Test: Depends on suspected etiology:
    - Chlamydia trachomatis: Nucleic Acid Amplification Testing (NAAT / PCR) of conjunctival scrapings.
    - Neisseria gonorrhoeae: Gram stain showing intracellular gram-negative diplococci combined with culture on Thayer-Martin media or NAAT.
    - Adenovirus / Viral: Real-time PCR assay of conjunctival swabs (or viral isolation in cell culture).

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Infection Control & Hygiene Measures: Strict hand hygiene, frequent handwashing, avoiding touching/rubbing eyes, using fresh towels/washcloths, avoiding sharing pillows or eye makeup, and staying out of school/work while actively infectious (typically 10 to 14 days in adenoviral EKC).
  - Supportive Symptomatic Care: Cold compresses over eyelids, preservative-free artificial tears, and topical OTC decongestants/antihistamines for viral and allergic conjunctivitis. Frequent eye lavage / saline rinsing to clear copious purulent secretions. Discontinuation of contact lens wear immediately. Avoid eye patching.
  - Avoidance of Inappropriate Topicals: Avoid topical corticosteroids in undiagnosed or viral conjunctivitis (can exacerbate HSV, prolong viral shedding, increase IOP, or cause corneal melting). Avoid empiric topical antibiotics in clear viral conjunctivitis.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Acute Bacterial Conjunctivitis (Mild to Moderate - Outpatient):
    - Broad-spectrum topical ophthalmic drops applied 4 times daily (or every 3 hours while awake) for 7 to 10 days:
      - Trimethoprim 0.1% - Polymyxin B 10,000 U/mL ophthalmic solution.
      - Fluoroquinolone drops: Ciprofloxacin 0.3%, Levofloxacin 0.5%, or Moxifloxacin 0.5% (Moxifloxacin approved down to age 3 years; lowest resistance rates across ages).
      - Sulfacetamide 10% ophthalmic solution.
      - Erythromycin 0.5% ophthalmic ointment (preferred in infants < 1 year due to low toxicity; applied 4 times daily).
      - Topical Azithromycin 1.5% ophthalmic solution (effective 3-day course).
  - Adult Inclusion Conjunctivitis (Chlamydia trachomatis):
    - Systemic Therapy Required (topical alone is ineffective):
      - Oral Azithromycin 1 g single oral dose (or 1 g once weekly for 2-3 weeks in chronic cases).
      - OR Oral Doxycycline 100 mg PO twice daily for 21 days (3 weeks).
      - OR Oral Erythromycin 500 mg PO 4 times daily for 21 days (preferred in pregnancy/lactation and children < 8 years).
      - MANDATORY: Concurrent evaluation and systemic treatment of all sexual partners.
  - Hyperacute Gonococcal Conjunctivitis (N. gonorrhoeae):
    - Medical Emergency requiring immediate hospitalization and systemic therapy:
      - Ceftriaxone 1 g intramuscular (IM) or intravenous (IV) single dose COMBINED with oral Azithromycin 1 g single dose (to cover co-existing Chlamydia).
      - Hourly saline conjunctival fornix irrigation.
      - Add topical atropine if corneal involvement is present.
  - Neonatal Conjunctivitis (Ophthalmia Neonatorum):
    - Neonatal Gonococcal (24-48h): Ceftriaxone 25 to 50 mg/kg IV or IM single dose (max 125 mg) PLUS frequent saline irrigation. Treat mother and partner.
    - Neonatal Chlamydial (5-14d): Oral Erythromycin ethylsuccinate suspension 50 mg/kg/day divided into 4 doses daily for 14 days (2 weeks); a second course if incomplete resolution. Topical erythromycin ointment as adjunct.
    - Neonatal Gram-Positive (2-5d): Topical Bacitracin or Erythromycin ointment for 2 weeks.
    - Neonatal Pseudomonas (5-18d): Topical Gentamicin, Tobramycin, or Ciprofloxacin drops 4 times daily for 2 weeks PLUS systemic parenteral antipseudomonal antibiotics.
    - Neonatal HSV (6-14d): Intravenous Acyclovir 60 mg/kg/day divided q8h for 14 to 21 days PLUS topical Vidarabine 3% ointment or Trifluridine drops.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Trachoma Mass Treatment (WHO SAFE Strategy):
    - Single oral dose of Azithromycin (20 mg/kg, max 1 g) or topical Tetracycline 1% eye ointment twice daily for 5 days each month for 6 months in endemic communities.
  - Vaccinia Conjunctivitis:
    - Mild/Moderate: Topical Trifluridine 1% drops 9 times daily for 2 weeks (adults) or Vidarabine 3% ointment 2-5 times daily for 2 weeks (children), plus single-dose Vaccinia Immune Globulin (VIG) 100 mg/kg IM or 6000 U/kg IV for moderate disease.
    - Severe: Single-dose VIG 100 mg/kg IM or 6000 U/kg IV, repeated in 48 hours if no improvement.
  - Fungal & Parasitic Conjunctivitis:
    - Microsporidial Keratoconjunctivitis: Oral Albendazole 400 mg PO twice daily PLUS topical Fumagillin (Fumidil B formulation) drops.
    - Rhinosporidiosis: Surgical excision of pedunculated conjunctival mass.
    - Phthiriasis / Demodex: Eyelid scrubs with Tea Tree Oil (Melaleuca alternifolia); mechanical removal of lice/nits; bland petrolatum ointment to smother lice.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Surgical Procedures:
    - Trachoma Trichiasis Surgery: Bilateral tarsal rotation surgical procedure to correct in-turned eyelashes (trichiasis) and prevent corneal scarring.
    - Marsupialization of Tarsoconjunctival Crypts: Performed in refractory chronic mucopurulent conjunctivitis harboring crypt bacterial reservoirs.
    - Excision of Lesions: Surgical excision of conjunctival molluscum contagiosum nodules, papillomas, or Rhinosporidium seeberi pedunculated masses.
    - Debridement & Membrane Removal: Gentle removal of conjunctival pseudomembranes and membranes in severe EKC or streptococcal/diphtheritic conjunctivitis to relieve discomfort and prevent symblepharon formation.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Secondary Corneal Involvement (Keratoconjunctivitis): Extension of inflammation to the corneal epithelium and stroma (punctate epithelial keratitis, subepithelial infiltrates, corneal ulceration).
  - Rapid Corneal Perforation & Endophthalmitis: Virulent organisms (N. gonorrhoeae, P. aeruginosa) can penetrate intact corneal epithelium in as little as 24 hours, causing rapidly progressive corneal melting, ulceration, perforation, and intraocular endophthalmitis, threatening permanent vision loss or loss of the eye.
  - Preseptal & Orbital Cellulitis: Extension of conjunctival infection into periocular lid structures or posterior orbital space.
  - Systemic Dissemination in Neonates: Disseminated gonococcal infection (arthritis, meningitis, sepsis), chlamydial infant pneumonitis or otitis media, or systemic HSV encephalitis.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Subepithelial Corneal Infiltrates & Glare: Persistent nummular subepithelial infiltrates in EKC (adenovirus 8, 19) lasting months to years, causing reduced visual acuity and disabling glare.
  - Conjunctival Cicatrization & Symblepharon: Chronic inflammation leads to tarsal conjunctival scarring, loss of goblet cells (severe dry eye syndrome / keratoconjunctivitis sicca), and symblepharon (adhesion of palpebral conjunctiva to bulbar conjunctiva or globe).
  - Trachomatous Trichiasis & Irreversible Blindness: In-turning of eyelashes (trichiasis) rubbing against the cornea causes chronic mechanical abrasions, severe corneal opacification, and irreversible blindness (trachoma is the leading infectious cause of preventable blindness worldwide, affecting 84 million active cases and leaving 1.3 million blind).
  - Punctal Occlusion & Canalicular Stenosis: Scarring of the lacrimal drainage puncta or canaliculi leading to chronic epiphora (tearing).

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Pink eye" (common lay term for acute infectious conjunctivitis).
  - "Limbic sparing" and "Ciliary flush" (ciliary flush indicates deep corneal/anterior chamber inflammation, whereas peripheral conjunctival hyperemia spares the limbus in simple conjunctivitis).
  - "Cobblestone papillae" (giant papillae on upper tarsal conjunctiva in vernal conjunctivitis).
  - "Horner-Trantas dots" (white limbal dots composed of eosinophils in vernal conjunctivitis).
  - "Halberstaedter-Prowazek bodies" (basophilic intracytoplasmic inclusions in C. trachomatis conjunctivitis).
  - "Arlt line" (horizontal scar on tarsal conjunctiva) and "Herbert pits" (limbal depressions) in end-stage trachoma.
  - "Sulfur granules" (yellowish concretions of Actinomyces israelii expressed during canaliculitis).
  - "Cylindrical dandruff" (collarette sleeves around lashes in Demodex infestation).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Absence of Preauricular Lymphadenopathy in Bacterial Conjunctivitis: Unlike viral (adenovirus, HSV) and chlamydial inclusion conjunctivitis which routinely feature preauricular lymphadenopathy, acute bacterial conjunctivitis does NOT cause preauricular lymphadenopathy—WITH THE EXCEPTION of hyperacute Neisseria gonorrhoeae conjunctivitis, which presents with tender preauricular lymphadenopathy.
  - Inability of Neonate to Form Conjunctival Follicles: Neonates with Chlamydia trachomatis conjunctivitis DO NOT develop a follicular response because their immature immune system lacks lymphoid tissue in the conjunctival stroma until approximately 6 to 8 weeks of age; instead, they present with papillary hypertrophy and copious mucopurulent discharge.
  - Penicillin Penetration of Cornea vs. Neisseria / Pseudomonas Virulence: Most bacteria cannot penetrate intact corneal epithelium and require an epithelial defect; however, N. gonorrhoeae, N. meningitidis, C. diphtheriae, and P. aeruginosa possess enzymatic machinery that allows them to penetrate completely intact corneal epithelium in 24 hours.
  - Antibiotic Eye Drops Ineffectiveness for Chlamydial Inclusion Conjunctivitis: Topical antimicrobial drops are ineffective for adult inclusion conjunctivitis because the organism resides intracellularly in deep mucosal and extraocular reservoirs (genital tract); systemic oral antibiotics (azithromycin or doxycycline) are mandatory.
  - Non-Infectious Conjunctivitis Mimics: Sweet Syndrome (acute febrile neutrophilic dermatosis) presents with conjunctivitis, episcleritis, and fever alongside neutrophilic skin plaques; Stevens-Johnson syndrome causes severe membranous conjunctivitis and cicatrization without primary microbial ocular infection.
