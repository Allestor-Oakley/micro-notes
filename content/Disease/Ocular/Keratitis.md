# Keratitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Microbial keratitis is a potentially vision-threatening infectious corneal inflammatory event caused by bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites. The cornea is an avascular, transparent structure approximately 0.5 mm thick centrally, consisting of a 5- to 7-cell-thick epithelium overlying Bowman's membrane, stroma (interstitium), Descemet's membrane, and endothelium. The cornea and tear film provide 65% to 75% of the refractive power of the eye, so keratitis characteristically causes decreased vision. Infection and inflammatory infiltration reduce corneal transparency, leading to pain, corneal ulceration, stromal melting (keratolysis), corneal perforation, and potential secondary endophthalmitis or loss of the eye.
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Etiologic / Microbiologic Categories:
     - Bacterial Keratitis: Accounts for 65% to 90% of ulcerative keratitis in developed nations (up to 80% to 90% in UK/temperate regions); includes Gram-positive (Staphylococcus spp., Streptococcus spp.) and Gram-negative (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacteriaceae) infections.
     - Viral Keratitis: Includes Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1, HSV-2; epithelial dendritic/geographic keratitis, stromal/necrotizing keratitis, neurotrophic/metaherpetic keratopathy), Varicella-Zoster Virus (HZO / herpes zoster ophthalmicus, pseudodendritic/dendritiform keratitis), Adenovirus (Epidemic Keratoconjunctivitis / EKC, subepithelial infiltrates), Vaccinia virus, Rubeola/Measles, Mumps, EBV, Enterovirus 70, Coxsackievirus.
     - Fungal Keratitis (Keratomycosis): Caused by Molds / Filamentous fungi (Fusarium spp., Aspergillus spp., Curvularia, Paecilomyces, Alternaria) or Yeasts (Candida albicans, Candida spp.); more common in warm, humid climates and agricultural settings.
     - Parasitic & Microsporidial Keratitis: Includes Acanthamoeba keratitis (free-living amebic infection associated with contact lenses), Onchocerciasis ("river blindness" caused by Onchocerca volvulus microfilariae leading to sclerosing keratitis), Leishmaniasis, Microsporidial keratitis (Vittaforma corneae, Encephalitozoon cuniculi), and African trypanosomiasis.
     - Chlamydial & Mycobacterial Keratitis: Includes Trachomatous keratitis (Chlamydia trachomatis serotypes A-C, causing superior corneal pannus and cicatricial entropion/trichiasis), Nontuberculous Mycobacterial (NTM) keratitis (M. chelonae, M. fortuitum, M. abscessus, M. gordonae following LASIK or trauma), and Leprous keratitis (Mycobacterium leprae).
  2. Anatomical & Depth-Based Categories:
     - Epithelial Keratitis: Inflammation confined to the corneal epithelium (e.g., initial HSV dendritic ulcers, superficial punctate keratitis in early adenoviral or vaccinia infection).
     - Stromal / Interstitial Keratitis (IK): Inflammation involving the corneal stroma without primary epithelial ulceration (e.g., Syphilitic IK, Lyme keratitis, HSV stromal keratitis, Vaccinia disciform keratitis).
     - Ulcerative Keratitis / Corneal Ulcer: Sharply demarcated epithelial defect with suppurative or nonsuppurative stromal infiltration and breakdown (e.g., P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, fungal ulcers).
     - Neurotrophic / Metaherpetic Keratopathy: Trophic epithelial breakdown with heaped-up gray borders occurring in an anesthetic cornea following recurrent HSV/VZV infections.
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Contact Lens Wear: Single most common risk factor in developed countries (accounts for >50% to 65% of cases in the US since the 1980s). Incidence ranges from 0.4 to 5.2 per 10,000 person-years for rigid gas-permeable and soft daily lenses, rising to >20 per 10,000 person-years for overnight/extended-wear lenses. Specific risk factors include poor storage case hygiene (OR 6.4), infrequent case replacement (OR 5.4), specific multipurpose solutions (OR 7.2), occasional overnight use (OR 6.5), high socioeconomic status (OR 4.1), and smoking (OR 3.7). Outbreaks of Acanthamoeba and Fusarium keratitis have been linked to specific recalled multipurpose contact lens solutions.
  2. Ocular Trauma & Environmental Exposures: Nonsurgical ocular trauma (vegetable or soil contamination) accounts for 48% to 65% of corneal ulcers in developing nations (leading risk factor globally) and 15% to 27% in the US.
  3. Ocular Surface Disease & Anatomic Alterations: Preexisting ocular surface breakdown (blepharitis, severe dry eye / Sjögren syndrome, neurotrophic cornea, chemical burns, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, ocular cicatricial pemphigoid, exposure keratopathy in Bell palsy or Graves exophthalmopathy), previous herpetic keratitis, and bullous keratopathy.
  4. Surgical Procedures: Refractive surgery (LASIK, PRK; predisposing to atypical NTM like M. chelonae, Nocardia, or fungal keratitis under the flap), corneal transplantation (penetrating/lamellar keratoplasty), and Boston type I keratoprosthesis implantation.
  5. Systemic Factors & Immunosuppression: Diabetes mellitus, systemic immunodeficiency (HIV/AIDS predisposing to microsporidia, MAC, or severe fungal keratitis), malnutrition and Vitamin A deficiency (measles keratitis leading to keratomalacia and perforation), alcoholism (Moraxella keratitis), and topical corticosteroid use (potentiates fungal, herpetic, and bacterial invasion).
  6. Disease Burden & Healthcare Impact: In the US, keratitis causes 930,000 clinic visits and 58,000 emergency department visits annually ($175 million direct cost, 250,000 clinician hours). Globally, corneal opacities from infectious keratitis cause 10% of avoidable visual impairment in developing countries.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Bacterial Pathogens (80% of ulcerative keratitis worldwide; 82% to 83% of bacterial isolates):
    - Gram-Negative Bacilli: Pseudomonas aeruginosa (most common Gram-negative isolate overall and predominant cause in contact lens wearers), Burkholderia spp., Proteus mirabilis, Serratia marcescens, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter, Morganella morganii, Aeromonas hydrophila.
    - Gram-Positive Cocci: Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis (and other coagulase-negative staphylococci), Streptococcus pneumoniae (historically predominant, still common), Streptococcus pyogenes, Viridans streptococci, Enterococcus faecalis, Peptostreptococcus spp.
    - Gram-Negative Coccobacilli & Neisseria: Moraxella catarrhalis, Moraxella lacunata, Moraxella nonliquefaciens, Neisseria gonorrhoeae (can penetrate intact epithelium), Acinetobacter calcoaceticus.
  - Viral Pathogens: Herpes Simplex Virus type 1 (HSV-1; leading cause of infectious corneal blindness in developed nations, 1.5 million global cases annually), Herpes Simplex Virus type 2 (HSV-2; 80% of neonatal cases), Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV / HZO), Adenovirus (serotypes 8, 19 in EKC; serotypes 3, 4, 7 in PCF), Vaccinia virus, Rubeola (Measles), Mumps, EBV, Enterovirus 70, Coxsackievirus A24.
  - Fungal Pathogens (16% of isolates in general series; up to 50% in tropical/developing regions):
    - Molds / Filamentous Fungi: Fusarium species (F. solani, F. oxysporum; predominant mold cause overall and in contact lens solution outbreaks), Aspergillus species (A. fumigatus, A. flavus), Acremonium, Alternaria, Curvularia, Bipolaris, Penicillium, Paecilomyces, Exophiala.
    - Yeasts: Candida albicans, non-albicans Candida species (C. glabrata, C. tropicalis).
  - Parasitic Pathogens (2% of isolates): Acanthamoeba species (A. castellanii, A. polyphaga; free-living amebae), Onchocerca volvulus, Leishmania species, Vittaforma corneae, Encephalitozoon cuniculi, Anncaliia algerae, Trypanosoma brucei.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Neonates (First 4 Weeks of Life): HSV-2 (acquired via vaginal delivery), Chlamydia trachomatis (serotypes D-K), Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa (causes rapid corneal melting/perforation and fatal sepsis in premature infants), S. aureus, Streptococcus species.
  - Pediatric Population (Children & Adolescents): Nonsurgical trauma is the primary predisposing cause. Pathogens include S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa (especially in contact lens users or underlying blepharitis/SJS), HSV-1 (60% infected by age 5), Measles virus (in malnourished/vitamin A-deficient children), and Acanthamoeba.
  - Adults & Elderly: Contact lens-related keratitis predominated by P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, Serratia marcescens, Acanthamoeba, and Fusarium. Surgical/LASIK-related keratitis predominated by atypical NTM (M. chelonae, M. fortuitum), Nocardia, and S. epidermidis. Ocular surface disease / compromised hosts predominated by S. aureus, C. albicans, Moraxella, and VZV.
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (NTM) & Actinomycetes: Mycobacterium chelonae (66% of post-LASIK mycobacterial cases), Mycobacterium fortuitum, Mycobacterium gordonae, Mycobacterium mucogenicum, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Mycobacterium leprae, Nocardia species (N. asteroides, N. brasiliensis), Corynebacterium matruchotii, Actinomyces israelii.
  - Opportunistic Fungi & Microsporidia: Microsporidia (Vittaforma corneae, Encephalitozoon cuniculi, Anncaliia algerae; causes keratitis in HIV/AIDS or rugby players exposed to mud), Scedosporium apiospermum, Paecilomyces (often misidentified in culture as Penicillium), Exophiala phaeomuriformis, Edenia gomezpompae, Nosema ocularum.
  - Fastidious Gram-Positive Bacilli: Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Listeria monocytogenes (both can invade intact corneal epithelium), Bacillus cereus (severe posttraumatic keratitis), Brevibacillus brevis, Clostridium perfringens, Clostridium tetani.
  - Unusual Gram-Negative & Spirochetal Pathogens: Raoultella planticola / Raoultella spp. (histamine-producing contact lens solution contamination), Delftia (Pseudomonas) acidovorans, Pseudomonas stutzeri, Bartonella henselae, Treponema pallidum, Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme interstitial keratitis).

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Epithelial Barrier Breach & Adherence: The intact corneal epithelium with tight desmosomal/hemidesmosomal junctions, along with tear film antimicrobial enzymes (lysozyme, lactoferrin) and mechanical eyelid blinking, normally prevents microbial entry. Microorganisms gain entry through an epithelial breach (contact lens abrasion, trauma, surgical flap, dry eye erosion).
  - Organisms Invading Intact Epithelium: A select group of virulent pathogens can adhere to and penetrate an intact, uninjured corneal epithelium: Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Listeria monocytogenes, and Shigella species.
  - Bacterial Toxin Production & Proteolysis: Attached bacteria (particularly P. aeruginosa) elaborate potent extracellular proteolytic enzymes (elastase, alkaline protease, exotoxin A) that actively lyse corneal collagen, proteoglycans, and cell membranes, causing rapid tissue liquefaction.
  - Viral Latency & Reactivation: HSV-1 and VZV establish lifelong latency in the trigeminal ganglion (ciliary/ophthalmic branch CN V1) following primary mucocutaneous infection. Reactivation sends infectious virions anterograde down nerve axons to the corneal epithelium and stroma, causing recurrent epithelial or immune-mediated stromal keratitis.
  - Parasitic & Fungal Invasion: Acanthamoeba trophozoites adhere to mannosed corneal glycoproteins, releasing matrix metalloproteinases and cytolytic toxins that cause radial perineuritis and stromal ring infiltrates; under adverse conditions, trophozoites encyst in deep stroma, resisting standard antimicrobials. Molds (Fusarium, Aspergillus) extend branching hyphae parallel and perpendicular to corneal lamellae, penetrating Descemet's membrane into the anterior chamber.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Leukocyte Migration & Infiltrate Formation: Bacterial antigens trigger chemotactic release, causing polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) to migrate rapidly from limbal blood vessels and tears into the avascular corneal stroma. Accumulations of replicating microbes and PMNs form opacified corneal "infiltrates."
  - Stromal Melting (Keratolysis): PMNs release free radicals, collagenases, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-9) intended to kill microbes, which inadvertently degrades the native corneal collagen matrix, leading to rapid stromal thinning, melting, and perforation in as little as 24 hours (especially with P. aeruginosa or B. cereus).
  - Corneal Edema & Loss of Transparency: Inflammatory fluid and PMNs disrupt the precise dehydration state maintained by corneal endothelial pumps, resulting in stromal edema, Descemet's membrane folds, and severe loss of corneal clarity.
  - Corneal Neovascularization: Chronic or severe acute stromal inflammation stimulates vascular endothelial growth factors, causing capillary sprouting from limbal arcades into the stroma. Regressed vessels persist indefinitely as empty "ghost vessels," providing a permanent pathway for recurrent inflammatory cells and compromising future corneal graft survival.
  - Anterior Segment Inflammatory Cascade: Microbial toxins and cytokines diffuse into the anterior chamber, inciting iris/ciliary body inflammation (iridocyclitis) with protein flare, white blood cells (cells), and layering of purulent exudate (hypopyon).

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Severe, excruciating eye pain and discomfort (cornea is one of the most densely innervated tissues in the body via trigeminal nerve endings; eyelid movement over the lesion worsens pain). Note: Reduced pain occurs in anesthetic/neurotrophic corneas (recurrent HSV/VZV, leprosy).
  - Marked photophobia (light sensitivity) and blepharospasm (involuntary eyelid squeezing).
  - Increased tearing (lacrimation) and variable discharge (mucopurulent in severe bacterial; watery/serous in viral, fungal, and amebic).
  - Decreased visual acuity (vision loss ranges from mild blurriness to severe reduction depending on lesion size and location on the visual axis).
  - Relevant history: Contact lens wear (overnight use, hygiene habits, solution brand), ocular trauma (vegetable/soil exposure), recent LASIK/refractive surgery, prior herpetic eye disease, or topical steroid use.
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Conjunctival & Limbal Injection: Diffuse bulbar conjunctival hyperemia with prominent ciliary flush (engorgement of deep episcleral vessels surrounding the limbus).
  - Corneal Infiltrate: Focal, multifocal, or diffuse gray-white or yellowish opacification within the corneal stroma due to leukocyte and microbial aggregation. Bacterial infiltrates are sharply demarcated and suppurative; mold infiltrates (Fusarium, Aspergillus) display gray-white, dry-appearing, feathery/filamentous margins with satellite lesions; Candida displays oval epithelial ulceration with densely focal suppuration.
  - Epithelial Defect / Corneal Ulcer: Area of epithelial loss overlying the infiltrate, pooling fluorescein dye under cobalt blue light. Slow-growing organisms (NTM, anaerobes) or deep stromal infections may present with an intact epithelium.
  - Stromal Melting & Thinning: Keratolysis with tissue loss, potentially progressing to Descemetocoele (herniation of intact Descemet's membrane) or frank corneal perforation.
  - Corneal Edema & Bullae: Microcystic or diffuse stromal edema with Descemet's folds; fluid accumulation can form painful bullae that rupture.
  - Anterior Chamber Reaction & Hypopyon: Fibrin ("flare"), white blood cells ("cells"), and layering of purulent exudate in the inferior anterior chamber (hypopyon). Posterior or peripheral anterior synechiae and elevated intraocular pressure (secondary glaucoma).
  - Specific Etiologic Signs:
    - HSV Epithelial Keratitis: Classic linear, branching "dendritic ulcer" with terminal bulbs and swollen, virus-laden epithelial edges that stain with fluorescein (ulcer bed) and rose bengal (swollen edges); expands into a "geographic ulcer" with corticosteroid use.
    - Neurotrophic / Metaherpetic Ulcer: Ovoid or dendritiform epithelial defect with gray, thickened, heaped-up borders in an anesthetic cornea.
    - Acanthamoeba Keratitis: Early diffuse epithelial/dendritiform lesions, severe pain out of proportion to exam, radial perineuritis / perineural infiltrates along enlarged corneal nerves, progressing to a central or paracentral stromal "ring infiltrate."
    - Epidemic Keratoconjunctivitis (Adenovirus): Superficial punctate keratitis evolving into deep epithelial keratitis and late subepithelial infiltrates (SEIs; coin-shaped / nummular opacities in anterior stroma).
    - Trachoma: Superior corneal pannus (neovascularization), epithelial keratitis, Herbert pits (depressions at limbus from cleared limbal follicles), Arlt line (horizontal tarsal scar), cicatricial entropion, and trichiasis.
    - Syphilitic Interstitial Keratitis (IK): Deep stromal haze, dense deep neovascularization ("salmon patches" of central cornea), followed by vessel regression leaving "ghost vessels."
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Branching dendritic ulcer with terminal bulbs staining centrally with fluorescein and peripherally with rose bengal (pathognomonic of active HSV epithelial keratitis).
  - Radial perineuritis / perineural corneal nerve infiltrates accompanied by excruciating pain out of proportion to clinical signs (pathognomonic of early Acanthamoeba keratitis).
  - Dry, gray-white stromal infiltrate with feathery/filamentous margins and surrounding satellite lesions (pathognomonic sign of filamentous fungal / Fusarium / Aspergillus keratomycosis).
  - Ring-shaped stromal infiltrate ("ring infiltrate") surrounding a central lesion (classic sign of P. aeruginosa, Acanthamoeba, fungal, or vaccinia keratitis).
  - Salmon patches (dense central deep stromal neovascularization) leaving permanent "ghost vessels" upon resolution (pathognomonic of syphilitic interstitial keratitis).

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Systemic Laboratory Testing: Routine systemic blood work (CBC, ESR, CRP) is NOT indicated for localized microbial keratitis.
  - Specific Systemic Indications: Indicated in suspected syphilitic interstitial keratitis (RPR / VDRL, FTA-ABS, TP-PA), Lyme keratitis (Borrelia serology/Western blot), vasculitic/rheumatoid corneal melting (ANA, ANCA, RF), or systemic mycobacterial/leprosy evaluation.
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - Slit-Lamp Biomicroscopy: Primary essential bedside tool to measure infiltrate and epithelial defect dimensions (length x width in mm), assess stromal depth/thinning, document hypopyon height, evaluate anterior chamber cells/flare, and perform fluorescein staining.
  - In Vivo Confocal Microscopy (IVCM): Non-invasive optical imaging providing high-resolution real-time optical sectioning of corneal layers; highly sensitive for detecting double-walled Acanthamoeba cysts, amebic trophozoites, and branching fungal hyphae directly in deep stroma without biopsy.
  - Anterior Segment Optical Coherence Tomography (AS-OCT): Quantifies corneal thickness, depth of stromal infiltration, stromal melting, and Descemetocoele formation.
  - Ocular B-Scan Ultrasonography: Indicated if dense corneal opacification or hypopyon obscures the view of the posterior segment to rule out secondary endophthalmitis or retinal detachment.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Corneal Scrapings & Specimen Collection: Performed under topical anesthesia (e.g., proparacaine, tetracaine) using a sterile Kimura platinum spatula, surgical blade (#15 or #11), or trypticase soy broth-dipped calcium alginate swab. Scrapings taken from the leading edge and base of the corneal ulcer.
  - Diagnostic Stains & Microscopy:
    - Gram Stain: Immediate identification of Gram-positive cocci (clusters/chains), Gram-negative bacilli (P. aeruginosa), or Gram-negative coccobacilli. Note: Melanin pigment granules from iris/RPE can mimic Gram-positive cocci on Gram stain (distinguished by copper refractility under fine focus modulation).
    - Giemsa Stain: Evaluates inflammatory cell types and identifies chlamydial intracytoplasmic inclusions or fungal elements.
    - Calcofluor White / KOH Wet Mount: Fluorescent stain binding to chitin and cellulose in fungal cell walls and Acanthamoeba cyst walls (highly sensitive).
    - Acid-Fast / Kinyoun Stain: Identifies atypical Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (M. chelonae) and Nocardia species (weakly acid-fast).
  - Culture Media Inoculation: Direct "C-streak" inoculation onto:
    - Blood Agar & Chocolate Agar (incubated at 35-37°C for aerobes, Neisseria, Haemophilus).
    - Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (incubated at 25-30°C without cycloheximide for fungi/molds).
    - Thioglycollate Broth (for anaerobes and fastidious bacteria).
    - Non-Nutrient Agar with E. coli Overlay: Specific medium for culturing Acanthamoeba trophozoites (produces track marks).
    - Lowenstein-Jensen Media: For mycobacterial culture.
  - Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR): Rapid, highly sensitive gold standard for detecting HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, Adenovirus, Acanthamoeba DNA, and 16S/18S rRNA pan-bacterial/fungal sequencing.
  - Diagnostic Corneal Biopsy: Indicated when deep stromal infiltrates (fungal, Acanthamoeba, NTM) are unresponsive to therapy and scrapings remain negative. Performed via 1- to 2-mm trephine or shave biopsy.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - Microbiological Isolation and Identification via Culture and/or PCR from Direct Corneal Scrapings or Tissue Biopsy: Represents the definitive gold standard for establishing the specific etiologic pathogen and antimicrobial sensitivities in microbial keratitis.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Emergency Ophthalmology Referral: Immediate evaluation by an ophthalmologist within hours for all suspected corneal ulcers/infiltrates.
  - Topical Cycloplegics: Cyclopentolate 1%, Scopolamine 0.25%, or Atropine 1% drops applied 2 to 3 times daily to relieve severe ciliary body spasm, reduce pain/photophobia, and prevent posterior synechiae.
  - Temporary Bandage Soft Contact Lenses & Collagen Shields: Used selectively as a bandage to promote epithelial healing and relieve pain in non-infectious or healing neurotrophic ulcers under antibiotic coverage; CONTRAINDICATED in active, uncontrolled bacterial or fungal keratitis.
  - Pain Management: Systemic oral nonnarcotic or short-term narcotic analgesics (oxycodone, acetaminophen) for severe pain. ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION: Topical anesthetics (e.g., proparacaine, tetracaine) MUST NEVER be prescribed for home use or outpatient pain relief due to severe epitheliotoxicity, delayed wound healing, and rapid corneal stromal melting/perforation.
  - Systemic Collagenase Inhibitors: Oral Doxycycline (100 mg PO twice daily) inhibits matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-9) and neutrophil collagenases, reducing stromal melting and stabilizing corneal structure.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Bacterial Keratitis (Empiric Therapy):
    - Fortified Combination Topical Antibiotic Therapy (Preferred for severe, central, or large > 2 mm corneal ulcers):
      - Fortified Cefazolin 5% (50 mg/mL) or Fortified Vancomycin 2.5%-5% (25-50 mg/mL) drops (covers Gram-positive bacteria including MRSA) COMBINED WITH
      - Fortified Tobramycin 1.3%-1.5% (13-15 mg/mL) or Gentamicin 1.3%-1.5% drops (covers Gram-negative bacilli including P. aeruginosa).
      - Administration: Dosed heavily at loading frequency (every 5 to 15 minutes for the first 1-2 hours), then hourly (24 hours/day) for the first 24 to 48 hours, tapering according to clinical response over 10 to 14 days.
    - Topical Fourth-Generation Fluoroquinolone Monotherapy (Preferred for mild-to-moderate or peripheral corneal ulcers):
      - Moxifloxacin 0.5% (Vigamox) or Gatifloxacin 0.3%-0.5% (Zymaxid) ophthalmic drops administered hourly. (Fourth-generation fluoroquinolones feature dual C-8 structural modifications inhibiting both DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, lowering resistance rates).
  - Viral Keratitis (First-Line Regimens):
    - Active HSV Epithelial (Dendritic / Geographic) Keratitis:
      - Topical Trifluridine 1% (Viroptic) drops 9 times daily for 7 to 14 days (max 14 days due to epitheliotoxicity); OR
      - Topical Ganciclovir 0.15% (Zirgan) gel 5 times daily; OR
      - Oral Acyclovir 400 mg PO 5 times daily (or 800 mg PO 3 times daily) for 7 to 10 days; OR
      - Oral Valacyclovir 1000 mg PO twice daily for 7 to 10 days.
      - Gentle mechanical epithelial debridement with a sterile cotton swab removes virus-laden cells and immune antigens.
    - Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus (VZV Keratitis):
      - Oral Acyclovir 800 mg PO 5 times daily for 7 to 10 days; OR Oral Valacyclovir 1 g PO 3 times daily for 7 days; OR Oral Famciclovir 500 mg PO 3 times daily for 7 days.
  - Fungal Keratitis (First-Line Regimens):
    - Filamentous / Mold Keratitis (Fusarium, Aspergillus): Topical Natamycin 5% ophthalmic suspension (only FDA-approved topical antifungal) applied hourly.
    - Yeast Keratitis (Candida albicans): Topical Amphotericin B 0.15%-0.5% drops (pharmacy compounded) applied hourly.
  - Acanthamoeba Keratitis (First-Line Regimens):
    - Combination Topical Antiamebic Therapy (applied hourly day and night initially):
      - Biguanide: Polyhexamethylene biguanide (PHMB 0.02%) or Chlorhexidine gluconate (0.02%-0.06%) COMBINED WITH
      - Diamidine: Propamidine isethionate (Brolene 0.1%) or Hexamidine (0.1%).
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Alternative / Adjuvant Fungal Regimens: Topical Voriconazole 1% drops (compounded from IV formulation) PLUS Oral Voriconazole (200 mg PO twice daily) or Oral Itraconazole (200 mg PO daily) as adjuncts for deep or recalcitrant mold ulcers. Topical Chlorhexidine 0.05%-0.2% as an alternative in resource-limited settings.
  - Alternative Amebic Regimens: Oral and topical Miltefosine as salvage therapy for refractory Acanthamoeba keratitis.
  - Atypical NTM Keratitis (Post-LASIK M. chelonae / M. fortuitum): Topical Amikacin 2.5%-5%, topical Clarithromycin 1%, topical Linezolid, or topical fluoroquinolones for months.
  - Role of Adjunctive Topical Corticosteroids: Highly controversial. Corticosteroids (e.g., Prednisolone acetate 1%) suppress PMN inflammation and reduce corneal scarring/neovascularization, but can retard epithelial healing, promote infection relapse, increase IOP, or precipitate corneal melting/perforation. SCUT trial findings: Added ONLY after 2 to 3 days of effective topical antibiotic coverage when culture is confirmed non-fungal/non-amebic, and STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED in active fungal, Acanthamoeba, or HSV epithelial keratitis.
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Corneal Epithelial Debridement: Gentle cotton-swab debridement of infected dendritic epithelium in HSV keratitis to reduce viral load and immune antigens.
  - Photoactivated Chromophore Corneal Cross-Linking (PACK-CXL / CXL): Application of riboflavin (Vitamin B2) drops followed by UVA irradiation; generates reactive oxygen species that sterilize the cornea, induce intra-pathogen DNA cross-links, and harden stromal collagen against matrix metalloproteinase digestion.
  - Corneal Tissue Adhesive & Bandage Contact Lens: Application of cyanoacrylate tissue adhesive (e.g., Dermabond) or fibrin glue over focal areas of severe stromal thinning or microperforation (<1 mm) to seal aqueous leaks and halt melting.
  - Conjunctival Flap (Gundersen Flap): Transposition of a vascularized pedicle of bulbar conjunctiva over a chronic, non-healing, or neurotrophic corneal ulcer to provide metabolic support and arrest melting.
  - Therapeutic / Emergency Penetrating Keratoplasty (PKP / Corneal Transplant): Excision of infected host cornea and replacement with donor graft ("keratoplasty a chaud"). Indicated for rapidly progressive necrotizing keratitis refractory to maximal antimicrobial therapy, impending or frank corneal perforation, or extensive endophthalmitis extension.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Severe Stromal Melting & Corneal Perforation: Rapid enzymatically mediated tissue destruction leading to Descemetocoele and open-globe perforation (can occur within 24 hours with P. aeruginosa or B. cereus).
  - Secondary Glaucoma & Intraocular Inflammation: Dense anterior chamber fibrin, debris, and synechiae obstructing trabecular meshwork outflow, causing acute intraocular pressure elevation.
  - Keratitis-Related Endophthalmitis: Extension of corneal infection through full-thickness cornea/Descemet's membrane into the aqueous and vitreous humors, threatening total loss of vision and the eye.
  - Anterior Synechiae & Cataract Formation: Adhesions between iris and cornea or iris and lens capsule, alongside secondary lens opacification.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Permanent Corneal Scarring & Astigmatism: Fibrotic stromal opacification and irregular corneal curvature causing permanent loss of visual acuity (corneal blindness).
  - Corneal Neovascularization & Graft Rejection Risk: Persistent stromal blood vessels ("ghost vessels") that compromise visual clarity and significantly increase the risk of immune rejection during future corneal transplant surgery.
  - Neurotrophic Keratopathy & Persistent Epithelial Defects: Permanent destruction of corneal sensory nerves leading to chronic epithelial breakdown and recurrent erosion.
  - Phthisis Bulbi & Enucleation / Evisceration: Atrophy and shrinkage of an unsalvageable, blind, painful eye following uncontrolled infection, perforation, or endophthalmitis.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Ring infiltrate" (annular stromal neutrophilic complex surrounding corneal lesions in P. aeruginosa, Acanthamoeba, fungal, or vaccinia keratitis).
  - "Dendritic ulcer with terminal bulbs" (branching fluorescein-staining epithelial defect characteristic of HSV epithelial keratitis).
  - "Feathery / filamentous margins" and "Satellite lesions" (classic descriptors for mold keratomycosis caused by Fusarium or Aspergillus).
  - "Radial perineuritis" (inflammation along enlarged corneal nerves in early Acanthamoeba keratitis).
  - "Ghost vessels" (regressed, empty corneal blood vessel channels remaining after chronic neovascularization).
  - "Salmon patches" (dense central deep stromal neovascularization in syphilitic interstitial keratitis).
  - "Herbert pits" and "Arlt line" (limbal depressions and horizontal tarsal scars in trachoma).
  - "PACK-CXL" (photoactivated chromophore corneal cross-linking for infectious keratitis).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - The Intact Epithelium Organism Exception: While the vast majority of bacteria require an epithelial defect to invade the cornea, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Listeria monocytogenes, and Shigella species can penetrate a completely intact, uninjured corneal epithelium.
  - Topical Anesthetic Home Prescribing Trap: Though topical anesthetics (proparacaine) immediately relieve severe eye pain during clinical examination, prescribing them for home use is a critical medical error due to severe epitheliotoxicity, persistent epithelial defects, and catastrophic corneal stromal melting.
  - In vitro Susceptibility vs. Ocular Pharmacokinetics Discrepancy: Standard systemic MIC laboratory susceptibility testing labels organisms "resistant" based on achievable serum drug levels; however, direct topical application achieves local ocular drug concentrations thousands of times higher than serum, successfully eradicating "resistant" microbes in vivo.
  - Contact Lens Case Culture False-Positives: Culturing contact lenses or storage cases yields false-positive growth in up to 75% of cases (corneal culture confirms case/lens culture in only 25%), as lenses are heavily colonized with non-causative commensals.
  - Paecilomyces vs. Penicillium Culture Misidentification: Paecilomyces fungal isolates are frequently misidentified in culture as harmless Penicillium contaminants, leading to inappropriate treatment delays and multiple corneal graft failures.
  - Steroid Contraindication in Herpetic Epithelial Keratitis: Corticosteroids are beneficial in HSV stromal immune keratitis but STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED in HSV epithelial (dendritic) keratitis, as steroids promote viral replication, transform dendrites into massive "geographic ulcers," and accelerate corneal perforation.
  - Pigment Granule Gram Stain Artifact: Melanin pigment granules released from iris/RPE mimic Gram-positive cocci on Gram stain, but are distinguished under microscope fine-focus modulation by their copper-colored refractility.
