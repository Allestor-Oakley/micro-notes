# Endophthalmitis

## Definition & Classification
- **Clinical Definition:** Endophthalmitis refers to a severe bacterial or fungal infection inside the eye involving the vitreous humor, the aqueous humor, or both. It is a sight-threatening medical emergency where delayed or inadequate therapy may result in irreversible, permanent vision loss. Infection can be introduced exogenously (from the outside in, such as post-eye surgery, intraocular injections, eye trauma, filtering blebs, or extension of corneal keratitis) or endogenously (via bloodstream seeding during bacteremia or fungemia).
- **Major Classifications:**
  1. Exogenous Endophthalmitis Categories:
     - Acute Postoperative / Postcataract Endophthalmitis: Most common type worldwide (incidences range from 0.03% to 0.2%, approximately 0.1% in the US); presents within 1 week of cataract surgery in 75% of cases; caused primarily by ocular surface flora contaminating the aqueous humor during surgery. Clear cornea sutureless incisions carry higher risk than scleral tunnel incisions. Rupture of the posterior lens capsule increases risk sixfold.
     - Chronic Postcataract (Pseudophakic) Endophthalmitis: Rare, indolent low-grade inflammation persisting for months postoperatively; responds initially to topical corticosteroids but recurs as steroids are tapered; caused almost exclusively by Cutibacterium (Propionibacterium) acnes.
     - Postinjection Endophthalmitis: Occurs with an incidence of approximately 0.1% per intravitreal injection of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) agents (administered for wet macular degeneration); viridans streptococci and coagulase-negative staphylococci are major pathogens.
     - Bleb-Related Endophthalmitis: Complication of a surgically created conjunctival filtering bleb for medically refractory glaucoma (0.5% to 1.3% per patient-year risk, up to 5-fold higher in leaking blebs); presents abruptly months to years post-surgery; often fulminant due to streptococci, enterococci, or Haemophilus influenzae.
     - Posttraumatic Endophthalmitis: Occurs in 0.9% to 10% of penetrating open-globe eye injuries; Bacillus cereus and coagulase-negative staphylococci are major pathogens (B. cereus causes rapid, fulminant destruction).
     - Keratitis-Related Endophthalmitis: Results when severe microbial keratitis extends through the full thickness of the cornea into the aqueous humor; molds (e.g., Fusarium) and bacteria are common causes.
     - Postkeratoplasty Endophthalmitis: Occurs in 0.03% of corneal transplants in the US, two-thirds of which are caused by Candida species contaminating donor corneas during storage.
  2. Endogenous Endophthalmitis Categories:
     - Endogenous Bacterial Endophthalmitis: Rare (0.04% of hospitalized bacteremic patients); arises from bacteremic seeding from endocarditis (approximately 40% of US cases), pyogenic liver abscess (dominant in East Asia and Taiwan due to hypermucoviscosity Klebsiella pneumoniae), gastrointestinal/hepatic abscesses, urinary tract infection, meningitis, infected central lines, endoscopy, or intravenous drug abuse (IVDU).
     - Endogenous Fungal Endophthalmitis (Ocular Candidiasis / Mold): Ocular candidiasis encompasses a spectrum from chorioretinitis (clear vitreous, white chorioretinal lesions) to endophthalmitis (marked vitritis, "fluff balls"). Main risk factors include central venous catheters, total parenteral nutrition, neutropenia, recent GI surgery/perforation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and IVDU (accounting for 44% of endogenous endophthalmitis and 75% of culture-positive fungal cases in recent series).
     - Endogenous Mold Endophthalmitis: Occurs in IV drug users or severely immunocompromised hosts (organ transplant, hematologic malignancy with pulmonary foci); Aspergillus and Fusarium are dominant.
  3. Panophthalmitis: Rare, severe extension where infection breaks out from the intraocular cavities (globe) into the surrounding orbital tissues, manifesting as endophthalmitis combined with orbital cellulitis (eyelid edema/erythema, proptosis, and extraocular movement limitation).
- **Risk Factors & Epidemiology:**
  1. Risk Factors: Intraocular surgery (cataract surgery with posterior capsule rupture, clear corneal incision, silicone intraocular lenses), repeated intravitreal anti-VEGF injections, glaucoma filtering blebs (especially leaking blebs), open-globe penetrating ocular trauma (dirty soil or organic foreign bodies), severe keratitis (corneal ulceration/melting), donor corneal rim contamination during keratoplasty, indwelling central venous catheters, total parenteral nutrition, broad-spectrum systemic antibiotics, neutropenia, recent GI surgery or perforation, IV drug abuse, bacteremia/fungemia (endocarditis, pyogenic liver abscess), diabetes mellitus, and systemic immunosuppression.
  2. Epidemiology: Postcataract endophthalmitis is the most common form worldwide (over 3.5 million cataract surgeries performed annually in the US). In tropical regions (e.g., India, Florida), fungal/mold endophthalmitis accounts for 10% to 22% of postoperative cases, whereas in temperate regions, nearly all postoperative cases are bacterial (>95% gram-positive). In East Asia (Taiwan, Singapore), Klebsiella pneumoniae pyogenic liver abscess causes up to 60% of endogenous bacterial endophthalmitis cases.

## Etiology (The Pathogens)
- **Most Common Pathogens (Overall):**
  - Acute Postoperative: Coagulase-negative staphylococci (Staphylococcus epidermidis, 70% of culture-positive cases), Staphylococcus aureus (10%), Streptococci (viridans streptococci, Streptococcus pneumoniae, beta-hemolytic streptococci, 9%), Gram-negative bacilli (6%).
  - Chronic Postcataract: Cutibacterium (Propionibacterium) acnes.
  - Postinjection: Coagulase-negative staphylococci and Viridans streptococci (25% of cases; streptococci cause severe fulminant endophthalmitis).
  - Bleb-Related: Streptococci (36%), Coagulase-negative staphylococci (18%), Staphylococcus aureus (11%), Enterococci (9%), Haemophilus influenzae (5%).
  - Posttraumatic: Coagulase-negative staphylococci, Bacillus cereus (most feared, fulminant destruction), Bacillus species.
  - Endogenous Bacterial: Streptococci (S. pneumoniae, S. anginosus group, Group A and Group B streptococci, 30% to 50% in US/Europe), Staphylococcus aureus (approximately 25%), Gram-negative bacilli (one-third in US/Europe; >60% in East Asia due to Klebsiella pneumoniae hypermucoviscosity phenotype).
  - Endogenous Fungal: Candida albicans, non-albicans Candida species (C. glabrata, C. tropicalis, C. krusei).
  - Mold / Keratitis-Related: Aspergillus species (50% to 90% of mold cases), Fusarium species.
- **Pathogens by Specific Age Groups:**
  - Children: Posttraumatic endophthalmitis from open-globe ocular injuries (Bacillus cereus, staphylococci, environmental organisms) and rare endogenous seeding.
  - Adults and Elderly: Postcataract (coagulase-negative staphylococci, Cutibacterium acnes), post-anti-VEGF injection (viridans streptococci), bleb-related (streptococci, H. influenzae), endogenous bacteremic or fungemic seeding (S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, Candida species in IVDU, endocarditis, or liver abscess patients).
- **Atypical or Opportunistic Pathogens:**
  - Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (NTM): Rapidly growing NTMs (Mycobacterium chelonae, M. abscessus, M. fortuitum) causing subacute or chronic endophthalmitis after cataract/glaucoma surgery, intravitreal injections, or keratitis extension.
  - Mold and Fungal Opportunists: Scedosporium apiospermum, Paecilomyces, Exophiala, Microsporidia, and Aspergillus/Fusarium in organ transplant recipients, hematologic malignancy patients, or following contact lens solution outbreaks.
  - Unusual Bacteria: Bartonella henselae, Neisseria meningitidis, Actinomyces, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Enterococcus faecalis.

## Pathophysiology
- **Mechanism of Infection & Spread:**
  - Exogenous Entry: Direct mechanical inoculation of intraocular cavities during intraocular surgery (incisional contamination from patient lid/conjunctival flora or surgeon oral flora), intravitreal injections (oral flora transfer without mask/silence), open-globe penetrating trauma, trans-bleb transmigration across thin conjunctiva in glaucoma filtering blebs, or direct inward extension from full-thickness corneal ulceration in microbial keratitis.
  - Endogenous Seeding: Hematogenous dissemination of circulating bacteria or fungi across the blood-eye barrier (choroidal vasculature). Microorganisms seed the highly vascular choroid first (producing chorioretinitis), then break through Bruch membrane and retina into the avascular vitreous humor, where explosive inflammation (vitritis) occurs.
  - Intraocular Proliferation & Immune Privilege Loss: The vitreous cavity is an avascular, gel-like, immune-privileged environment with poor intrinsic clearance. Bacterial proliferation or fungal growth triggers an intense neutrophilic inflammatory influx (suppuration) into the anterior chamber (hypopyon) and vitreous (vitritis), breaking down the blood-ocular barrier.
- **Host Tissue Response & Damage:**
  - Vitreal Inflammation & Abscess Formation: Neutrophil accumulation and release of proteolytic enzymes, collagenases, and inflammatory cytokines cause liquefaction of the vitreous, vitreous clouding ("fluff balls" or diffuse haze), and retinal vasculitis/ischemia.
  - Toxin-Mediated Destruction: Organisms like Bacillus cereus produce potent pore-forming enterotoxins, hemolysins, and phospholipases that induce rapid, total retinal necrosis, panophthalmitis, and complete loss of vision within 12 to 24 hours.
  - Increased Intraocular Pressure & Structural Damage: High intraocular pressure (IOP > 20 mmHg) frequently develops from inflammatory debris blocking trabecular meshwork drainage, causing secondary glaucoma. Chronic inflammation leads to retinal detachment, cystoid macular edema, macular infarction, and phthisis bulbi (shrunken, non-functional globe).

## Clinical Presentation
- **Classic Symptoms (Patient History):**
  - Acute or subacute decrease in visual acuity (ranging from mild blurriness to count fingers, hand motion, or light perception only).
  - Eye pain (present in 74% of acute postcataract cases; may be mild or absent in C. acnes or Candida, but excruciating in B. cereus or streptococcal cases).
  - Ocular redness, eyelid swelling, and photophobia.
  - Recent history of eye surgery (cataract within 1 week for acute, months for chronic C. acnes), anti-VEGF injection, ocular trauma, glaucoma bleb surgery, contact lens wear, or systemic risk factors (indwelling central venous line, IV drug use, fever/bacteremia, endocarditis, liver abscess).
- **Physical Examination Findings (Signs):**
  - Conjunctival Hyperemia & Ciliary Flush: Marked injection of the bulbar conjunctiva and episcleral vessels.
  - Hypopyon: Layer of white blood cells (neutrophilic exudate) settling in the inferior portion of the anterior chamber (present in the majority of acute cases).
  - Anterior Chamber Cell and Flare: Protein ("flare") and white blood cells ("cells") floating in aqueous humor on slit-lamp examination.
  - Vitritis & Loss of Red Reflex: Dense white blood cell infiltration in the vitreous humor causing a cloudy/hazy vitreous, loss of the normal red reflex, and obscuration of the funduscopic view of the retina. Fungal cases show characteristic vitreal "fluff balls" or "string of pearls" connected by fungal hyphae. Chronic C. acnes shows white plaques on the posterior lens capsule.
  - Intraocular Pressure (IOP) Changes: Frequently elevated IOP (>20 mmHg) due to trabecular meshwork obstruction by inflammatory cells and debris.
  - Absence of Systemic Signs in Exogenous Cases: Patients are typically afebrile with normal or only mildly elevated WBC counts; systemic fever or leukocytosis indicates an endogenous bacteremic focus or panophthalmitis.
- **Pathognomonic Features:**
  - Layering of white blood cells in the inferior anterior chamber (hypopyon) in a patient with recent intraocular surgery or intravitreal injection.
  - White "fluff ball" or "string of pearls" vitreous opacities in endogenous Candida endophthalmitis.
  - White plaque on the posterior lens capsule in chronic postcataract Cutibacterium acnes endophthalmitis.

## Diagnostics & Workup
- **Initial Laboratory Tests (CBC, Inflammatory markers):**
  - Systemic Blood Work: CBC and inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) are generally normal in exogenous endophthalmitis. In endogenous cases, blood work reveals leukocytosis, elevated ESR/CRP, and markers of underlying systemic infection (endocarditis, liver abscess).
  - Blood Cultures: Negative in exogenous cases. Positive in approximately 75% of endogenous bacterial endophthalmitis cases (S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, Streptococci) and 6% of outpatient IVDU-related Candida cases (due to transient prior candidemia).
- **Imaging Modalities:**
  - B-Scan Ultrasonography (Ocular Ultrasound): Essential diagnostic imaging tool when dense cataract, corneal opacity, or severe anterior chamber/vitreous inflammation obscures the funduscopic view of the retina. Confirms the presence and severity of vitreous opacities/vitritis, excludes or detects retinal detachment, choroidal detachment, or intraocular foreign bodies.
  - Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) & Radiography: CT or MRI of the orbit is indicated only if panophthalmitis or orbital cellulitis is suspected.
- **Microbiological Diagnostics (Cultures, PCR, Stains):**
  - Intraocular Specimen Collection:
    - Vitreous Sampling (Primary Diagnostic Procedure): Vitreous yields significantly higher culture positivity (50% to 90%) than aqueous. Obtained via in-office needle aspiration ("tap and inject", 0.2 to 0.3 mL using a 27-gauge needle) or in-OR mechanized pars plana vitrectomy / biopsy (using 20-gauge or 23-gauge vitrector). Vitreous washings from vitrectomy canisters are filtered through a 0.45-micrometer filter and plated onto agar.
    - Aqueous Sampling: Aqueous humor aspirate (0.1 mL) is easily performed in office but yields unique positive cultures in only 4% of cases.
  - Stains & Microscopic Examination:
    - Gram Stain & Fungal Stains: Positive in approximately 50% of bacterial cases. Demonstrates gram-positive cocci in clusters/chains, gram-negative bacilli, or fungal hyphae/pseudohyphae.
    - Microscopic Artifact - Pigment Granules: Melanin pigment granules released from iris/retinal pigment epithelium appear as purple spherical or football-shaped structures on Gram stain that mimic gram-positive cocci; distinguished under fine focus modulation because pigment granules appear highly refractile and copper-colored, whereas bacteria do not.
  - Culture Media: Inoculation onto blood agar, chocolate agar, thioglycollate broth, and Sabouraud dextrose agar. Positive culture defined as growth on 2 or more media or abundant growth on 1 medium (one-third of clinically diagnosed cases remain culture-negative).
  - Molecular Diagnostic Testing (PCR): Eubacterial/pan-fungal 16S/18S rRNA PCR assays provide rapid pathogen identification (91% sensitivity vs 75% culture in postcataract cases), especially in patients who previously received intravitreal antibiotics; false-positive PCR rates in uninfected control eyes range from 5% to 14% due to normal ocular surface colonizers.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test:**
  - Microbiological Culture and PCR Isolation of Pathogen from Vitreous Humor Sample (Vitreous Aspirate or Vitrectomy Washings): Represents the definitive gold standard diagnostic test for endophthalmitis.

## Management & Empiric Therapy
- **Initial Resuscitation & Supportive Care:**
  - Emergency Ophthalmology Consultation: Acute endophthalmitis is a true medical emergency requiring immediate ophthalmologic evaluation and intervention within hours of presentation.
  - Local Supportive Care: Topical cycloplegic drops (e.g., scopolamine or atropine) to relieve ciliary spasm pain and prevent posterior synechiae. Topical pressure-lowering drops (e.g., timolol, brimonidine, dorzolamide) for elevated intraocular pressure (avoid miotics like pilocarpine).
  - Systemic Resuscitation for Endogenous Cases: Treatment of underlying systemic bacteremic/fungemic focus (e.g., endocarditis, liver abscess) and management of systemic sepsis.
- **First-Line Empiric Antimicrobial Regimens:**
  - Acute Bacterial Endophthalmitis (Exogenous & Endogenous) - Direct Intravitreal Injection (Tap and Inject / Vitrectomy):
    - Empiric Intravitreal Antibiotic Regimen (Standard 0.1 mL injections into vitreous cavity):
      - Vancomycin 1 mg in 0.1 mL sterile water/saline (covers Gram-positive cocci including MRSA, staphylococci, streptococci, C. acnes) COMBINED WITH
      - Ceftazidime 2.25 mg in 0.1 mL sterile water/saline (covers Gram-negative bacilli including Pseudomonas aeruginosa).
      - Note: Intravitreal antibiotics persist in vitreous for 24 to 48 hours; repeat injection may be required if no clinical response in 24 to 48 hours.
  - Systemic Therapy Indications:
    - Exogenous Bacterial Endophthalmitis: Systemic (oral or IV) antibiotics are generally NOT effective or necessary for exogenous bacterial endophthalmitis (as shown in the Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study / EVS), EXCEPT in cases of panophthalmitis or severe open-globe trauma.
    - Endogenous Bacterial Endophthalmitis: High-dose intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics matching the systemic source (e.g., IV Vancomycin plus Cefepime or Piperacillin-tazobactam) ARE required in addition to intravitreal antibiotics.
- **Alternative Regimens:**
  - Cephalosporin Allergy / Resistance Alternative: Intravitreal Amikacin 0.4 mg in 0.1 mL substituted for Ceftazidime (used cautiously due to rare risk of irreversible macular infarction/blindness).
  - Endogenous / Exogenous Fungal Endophthalmitis (Ocular Candidiasis):
    - Intravitreal Antifungal Injections: Intravitreal Amphotericin B (5 to 10 mcg in 0.1 mL sterile water) or Intravitreal Voriconazole (100 mcg in 0.1 mL sterile water). Voriconazole is preferred for sensitive strains due to lower retinal toxicity.
    - Systemic Antifungal Therapy: Systemic Fluconazole (loading dose 800 mg / 12 mg/kg, then 400 to 800 mg daily) OR Systemic Voriconazole (loading dose 400 mg IV q12h for 2 doses, then 200 to 300 mg IV/PO twice daily). Note: Echinocandins (micafungin, caspofungin) achieve excellent systemic levels but POOR vitreous penetration (0.5% of plasma levels) and are NOT recommended for Candida endophthalmitis with vitritis. IV Liposomal Amphotericin B (3 to 5 mg/kg daily) with or without Flucytosine 25 mg/kg 4 times daily for azole-resistant Candida.
    - Candida Chorioretinitis (Without Vitritis): Treated with systemic oral/IV Fluconazole or Voriconazole ALONE (intravitreal injection/vitrectomy not required unless macula-threatening).
  - Exogenous / Endogenous Mold Endophthalmitis (Aspergillus, Fusarium):
    - Intravitreal Voriconazole (100 mcg in 0.1 mL) or Amphotericin B (5 to 10 mcg in 0.1 mL) PLUS Systemic IV/PO Voriconazole (susceptible molds achieve excellent intraocular levels).
  - Prophylaxis Protocols:
    - Postcataract Prophylaxis: Intracameral Cefuroxime (1 mg in 0.1 mL) at the conclusion of cataract surgery reduces endophthalmitis incidence from 0.3% to 0.06% (ESCRS study). Povidone-iodine 5% topical drops on conjunctiva pre-op.
    - Open Globe Trauma Prophylaxis: Immediate IV Vancomycin plus Ceftazidime (or Cefepime) for 48 hours reduces posttraumatic endophthalmitis risk to <1%.
    - Intravitreal Anti-VEGF Injection Prophylaxis: Masks and strict silence during procedure; topical antibiotic drops post-injection are CONTRAINDICATED (increase postinjection endophthalmitis risk).
- **Surgical or Procedural Interventions :**
  - Pars Plana Vitrectomy (PPV): Surgical removal and débridement of infected vitreous humor using a 20-gauge or 23-gauge mechanical vitrector. Indicated as initial treatment for patients presenting with fulminant infection, severe vision loss (light perception only in EVS study), or marked vitritis (Candida / mold endophthalmitis). Clears bacterial/fungal load, toxins, and inflammatory debris, improving visual outcomes.
  - Tap and Inject: Diagnostic vitreous needle aspiration (0.2 to 0.3 mL) followed by immediate intravitreal antibiotic injection, performed as an in-office procedure for mild-to-moderate presentations (vision better than light perception).
  - Intraocular Lens (IOL) and Capsule Removal: Mandatory total capsulectomy and IOL removal/exchange for chronic Cutibacterium acnes postcataract endophthalmitis (achieves 100% cure rate) and exogenous fungal/mold endophthalmitis.
  - Therapeutic Keratoplasty / Corneal Transplantation: Performed to debulk infection in endophthalmitis resulting from progressing fungal keratitis.

## Complications & Sequelae
- **Acute Complications:**
  - Rapid Irreversible Visual Loss: Complete loss of vision (No Light Perception / NLP) occurring within 24 to 48 hours, especially with virulent organisms like Bacillus cereus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, or Streptococci.
  - Retinal Detachment & Choroidal Detachment: Occurs secondary to vitreal traction and severe intraocular inflammation.
  - Macular Infarction & Retinal Vasculitis: Toxic retinal damage or aminoglycoside-induced macular infarction (causing permanent central blindness). Vancomycin-associated Hemorrhagic Occlusive Retinal Vasculitis (HORV) is a rare, delayed (1 to 21 days) immune reaction causing ischemic retinal hemorrhages.
  - Panophthalmitis: Extracapsular spread of infection into orbital tissues causing proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, and severe periocular edema.
  - Secondary Glaucoma: Severe elevation of intraocular pressure causing optic nerve damage.
- **Long-term Sequelae:**
  - Permanent Severe Visual Impairment or Blindness: Persistent legal blindness (<20/200 visual acuity) or No Light Perception (NLP).
  - Phthisis Bulbi: Atrophy, shrinkage, and disorganization of the non-functional globe following end-stage inflammatory destruction.
  - Enucleation or Evisceration: Surgical removal of the eye globe for blind, painful, or unsalvageable panophthalmitis.
  - Chronic Scotoma & Cystoid Macular Edema: Permanent visual field defects and central macular scarring.

## Trivia & Edge Cases
- **Buzzwords & Descriptors:**
  - "Hypopyon" (layered collection of white blood cells in the inferior anterior chamber).
  - "Vitritis" (dense white blood cell inflammatory infiltration in the vitreous humor).
  - "Fluff balls" and "string of pearls" (classic vitreal fungal cotton-like opacities in Candida endophthalmitis).
  - "Clear cornea incision" vs "Scleral tunnel incision" (sutureless clear cornea cataract incisions carry a higher risk of endophthalmitis due to intermittent wound gaping).
  - "Tap and inject" (in-office vitreous needle aspirate followed by intravitreal antibiotic injection).
  - "Pars plana vitrectomy" (surgical vitreous débridement).
  - "Pigment granules" (melanin granules on Gram stain that refract coppery under fine focus, mimicking gram-positive cocci).
  - "EVS" (Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study).
- **Exceptions to the Rule:**
  - Absence of Systemic Fever and Leukocytosis: Patients with acute exogenous endophthalmitis are completely afebrile, feel systemically well, and have normal WBC counts, despite having an explosive intraocular infection.
  - Refractory Nature of Echinocandins in Ocular Candidiasis: Despite being first-line systemic therapy for candidemia, echinocandins (micafungin, caspofungin) achieve subtherapeutic vitreous levels (<0.5% of plasma) and FAIL in Candida endophthalmitis with vitritis; azoles (fluconazole/voriconazole) or amphotericin B must be used.
  - Intravitreal Vancomycin HORV Reaction: Prophylactic intracameral/intraocular vancomycin can trigger delayed (1 to 21 days) Hemorrhagic Occlusive Retinal Vasculitis (HORV), a non-infectious, devastating ischemic retinal reaction causing 20/200 or worse vision in 67% of eyes.
  - Inefficacy of Systemic Antibiotics in Exogenous Endophthalmitis: Systemic (oral or IV) antibiotics do NOT cross the blood-eye barrier in sufficient concentrations to treat exogenous bacterial endophthalmitis and offer no benefit over intravitreal injection alone (as proven by the Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study).
  - Candida Donor Cornea Contamination: Donor corneas stored in standard preservation media containing broad-spectrum antibacterial antibiotics lack antifungal agents, resulting in a 3% risk of Candida endophthalmitis if donor rim culture grows Candida species.
  - Complete Lack of Systemic Symptoms in B. cereus Destruction: Bacillus cereus causes catastrophic eye destruction and panophthalmitis within 12 to 24 hours through potent exotoxins, while the patient remains systemically afebrile without bacteremia.
